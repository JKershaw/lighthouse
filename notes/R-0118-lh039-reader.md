## What a reader would believe, written before reading the record

Principal finding, said back: after WordPress fixed its updater flaw on 28 October, the fixed version spread steadily but not fast among websites. On the newest branch about a fifth of sites had it after twelve days and over a third after three weeks (43% by day 24 if you count the next security release), and about a quarter on the older branches. Developers' build downloads switched to the fix within days on 7.1. And WordPress, despite updating itself automatically, spread no faster than PHP's fix did over similar ages.

Observed: W3Techs's share of crawled sites on each version at each read; Composer download counts per release per day; WordPress's release dates. Explained: nothing causally; the record says the PHP comparison is a description. But a reader will still come away with an implied explanation: auto-update did not make WordPress quicker than PHP, so maybe auto-update matters less than expected; and the gap between Composer and W3Techs suggests builds move faster than live sites.

Limits a reader would notice: W3Techs's visiting schedule may set the pace; downloads are fetches, not sites; the second release on 18 November muddies the later figures; some WordPress instruments were refused by robots.txt; nothing is reviewed. A reader would probably not notice that "fixed share within branch" excludes sites on other branches, that PHP's comparison is across different software ages and update mechanisms on the same crawler, or how the treatment of 7.1.5 was chosen.

Read: the rest of LH039.md 1.1, brief.md with its amendment, before-the-brief.md, record-1.0-tabulation.txt (every W3Techs row and the history pages), the one-off read's archive.facts.json, investigations/software-updates.md (LH039 appears only in the source list), programme.md's Next, and LH033's record and brief. The central question: yes, these measurements could be right while the reading a reader takes from them is wrong. The record itself claims no cause, and its figures reproduce; the problem is what the In short and Answer let a reader infer about sites and about self-updating software.

## What the round establishes

- On W3Techs, the share of each watched branch's detected sites carrying the fixed release (or later) rose at every read from 9 to 21 November, on 7.1 from 19.6% to 36.3% before the second release and 38.2% for 7.1.4 alone at day 24; 7.0 and 6.9 reached about 22 to 23% for the first fix alone. These are shares of W3Techs's detections within a branch, not counts of sites.
- In Composer downloads the fixed 7.1 release took most of its branch's fetches within one to five days; the older branches' fetches stayed mostly on unfixed releases. These are fetches by builds, labelled as such.
- On the comparison named in advance, WordPress 7.1 fell inside the span of PHP's four branches on the same survey at nearby ages.
- WordPress shipped a second security release on 18 November; the brief's rule then counts it, and the record says so and gives the first-fix-only figures.
- Nothing establishes when sites updated, whether self-updating made any difference, or how W3Techs detects a WordPress release.

## Material claim problems

1. **The pace may be W3Techs's, and the summary drops that.** Passage: LH039.md, In short: "We asked how fast the websites ... moved to its fix ... the fixed share of the newest branch, 7.1, rose at every daily read, from about 20% twelve days after the fix to 36% at day 21". A reader takes this as sites updating gradually over three weeks. Evidence: the qualifier sits only at the end of Answer 1 and in Limits ("How much of the pace is W3Techs's own visiting schedule ... can't be told"). The 7.1.4 series is a straight line, 1.83 points a day, max residual 0.44, crossing zero at day 1.2 (review/r0118_check_output.txt). That is exactly what a survey revisiting each site about once a month would show if every site that updated did so on day 0 or 1. The history pages point the same way: superseded releases kept gaining share after the fix, 7.0.7 from 20.0% to 28.4% and 6.9.10 from 23.8% to 28.4% between 1 and 21 November (record-1.0-tabulation.txt, history section). Automatic updates would not move sites onto a superseded release, so this looks like the survey still catching up on updates made weeks earlier. The brief's own expectation was that W3Techs "should make its view lag far behind" (brief.md, What is already known). Resolution: in the In short, and at the head of Answer 1, say that these are lagged views of the sites, and that a steady rise is what the survey's roughly monthly visits would produce even if sites updated at once. Give the superseded-release observation as the evidence, labelled post hoc. Drop "how fast" as a description of the result, though it can stay in the question.

2. **"Inside PHP's range" understates which way the comparison points, and it can't speak to self-updating.** Passage: In short: "Beside PHP's fix in the same survey at about the same ages, WordPress 7.1 sat inside PHP's range, not clearly above it". Answer 2: "the brief's writer had expected WordPress, which updates itself, to move much faster". A reader concludes WordPress moved at about PHP's pace, so automatic updates bought little. Evidence: the four PHP branches span 10.7% to 23.8% at day 12 and 32.5% to 55.4% at day 26 (Findings 4), so almost any value lands "inside the range". Matched newest to newest, 7.1 against 8.5, WordPress was below at every pairing: by 4.2 points at day 12, 6.9 at day 19 and 12.1 at day 24 against PHP day 26. WordPress's 7.0 and 6.9 were below all four PHP branches at every pairing (r0118_check_output.txt; this branch matching is post hoc, 9 comparisons). Two more things matter. Under problem 1, a lagged survey's shares at these ages mostly reflect how many sites updated promptly, not when. And the record does not establish that W3Techs detects a WordPress release the way it detects a PHP version; both LH033 and LH039 list W3Techs's detection as unknown. So "same instrument" means the same survey, not necessarily the same measurement. Resolution: say that WordPress 7.1 was below PHP's newest branch at every comparison and above its older ones, and add (post hoc) that WordPress's older branches were below every PHP branch. State that this comparison cannot test whether self-updating helps, because detection and branch roles differ between the two programs. Keep "no cause is claimed".

## Readability

- In the In short, give the second-release-inclusive and first-fix-only figures in one place, or lead with 38% for the first fix and give 43% as "with the later release". Three numbers for day 21 to 24 are hard to hold.
- Add a Limits line saying that a within-branch share also moves when sites join or leave a branch. Over the window 7.0.4 fell from 26.4% to 10.4% while unfixed 7.0.6 and 7.0.7 rose by about the same factor (1.19). The effect on the headline is small.
- Packagist's day-1 figure (76.8%) sits in the same sentence as the website figures. A short "a different thing, counted differently" between them would stop a reader setting downloads against sites as fast against slow.

## Next step

The record's Next item (1), W3Techs's 1 December monthly point, is worth taking, but on its own it can't separate the two explanations above. The step that can is already half in the repository. W3Techs's history pages for 7.1, 7.0 and 6.9 give monthly shares for WordPress's earlier patch releases on the same survey, for example 7.1.3 at under 0.1% on 1 October and 54.7% on 1 November, and 6.9.10 and 7.0.7 first listed on 1 November. That is a same-software, same-survey baseline, like LH033's measure 6 for PHP. It answers the question the PHP comparison can't: did the emergency fix spread differently from WordPress's ordinary releases at the same ages? It also shows the survey's own lag curve, so the reading in problem 1 can be tested.

Recommendation: a dated amendment or proposal, labelled post hoc because the history rows have been seen, that does three things. It takes release dates for the 7.1, 7.0 and 6.9 patch releases from one one-off read of WordPress's release archive (the archive body wasn't kept). It compares 7.1.4, 7.0.8 and 6.9.11 at 1 November and 1 December with each earlier release at its first and second monthly points. It also records whether 7.1.4's share levels off after about day 30, which a monthly-revisit lag predicts and steady site updating does not. Do this before any sentence on the investigation page or the synthesis compares WordPress with PHP. Next item (2), a future same-day cross-software test, is weaker than this within-WordPress baseline and shouldn't come first. Item (4), W3Techs's methodology pages, would help with both problems and is cheap.

Material problems:
1. The summary presents W3Techs's steady rise as the pace of sites, when the record can't tell that apart from the survey's own revisit lag, and its own history pages suggest the lag (In short; Answer 1).
2. "Inside PHP's range, not clearly above it" understates that WordPress was below PHP's newest branch at every comparison, and that its older branches were below all of PHP's (post hoc). It also invites a reading about self-updating that the comparison can't support (In short; Answer 2).
