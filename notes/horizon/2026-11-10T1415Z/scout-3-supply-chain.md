## Five best leads

1. **Dated clocks for the three October and November registry compromises (LiteLLM 21 Oct, the Claude Code extension 29 Oct, bee-js 9 Nov).** Sources 6, 7, 11 and 12 give timestamps to the minute for upload, report and quarantine or takedown (LiteLLM: 11:39 upload, 12:06 report, 13:23 quarantine; the extension: 09:27 to 20:35 exposure). Why it matters: the charter's question needs how far automated installers, CI jobs and agents pull a bad release before a human stops it; these are three recent cases with published windows. Fits an active investigation: yes, the software-update work (release spread) and what AI activity costs the shared internet, since the LiteLLM and extension victims are AI tooling. Lost if nobody looks: bee-js hourly download counts and the registry's version history are best read within days to weeks (the window is roughly now to late November); LiteLLM's hourly counts from ClickPy or pypistats should still be available, but mirror and image-cache traces fade.
2. **Registry trust signals that cannot tell a malicious release from a real one.** Sources 11, 21 and 23: bee-js 13.3.0 carried valid SLSA provenance through an existing trusted-publishing workflow; Bloom's name-reuse flaw exposed more than 1,500 packages; the Red Hat case used a throwaway branch. Why it matters: it shows how a stolen credential can publish through legitimate infrastructure, which is the path any self-propagating activity would use. Fits: yes, software updates (how fast do consumers adopt a release, and does provenance change that). Lost if nobody looks: low urgency for the flaw itself, but whether trusted-publishing configurations expire is a one-off change (GitHub's 2 October rule, 48 hours) with a before and after that is easiest to see now.
3. **Worm payloads that write into AI agent configuration, and the one report that says it never spread.** Source 10 (strutil-core, 1 Nov, removed in about five hours, "not confirmed any successful downstream publication") and source 22 (an npm record writing `.claude/settings.json` hooks and `CLAUDE.md`). Why it matters: it is the cleanest example of residue that tells agents to run something, in repositories, with the report itself saying spread was not seen; it separates capability from observed propagation, which is the charter's distinction. Fits: partly, it extends the registry-worm timeline (LH041) with the November entries, and "AI activity" only as a target, not an actor. Lost if nobody looks: the dated records at ossf.github.io/malicious-packages (updated 10 Nov 00:34 UTC) persist, but committed config files in public repositories are deleted within hours or days (window: weeks).
4. **GitHub's package firewall (npm only, Enterprise Cloud, preview since 20 October) and PyPI's own controls as a change in how fast installs can be blocked.** Sources 8, 9 and 3 (PyPI's 14-day upload rule from 22 July; no new control since). Why it matters: a cooldown between publish and install changes how much of the shared registry traffic a bad release can reach, and it names agents and CI hosts as its targets. Fits: yes, software updates, as a policy-change counterfactual. Lost if nobody looked: no, the preview is open-ended, but adoption cannot be seen from outside; worth watching for a general-availability date and for PyPI support.
5. **The malware-advisory flow as a daily instrument (GitHub advisory database, OSV and OpenSSF).** Source 22 counted 112 malware advisories in three days (96 npm, 10 PyPI) and notes that "89 of the 112" carry only standard text; source 20 offers `all.csv` with an update timestamp. Why it matters: it is a public, dated record of how much automated or opportunistic malware reaches the registries, and it includes AI-brand lures and agent-config writers. Fits: yes, what AI activity costs the shared internet, as a baseline that could show whether agent-aimed lures rise. Lost if nobody looks: advisories are later edited, withdrawn or reworded ("a moving target"), so first-published dates and wording are best captured now and then daily (window: continuous).
