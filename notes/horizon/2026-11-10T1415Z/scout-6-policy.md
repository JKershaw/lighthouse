## Five best leads

### 1. The shared `__usage_ai-training` robots.txt token: a baseline before Bing's 15 December transition and before honouring starts in 2027
- Reported: ppc.land and Search Engine Roundtable, 30 Oct 2026 (rows 19, 20), with the Anthropic-authored draft (7 Nov file, row 4 there) and the WG's attach-05 (row 18). Google, Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic each announced the same token; Microsoft's Bing Webmaster Tools transition opens 15 Dec 2026 and honouring starts 1 Feb 2027; Google says "early 2027"; OpenAI gives no date. No major crawler honours it yet, as reported.
- Why it matters to the charter question: robots.txt is where the web states what AI systems may do, and a token written by an AI lab and adopted by four labs in one day gives a before-and-after on how publishers answer, and whether crawlers comply. It bears on the shared infrastructure only indirectly (preferences are about use, not load), but compliance is exactly the "rules of the road" map the keeper described for P-0022.
- Fits an investigation: partly "what AI activity costs the shared internet" (crawler behaviour and consent signals); the 8 Nov L-0013 lead (a robots.txt AI-token baseline) now has a concrete token name and dates. Not a new investigation.
- First observations lost if nobody looks soon: yes, the window is about 5 weeks (to 15 Dec 2026, the first date any change is documented to arrive; sites will start writing the token after that, so the pre-adoption baseline of zero is only clean until then). Archives (HTTP Archive, Common Crawl) hold older robots.txt files, so partial loss only.
- Measurable: prevalence of the token in a fixed top-sites sample's robots.txt, monthly; any mention in crawler documentation; first crawler requests that change behaviour. Cheap. Lighthouse follows robots.txt fetching rules (reading robots.txt itself is allowed).

### 2. Regulators' reporting regimes for agent incidents are forming now: US voluntary practices (16 Oct), Australia's announced mandate, EU RFIs (14 Oct)
- Reported: NIST/NSA/CISA PDF (row 26): voluntary reporting of "SI control incidents and near-misses" with attention to third parties "not the final target"; Australia's PM announced a mandate on 24 Sep (row 28, a CSA note, partly unverified); EU AI Office RFIs on 14 Oct (rows 23, 24; the first via an AI-generated page); US bills and a 27 Oct letter asking for 48-hour notice (row 29). Penalty-procedure consultation closed 9 Nov.
- Why it matters: it decides whether the next case of an agent touching other people's systems will be visible to anyone outside the lab, and so whether Lighthouse could ever see "AI activity sustaining and spreading itself". It is also the one place where the shape of public evidence (what is reported, to whom, how fast) is being written.
- Fits an investigation: "when a model finds the bug" (disclosure routes for vulnerabilities found by agents; row 26 item 9.2 says to report vulnerabilities through coordinated mechanisms) and "what AI activity costs the shared internet" (third-party effects). A dated table of who must report what, by jurisdiction, is a desk piece, not a scan.
- Lost if nobody looks soon: partly. Australian legislation is hoped for by end of 2026, the EU and US texts are in motion, and drafts change; a dated snapshot of each text now is cheap and cannot be recovered later in its draft form. Window: weeks to the end of 2026.
- Caveat: several of the underlying facts come from secondary or AI-generated pages; a study would need the primary releases (not opened).

### 3. The IETF 127 sessions, with a frozen draft set: a census and what changes in the room (16 to 20 November)
- Reported: Datatracker (rows 1 to 12). No draft in the five groups is dated after 2 Nov, so the set going into the meeting is fixed: webbotauth 12, agentproto 21, dawn 29, aipref 21, audit 16. The signature protocol is now a WG document (draft-ietf-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol-01, Cloudflare and Google authors), with "a couple of public implementations"; SAIL (Anthropic) and JAFAR (Illyes) would publish IP ranges at well-known locations; the audit BOF is on 17 Nov; dawn discusses new DNS record types.
- Why it matters: these are the proposals for how agents are identified, discovered and held to account on shared infrastructure; each leaves a countable public artefact if deployed (well-known files, DNS records, signed requests).
- Fits an investigation: it overlaps "what AI activity costs the shared internet". It is already under LH045's IETF census (the 8 Nov note), so this is a deepening: record the post-meeting minutes, the audit BOF's charter outcome, WG adoption calls and the interim-meeting decision, not a new study.
- Lost if nobody looks soon: minutes and chat are posted after the meeting and are easy to read later; what is perishable is the pre-meeting draft state (fixed now, shown on Datatracker history anyway). Low loss. The agenda page for webbotauth says "Wednesday" against the meeting agenda's Tuesday; check at the time.

### 4. Cloudflare's "signed agents" and the first deployments of Web Bot Auth: what is signed, by whom, and what a valid signature does not mean
- Reported: Cloudflare docs, updated 8 Oct 2026 (row 30): signing only for verified bots, experimental transitive trust, no nonce or replay database. The draft (row 15) lists public client and server implementations. Search results, from blogs I did not open, say Google-Agent signs but Googlebot does not, and that OpenAI cloud browser agents sign.
- Why it matters: if agents sign their requests, public bot-traffic instruments could count agent traffic by operator for the first time, a direct measure of "what AI activity costs". The missing replay protection means the identity layer itself is spoofable, which affects how far a signed-traffic count can be trusted.
- Fits an investigation: "what AI activity costs the shared internet" (agent traffic attribution); links to L-0010 (Cloudflare bot classes and verified-bot directory snapshots).
- Lost if nobody looks soon: moderately. The signed-agent directory and docs change without a public history except web archives; a dated snapshot now costs one page read, and the WG is deciding in the next week what the protocol requires.

### 5. Ninth Circuit's "the user accessed" holding and Amazon's pending rehearing petition (18 August), as a driver of how agent traffic is attributed
- Reported: Cooley, 6 Aug, and MLex, 18 Aug (rows 21, 22): the panel held on 4 Aug that the user, not the agent's maker, "accessed" Amazon's computers; terms-of-service and contract theories remain open; Amazon sought a rehearing. No result reports a decision on that petition. Separately, search snippets (not opened) say Reddit's claims against Perplexity and SerpApi survived dismissal and that Google's case against SerpApi was dismissed (reported 26 Jul).
- Why it matters: the law of whether an agent's access counts as the operator's or the user's decides who may block agents and whether signed identity is needed; it shapes the incentives behind the IETF work in leads 3 and 4.
- Fits an investigation: only loosely (context for "what AI activity costs the shared internet"); a desk note, not an instrument. Already L-0024 in proposals.md.
- Lost if nobody looks soon: no. A rehearing order is a docket entry that stays; the only perishable item is the date of the decision on the petition, so this is a low-priority item to track.
