# R-0157: reader-and-inference review of LH030 record 1.1

## What a reader would believe, written before reading the record

**Principal finding, said back.** PHP told people on 23 October to switch off one feature until its next release. Over the following month, of 15 public sources we watched, only CERT-EU passed that advice on. None of the vulnerability databases did. The databases only opened entries around the time PHP shipped its fix on 19 November, and by 23 November three of them listed fixed packages (five, if you read two more generously). Two security bodies' pages still call the flaw unfixed, and PHP's own release notes don't name it.

**Observed.** What each fixed URL showed at each of 29 reads: CERT-EU carrying the advice from the first read; no tracker entry until SUSE's in early November; five more entries appearing between the 19 and 23 November reads; Fastly naming it only at the last read; CISA and Akamai refusing every read.

**Explained.** Very little. The record describes timing and wording, and doesn't say why the advice didn't spread or why the records waited.

**Caused.** Nothing is claimed outright. Still, "after PHP's releases" and the bunching of entries on 19 November lead a reader to infer that the fix release, or the CVE record's publication that day, triggered the database entries.

**Limits a reader would notice.** A fixed panel, not reach: silence at a URL isn't silence elsewhere. Everything from 2 November is unreviewed. The "five of seven" and the Red Hat reading were made after outcomes were seen. A tracker's "fixed" is its own claim, untested. Two sources were unreadable. A careful reader may also trip on "three of the six" in the opening against "three of seven" in the Answer.

### What the round establishes

The observations hold up as stated. The classifier replay shows 0 mismatches over 29 reads, and the per-entry first-seen changes in record-1.0-output.txt match the Findings table. CERT-EU carried the instruction from the baseline to the last read. Only SUSE's record existed before 19 November. Five records appeared between the 19 November 14:18 and 23 November 14:21 reads. ### Material claim problems

1. **The records' "no" answers a different question, and the summary drops the post hoc label.**
   - *Passages:* LH030.md In short, "the advice reached one notice, CERT-EU's, and no database repeated it as advice to users", and Answer paragraph 1, "so no other notice and no vulnerability record repeated PHP's advice".
   - *Evidence:* LH030.md table and record-1.0-output.txt. Six of the seven trackers had no record at all until after 19 November 14:18 UTC, and OSV never had one. So for six of seven, the advice "until the next regular bugfix release" could only have been carried by a record that opened after that release. This is the brief's frame answering a different question (could a record have carried interim advice at all?), not a finding that trackers chose not to relay it. Only SUSE (from 5 November) could have, and it didn't. Separately, Red Hat's 23 November `statement` says that disabling session upload progress "provides a mitigation for this issue", and its `mitigation` field reads "See the security bulletin for a detailed mitigation procedure.", a bulletin that wasn't read (LH030.md, records at the last read). "No vulnerability record" is therefore partly unknown, not a plain no. And "as advice to users" is decision 2's post hoc reading, which the In short presents without its label or the pre-set count of 2.
   - *Proposed resolution:* In the In short and Answer, say that six of seven trackers held no record until the fix releases, so only SUSE's could have carried the interim advice, and it didn't. Say that Red Hat's record names disabling the feature as a mitigation for its own older packages and points to a bulletin that wasn't read. Keep "2 by the brief's classifier, 1 on a post hoc reading" wherever the count appears, the In short included.

2. **The timing reads as tracker lag behind PHP's notice, and as caused by the releases.**
   - *Passages:* LH030.md Answer paragraph 2, "The records came a month after PHP's notice, and nearly all at once", and In short, "after PHP's releases of 19 November, three of the six list fixed packages".
   - *Evidence:* LH030.md Findings. The CVE record has `dateReserved` 2026-10-22T22:24Z and `datePublished` 2026-11-19T14:42:48Z, assigner `php`. Red Hat's `public_date` is 14:41:13Z, NVD's `published` is 15:17, and Ubuntu's 14:00 (no zone) is earlier than both. These clocks fit the records waiting on the identifier's publication by PHP as numbering authority, or on a shared disclosure time. They fit "tracker lag of a month" no better than that, and the study can't tell these apart. The interval of about four days and the sources' own clocks are all it has.
   - *Proposed resolution:* Add one sentence to the Answer. By their own clocks, the records that give times put publication on 19 November, beside the CVE record's publication by PHP's numbering authority at 14:42 UTC, after the identifier had been reserved since 22 October. The study doesn't show why the records waited, and it measures no tracker delay. Label it as observation, with the reason left untested.

3. **Other checks.** The In short's "still describe it as unfixed" for CERT-EU is a slight overreach, because CERT-EU's page keeps pre-release wording ("until the next regular bugfix release") rather than saying the flaw is unfixed. It's minor (Readability 2). I found no material problem with the stop, the php-advisory-gate finding (correctly "unsettled from LH030's data"), the retirement counts or the Limits.

### Readability

3. "The advice reached one notice" implies a route from PHP. CERT-EU cites PHP's front page at its 20 October entry and a news article, not PHP's 23 October notice, and it adds "restart every worker". "Appeared on one notice" says only what was observed.
