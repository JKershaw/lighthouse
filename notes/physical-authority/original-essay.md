# When the Machine Is Working as Intended

*AI, autonomous weapons, institutional power, and the civilian perspective.*

**12 November 2026 — A proposed research direction for Lighthouse**

## The robot in the tunnel

Imagine walking through an airport ten years from now and passing an armed, humanoid robot. It patrols the terminal, observes passengers, communicates with other security systems, and has some degree of authority to intervene.

Perhaps it is controlled remotely. Perhaps it can act independently in certain circumstances. Perhaps the distinction is difficult to establish from looking at it.

What matters, as a civilian walking past it, is that a machine now exercises some of the physical authority of the state over you.

This is not a prediction about what airports will look like in 2036. It is a thought experiment about a technological direction that is already taking shape. And this week, a story emerged that brings that direction into unusually sharp focus.

The [Financial Times reported](https://www.ft.com/content/8117b912-ebc7-46b2-90cd-a1ec32e12c5a) that Foundation Future Industries, an American humanoid robotics company backed by Eric Trump, is in preliminary discussions with the Israeli military about supplying as many as 1,000 bipedal robots for operations in the tunnels beneath Gaza.

The company's president, Mike LeBlanc, reportedly visited Gaza's tunnels with one of its Phantom MK1 robots in September. The initial applications being discussed include detecting explosives and dealing with hazards that would otherwise endanger soldiers. The company also envisages future combat applications.

This ambition is not new. In July 2026, [WIRED reported](https://www.wired.com/story/humanoid-robot-soldier-eric-trump-foundation-future-industries/) that Foundation's CEO, Sankaet Pathak, was exploring weapons systems and intended to give the company's humanoid robots lethal capabilities.

There are important distinctions to preserve. A discussion about supplying 1,000 robots is not an order for 1,000 robots. A robot capable of navigating a tunnel is not necessarily capable of fighting in one. A weaponised robot is not necessarily an autonomous weapon. And there is no evidence in this reporting that Foundation's robots have autonomously selected and killed people.

Nevertheless, something significant is happening. A commercially developed, general-purpose humanoid robot is being considered for military operations in a conflict affecting millions of civilians. Its developers are openly pursuing combat capabilities. It has politically connected investors, military funding, and ambitions for large-scale industrial production.

We are not speculating about whether somebody might eventually want to build this technology.

Somebody is actively trying to build it.

For Lighthouse, this presents an opportunity to observe a potentially important technological transition while it is still unfolding.

## The problem with the way we talk about AI safety

Much of the discussion about advanced AI safety revolves around the possibility that artificial intelligence might escape human control.

We imagine an agent exceeding its permissions, compromising critical infrastructure, replicating across networks, or pursuing an objective that diverges from what its operators intended.

These are important risks. They form part of the motivation for Lighthouse's existing work on AI activity, software security, and the mechanisms by which computational systems might sustain and propagate themselves.

But there is another possibility that deserves equal conceptual attention.

**AI does not have to escape human control to be harmful. It can be harmful because it remains under human control.**

Consider three hypothetical events.

An AI agent escapes its sandbox and attacks critical infrastructure. An autonomous drone mistakenly identifies a civilian as a combatant. A government deploys autonomous robots to suppress peaceful protests, and the robots carry out their instructions flawlessly.

All three involve AI-related harm, but they have different causes.

In the first, the system exceeds the authority granted to it. In the second, it fails to exercise that authority correctly. In the third, the authority itself is the problem.

The third example is especially uncomfortable for the language of AI alignment.

A robot that more reliably follows the instructions of an abusive government might be better aligned with its operator. It might be more predictable, more secure, less susceptible to interference, and more effective at achieving its assigned objectives.

From the perspective of the people subjected to its actions, none of those improvements necessarily makes it safer.

Indeed, they could make it considerably more dangerous.

This suggests that Lighthouse should consider three overlapping sources of AI-related harm:

- **Accidental harm:** systems make mistakes, malfunction, or produce unintended consequences.
- **Autonomous harm:** systems exceed human control or acquire capabilities and authority beyond their intended boundaries.
- **Institutional harm:** people and organisations use AI to exercise power in ways that harm others.

These are not mutually exclusive. An institution can deliberately deploy a system whose errors harm civilians, or give a system more autonomy than it can responsibly supervise. But distinguishing them matters because the remedies are different.

Better engineering may reduce accidental harm. Better containment may reduce autonomous harm. Neither necessarily addresses institutional harm.

The question is not simply whether AI is safe.

It is safe for whom, aligned with whom, and operating under whose authority?

## The economics of power

One of the most interesting developments in contemporary AI is the changing relationship between human effort and machine activity.

Harbour, Lighthouse's companion project, explores this in the context of software development. A human can increasingly direct substantial amounts of computational work through agents, with a growing separation between the number of people giving instructions and the number of systems carrying them out.

This is exciting because it expands what individuals and small teams can accomplish.

But the same economic transformation can apply to other forms of activity.

Consider the exercise of military or policing power.

Maintaining a large human force requires recruitment, training, salaries, accommodation, logistics, coordination, and a continuous supply of people willing to perform the work. Human beings experience fatigue, fear, uncertainty and moral conflict. They can question an instruction or refuse to follow it.

Robotic forces would have substantial costs and constraints of their own: manufacturing, energy, maintenance, communications, transport, software reliability and human supervision. It would be a mistake to assume that a robotic force will automatically be cheaper or more effective than a human one.

Nevertheless, if the costs of physical autonomy decline sufficiently, the relationship between the size of an institution and the amount of physical power it can exercise could change.

A relatively small organisation might be able to direct a much larger number of machines. Those machines might maintain continuous surveillance, operate for longer periods, coordinate their activities rapidly, and follow instructions without many of the psychological limitations of human beings.

This could produce enormous benefits. Robots could remove explosives, rescue people from dangerous environments, and reduce the need to expose emergency workers or soldiers to lethal hazards.

It could also produce enormous harms.

A government might be able to sustain surveillance or coercion over a population at a lower cost. A military might become less constrained by the risk of casualties among its own personnel. An authoritarian regime might acquire a force that does not experience moral reluctance to obey an order.

The technology need not be different in each case. The institutional objectives and political circumstances are different.

This is a hypothesis about the economics of power, not a claim that robotic armies are already cheaper than human armies. It is something we should try to measure as the technology develops.

The central question is whether AI changes the cost, scale and persistence with which institutions can exercise power over individuals.

And importantly, who benefits from that change?

## Gaza and the asymmetry of protection

The proposed use of Foundation's robots in Gaza illustrates why the civilian perspective needs to be central to this research.

The argument for robotic tunnel operations is straightforward: why send a soldier into a dangerous environment when a machine could go instead?

From the perspective of the soldier, the family of that soldier, or the military responsible for their safety, this is a compelling argument.

But protecting the people operating a weapon is not the same as protecting the people subjected to it.

If a robot replaces an Israeli soldier in a tunnel, the risk to that soldier may decrease. The consequences for Palestinian civilians are a separate question.

A robot used to identify explosives or safely clear dangerous environments might protect people on both sides. A robot that makes destructive military operations easier to conduct might have very different consequences. A system that makes an occupying force easier to sustain could alter the wider political and humanitarian dynamics of a conflict.

These outcomes cannot be inferred from the robot's technical performance alone.

The company's stated ambition to make warfare more precise and reduce civilian casualties is a claim that should be taken seriously enough to investigate, rather than accepted as an inevitable consequence of better technology.

Does greater precision reduce the total amount of harm? Does it increase the number of operations considered feasible? Does reducing the risk to soldiers lower political barriers to military action? Can greater tactical accuracy coexist with worse overall humanitarian outcomes?

These are empirical questions. They are not answered by a demonstration of a robot climbing stairs, navigating rubble, or accurately recognising an object.

For Palestinian civilians, moreover, the potential arrival of these systems is not an abstract question about a speculative future. It concerns the authority a new class of military machine might exercise in their immediate environment.

Who decides whether a person is a threat? What information does the machine use? Can it recognise surrender, injury, or the presence of civilians? What happens if it gets something wrong? Can the person subjected to its actions establish who made the decision and challenge it?

The [International Committee of the Red Cross](https://www.icrc.org/en/article/faq-autonomous-weapon-systems) has emphasised precisely these difficulties in its work on autonomous weapons. Determining whether a person may lawfully be attacked is highly dependent on circumstances, including conduct, intent and changes in status. These are not simply problems of identifying a shape or recognising a pattern.

International humanitarian law already applies to weapons and military operations regardless of the technology involved. The obligations to distinguish civilians from combatants, avoid disproportionate civilian harm and take feasible precautions do not disappear when software enters the decision-making process.

The ICRC advocates new international rules prohibiting unpredictable autonomous weapons and systems designed to attack people autonomously, alongside strict restrictions on other autonomous weapons.

Lighthouse need not reproduce the considerable humanitarian, legal and policy research already being undertaken in this field. But it should understand that work, build upon it, and treat civilian outcomes as an essential part of understanding what AI does in the world.

## From the battlefield to the airport

The idea of an armed humanoid robot patrolling a civilian airport may feel futuristic.

Parts of that scenario are already ordinary reality.

In April 2023, the [Singapore Police Force began deploying autonomous patrol robots](https://www.police.gov.sg/media-hub/news/2023/20230615_operationalisation_of_patrol_robots_at_changi_airport_terminal_4) at Changi Airport's Terminal 4. The robots provide surveillance, communicate with members of the public, issue warnings and assist police officers in managing incidents.

They are not armed autonomous weapons. They represent an application of robotics to civilian policing.

Separately, in 2022, San Francisco's Board of Supervisors initially approved a policy allowing remotely controlled police robots to be used with potentially lethal force in exceptional circumstances. The proposal faced significant public opposition and the decision was subsequently reversed.

Neither example establishes that armed autonomous robots will become commonplace in civilian environments.

But they illustrate how the constituent parts of that future are emerging through separate technological and institutional decisions.

Navigation becomes autonomous. Surveillance becomes continuous. Information from sensors is interpreted by software. Machines communicate warnings and instructions. Police departments acquire greater robotic capabilities. Governments debate the authority those machines should have to apply force.

There is no single moment at which a humanoid robot becomes a fully autonomous police officer. There are many smaller decisions about what machines are permitted to observe, infer, decide and do.

Some of those decisions may be made by legislatures. Others by police departments, procurement teams, software developers, or companies introducing new products.

The battlefield is one environment in which pressure to expand robotic capabilities is particularly strong. Military customers have compelling incentives to reduce danger to their personnel, extend operational reach and respond rapidly to threats.

Civilian security organisations have some similar incentives. The underlying technologies, including batteries, processors, navigation systems, computer vision, robotics software and manufacturing infrastructure, can serve both markets.

Foundation is an unusually clear example of this overlap. In July 2026, [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/business/eric-trump-backed-foundation-partners-with-amd-develop-humanoid-robots-2026-07-23/) on its partnership with AMD to develop robots for industrial and military applications. The company was already claiming automotive manufacturing deployments while announcing ambitious plans for future production.

A general-purpose robotic platform can benefit from advances made in factories and warehouses, while military customers provide funding and incentives for new capabilities.

Whether and how those capabilities subsequently transfer into civilian security is a research question in its own right.

The future is not inevitable. Public opposition, regulation, technical limitations and institutional choices can alter the course of deployment. The San Francisco example is a reminder of precisely that.

But we should be observing the trajectory rather than assuming either that it cannot happen or that it cannot be prevented.

## The political and commercial feedback loop

Foundation's political connections provide another reason to pay attention.

Eric Trump, the son of the serving United States president, is an investor and strategic adviser to the company. The Financial Times reports that Foundation and a business it acquired have received substantial American military contracts and funding, citing the company's president.

This has prompted questions about potential conflicts of interest. The company and Eric Trump's representatives deny that he played a role in securing government contracts.

The relationship deserves scrutiny, but the existence of a political connection is not proof that improper influence occurred.

For Lighthouse, there is also a broader structural question that exists independently of this particular family or administration.

Military demand can fund commercial research. Commercial research can produce more capable machines. Improved capabilities can make new military applications possible. New applications can generate further government demand, investment and research.

A reinforcing loop may emerge.

This is particularly interesting when the technology is general-purpose. Improvements made for civilian industrial customers can benefit military products, while military funding can help finance capabilities that later become commercially available elsewhere.

The result could be a growing infrastructure for physical autonomy that is supported simultaneously by commercial, military and governmental incentives.

Those incentives need not be malicious for the overall direction to create serious risks.

A company wants to sell robots. A military wants to protect soldiers. A police department wants to increase coverage. An investor wants a return. A government wants to reduce costs.

Each participant may be pursuing a locally reasonable objective.

The combined effect could nevertheless be a substantial increase in the amount of surveillance, coercion and physical force that institutions are able to exercise.

This is exactly the kind of system-level effect Lighthouse should be interested in: outcomes that arise from interacting technologies, institutions and incentives, rather than from the behaviour of any one AI model.

## Where this fits within Lighthouse

Lighthouse began with an interest in AI activity across the internet: the computational work taking place, the traces it leaves, and the mechanisms by which its influence might propagate.

Our existing work asks how AI affects software production and security, how its activity can be observed, and what happens when increasingly capable agents interact with the systems that sustain the digital world.

Robotics introduces a new dimension.

A computational system can now acquire physical agency. Its decisions can influence the movement of machines, the operation of infrastructure, and potentially the application of force against human beings.

There are several natural connections to our existing research.

**Software security and dependencies.** The integrity of software becomes especially consequential when that software controls physical machines. Vulnerabilities, compromised dependencies, insecure update mechanisms and failures of access control can acquire direct physical consequences.

**Capability diffusion.** Research, models, software and hardware can move between civilian and military applications. Observing the pathways through which capabilities become available may help explain how relatively specialised systems evolve into widely accessible infrastructure.

**The economics of autonomy.** The costs of directing computational and physical activity influence who can deploy AI, at what scale, and with what consequences. Harbour's work on agent economics offers a useful conceptual starting point, although physical robotics has very different cost structures.

**Human authority and supervision.** As systems scale, the relationship between the human operator and the machine's decisions changes. Formal human oversight is not necessarily meaningful oversight. A person nominally responsible for hundreds of machines may have limited ability to understand or intervene in their actions.

**Observability and accountability.** When an AI-mediated decision harms someone, can we reconstruct what happened? Can we distinguish the actions of the model, the software, the operator and the institution? Can affected individuals obtain the information necessary to challenge a decision?

This last question deserves particular attention.

In software engineering, observability helps us understand and debug systems. In the context of institutional power, observability can also be a prerequisite for accountability.

If AI allows decisions to become faster, more distributed and less intelligible to the people affected by them, it may also change the practical ability of society to hold institutions responsible.

Lighthouse does not need to become an observatory of military operations. Its distinctive contribution could be to observe the computational ecosystem that enables those operations, the diffusion of capabilities between domains, and the consequences of increasing delegation to machines.

But the perspective must extend beyond the organisations deploying the technology.

We cannot understand the safety of these systems solely by observing how well they serve their operators.

## A modest beginning

The Foundation story offers a useful starting point precisely because much of what is being discussed has not yet been established as reality.

There is an opportunity to preserve a baseline: what the company has proposed, what it has demonstrated, what has been funded, what has actually been deployed, and what remains unknown.

Over time, that record could reveal how a proposed military application evolves into an operational capability, changes direction, or fails to materialise.

The initial research need not be a large undertaking. A bounded investigation could establish the relevant history, connect existing work on autonomous weapons and civilian protection, and identify what Lighthouse is genuinely positioned to observe.

Some suggested questions:

- How do general-purpose robotic systems acquire military applications, and how do those capabilities subsequently diffuse?
- Which decisions are actually delegated to machines, and how does that delegation change over time?
- What evidence supports claims about lower costs, increased precision, reduced casualties, or greater operational capacity?
- How are the benefits and harms distributed between operators, combatants and civilians?
- What technical, institutional and legal safeguards exist, and what evidence is there that they are effective?
- Which of these developments leave observable traces in the public computational, scientific and commercial ecosystem?

This could also be an early application of Lighthouse's emerging work on research lineage: linking technical developments, economic evidence, humanitarian research and policy decisions into a connected record that can grow over time.

There are important limitations. Military capabilities are frequently secret, corporate announcements are not independent evidence, and the absence of public reporting cannot establish the absence of harm. Evidence about civilian experiences should not be subordinated to whatever information is easiest to obtain from technology companies and governments.

Nor should the research become a means of improving weapons deployment. Its purpose would be to understand technological development, civilian consequences, accountability and the risks of increasing autonomy, not to optimise targeting or military effectiveness.

The initial investigation should help establish whether this is a durable research direction for Lighthouse, and what its distinctive contribution might be.

## The machine that obeys

There is a familiar image in discussions about the future of artificial intelligence: the machine that rebels against its creators.

It is a powerful story. It captures a genuine anxiety about creating systems that we can no longer understand or control.

But perhaps we should spend more time thinking about a different machine.

The machine that obeys.

It follows its instructions. It works reliably. It makes its operator more productive. It can act at a scale and speed that would previously have required many people. It does not question the authority of the institution that controls it.

It could be a wonderful technology.

It could also be an instrument of extraordinary oppression.

The difference may have very little to do with how intelligent the machine is.

Lighthouse watches what happens as machines acquire agency. This research direction would add a complementary question: what happens as people acquire power through those machines?

The two transformations are related, but they are not the same.

And one day, if we do find ourselves walking through an airport past an armed robotic security officer, the most important question may not be whether the machine is intelligent, conscious, or aligned with its operator.

It may simply be this:

**Who is accountable for what this machine is allowed to do to me?**
