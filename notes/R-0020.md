# R-0020: rarer-further-down, reader review

Stage one, written after reading only the full piece, its short form and the README's "Current focus", and nothing else in the repository.

## What a reader would believe, written before reading the record

**The finding, said back.** Among Python's fifty most downloaded libraries, a new version usually became half of the downloads within two days (26 of 37 that released). Further down it is rarer: about half of libraries ranked 51 to 500, a quarter of those ranked 501 to 5,000. New versions there start from a smaller first-day share (typically 58, 48 and 23 in a hundred) and mostly keep it for a month.

**Observed.** Daily downloads by version from a public copy of PyPI's log, for 80 libraries drawn at random from each band, 52 and 35 of which released between April and August 2026, measured as the top fifty were, by rules fixed beforehand. Libraries mostly fell wholly one way or the other. Mirrors fetched under one per cent and changed no result.

**Explained.** Only that the drop is mostly in the size of a new version's share, not its speed, and that mirrors are not behind it. Nothing is said to cause anything: "rank is not a reason", and dependents, pinning, Python versions and release frequency were not measured. A reader learns that lower rank goes with smaller early uptake, not why.

**Limits a reader would notice.** Small samples with wide intervals, so the first band may be over half. A download is a fetch, not a person or machine, so a small share need not mean a slow spread. Pillow and sentry-sdk were chosen afterwards as illustrations. The short form's summary and the README say 52 and 35 libraries were "drawn"; the full piece says 80 were drawn and 52 and 35 released.

## What the round establishes

Stage two read the record (LH014.md 0.1), its brief, amendment 1, sources, scripts and retained data, investigations/software-updates.md, programme.md's Next and LH013's brief, and LH011's retained releases and counts for the top fifty; not notes/, releases.md, commit messages or review/r0019_*. Every calculation is in studies/LH014/review/r0020_inference.py and r0020_inference.txt, offline from the retained counts, no ClickPy read and no day after 27 September; the script recomputed every release's shares and matched the retained outcomes for all 915 releases before anything else was read. All of it is post hoc description.

The measurements are sound, and what a reader would believe (above) is what the evidence supports, with one wording error. The piece's one explanatory claim, that the drop is in the size of a new version's share rather than its speed, holds when measured on single days: the share of libraries whose releases mostly hold half on day 30 is 70, 50 and 31 per cent (top fifty, band A, band B) against 70, 48 and 26 within two days, so a month barely narrows the gap. Counted as "reached half on any day up to 30" the bands read 62 and 49 per cent, but that maximum over days counts a release that crosses half for a day and falls back, as libcst's, junitparser's and service-identity's do.

The record can say what the "size" is. For releases whose replaced version was at least two weeks old (56, 71 and 45 releases in 26, 33 and 21 libraries), the share that "the previous newest version or newer" already held on the day before the release, E, was a median 62, 50 and 31 per cent; the new release's day-30 share differed from E by a median −0.2, −2.1 and 0.0 points and lay within ten points of it for 82, 72 and 75 per cent of releases; among releases with E below one half, 0 of 21, 1 of 38 and 1 of 32 reached half within two days, and among those with E at or above one half, 31 of 35, 29 of 33 and 8 of 13 did; a library's median E sits on the same side of one half as its two-day outcome for 26 of 26, 28 of 33 and 18 of 21 libraries. A new version takes over the share its predecessor ran at, in every band. The gradient is in how much of a library's traffic floats to its newest version at all, visible before any release, which is what LH012 and LH013 ask of the top fifty as "what stays behind". Speed is the smaller part: the day-2 share fell short of E by a median 3.7, 5.2 and 3.0 points, with a heavier tail in the bands (lower quartiles −8, −14 and −13).

Also: the two-day difference between the top fifty and band A is 22 points with a resampled interval of 2 to 42 (the brief fixed no such test), so "fewer" holds for both bands; within a band, rank and the day-1 share are barely related (rho −0.21 in A, +0.02 in B; −0.33 across the three groups), so the gradient lies between the groups, not along the ranks within one; the all-or-nothing pattern holds among libraries with three or more releases (19 of 25 and 15 of 18), though 16 and 11 single-release libraries are counted in it; the three named mirror tools exceeded one per cent of a typical release's own first-two-day downloads for 10 of 35 band B libraries, a tenth for djlint, still changing no outcome.

## Material claim problems

1. **The sample misstated in the short pieces.** articles/short/rarer-further-down.md, summary: "about half of 52 drawn from ranks 51 to 500, and about a quarter of 35 from ranks 501 to 5,000"; README.md, Current focus: "25 of 52 drawn at random from those ranked 51 to 500, and 9 of 35 from those ranked 501 to 5,000"; investigations/software-updates.md, Where the evidence stands: "for 25 of 52 libraries drawn at random from those ranked 51 to 500 ... and for 9 of 35 from those ranked 501 to 5,000". Evidence: amendments.md and data/drawn.csv, 80 drawn from each band; data/analysis/summary.csv, "drawn projects" 80 and "projects with a qualifying release" 52 and 35. A reader of these three believes 52 and 35 libraries were drawn and every one released, and does not learn that 35 and 56 per cent of the drawn libraries put out no qualifying version in five months, which is part of what "further down" is like. Resolution: "25 of the 52, of 80 drawn at random, that released", and likewise for band B; the full piece has it right.

None other. The headline, the intervals, the opening examples (Pillow 52.7 per cent of 20,043,783 downloads on day 1 and 63.6 on day 30; sentry-sdk 30.8 and 31.2), the settling figures, the mirror figures and the post hoc exclusion all match the retained data, and the piece's causal restraint matches the brief's.

## Readability

2. Full piece, "Fewer reached half further down": say that 16 and 11 of the libraries that went "one way or the other" had a single new version, and that among those with three or more it still held for 19 of 25 and 15 of 18.

## Next step

**Adjust the next pace reading's brief, and otherwise wait for LH013; do not draw again on the same measure now.**

- The explanations in play for the gradient are sampling (band A against the top), a smaller floating fraction further down, and slower arrival of a library's traffic on its newest version. The retained data already separate them as a description: sampling is unlikely (2 to 42 points), the floating fraction carries most of the gradient, and speed a minority with a heavier tail in the bands. The record's proposed fresh draw on September releases (LH014.md, Next) repeats the two-day measure and would test sampling and the tool and young-project exclusion, but as it stands it cannot separate the other two. Fixing two measures in that brief would: E, which needs the replaced version of each library's first release kept out of the "~other" fold (a small change to collect_counts.py's numerator set), and the day-2 share as a fraction of E; with a test that the two-day outcome among releases with E at or above one half differs between bands by no more than a fixed margin. Its evidence is held from about 1 November 2026, when September's last releases have their day 30.
- Because the floating fraction dominates, the gradient is a gradient in what stays behind, which LH013 asks of the top fifty only, from about 13 October. Once LH013 says whether the older share holds week to week, widening LH012's measures (older share, Python exclusion, walk-shaped profiles) to the 87 band libraries on LH013's weeks would say what a smaller floating fraction further down is made of, at ClickPy's cost only and cheaper per library than at the top; that sequencing meets the programme's objection to widening before the baseline is known to hold.
- For the watch, the usable result now: a library's own E, read from the day before a release, is the baseline for its new version's share; a band's share is a coarse substitute for it.
- Before release: the wording fix above; and the E reading could go into the record's Next as a post hoc description with a pointer to review/r0020_inference.txt. I edited nothing but this note and the two review files; I did not rerun the replay or render the figure.
