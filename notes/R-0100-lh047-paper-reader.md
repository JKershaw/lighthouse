## What a reader would believe, written before reading the record

**Principal finding, said back.** Most of what websites ask of bots is a request nothing enforces, and published counts of compliance disagree, partly because operators say user-triggered fetches fall outside robots.txt. When Lighthouse audited its own agents for one evening, 113 of at least 3,497 requests broke a rule it had fixed in advance (it had known of 2); most were one careless collector hitting PyPI's API, all "not found". Adding a post hoc class gives 172, a rule adopted later 191.

**Observed.** The requests, their timestamps, responses and the robots.txt text (some from later copies); the dashboard incident; others' published figures, as quoted.

**Explained.** The breaches are explained by habits: tools and one-off commands that never read robots.txt, one truncated read, one unreachable file ignored, an anonymous user agent. The disagreement among published counts is explained, "we think", partly by scope over user-triggered fetchers.

**Caused.** The collector not reading robots.txt caused the 85; a partial read caused the dashboard requests. No harm is implied: requests, not incidents.

**Limits a reader would notice.** One evening, self-judged, by agents of the same model family; 3,497 is a floor; some verdicts rest on later copies of robots.txt; 113 versus 172 versus 191 depends on rule choices; most counted breaches are trivial (not found, robots.txt-read counted as a request); not a rate for AI agents generally. A reader might still take "113" as a measure of how badly Lighthouse behaves rather than how strict its rulebook is.
