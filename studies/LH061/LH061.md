# LH061: What does published evidence measure about whether containment and recovery work, and what does it only assert?

**In short.** We asked what published research and exercise reports measure about whether containing and recovering from internet incidents works, and what they only assert. For containment (taking down domains and botnets, sinkholing, removing or revoking leaked keys and certificates), seven research documents were included, one of them a laboratory test of recovery that the brief named for this part, and 22 of their 24 outcome claims were measured; but the study admitted only documents that report an outcome and kept vendors' pages and blogs out as leads, so that share describes the outcome studies it let in, not what's said about containment generally, and their results, too varied to add up, run in every direction. For recovery exercises, two organisers' after-action reports were included and neither reports a measured outcome, only participants' answers and organisers' statements (three of the five named exercise series gave nothing to read: two were walled or refused, and for the third we found no public report); since the two parts drew on different kinds of document, the gap between them says as much about the sources as about containment and recovery, and the study's only measured recovery outcomes come from that laboratory test. This version isn't released: a fresh check of what its last two revisions wrote cleared it with small corrections, and a line on the investigation page comes next.

## Answer

**Part A, containment.** Seven documents were included: five on stopping harm in the field (takedowns, sinkholing, and revocation or removal of exposed credentials and certificates), one on repository owners removing their own leaked secrets (A02, whose fit is a judgement), and one laboratory test of recovery (A08) that the brief named for this part. Their authors' 24 outcome claims are 22 measured outcomes, 1 modelled and 1 asserted (derived, tally). That share is mostly built into the sample: the brief admitted only documents from its listed venues (security conferences and journals, arXiv, and six agencies' and bodies' reports) that measure, model or report an outcome, excluded guidance that reports none, and kept vendors' pages, blogs and news out as leads only, though its writer expected credential-revocation outcomes to come mostly from vendors. So 22 of 24 describes the outcome studies the rule let in, which report outcomes by construction; it doesn't say how much of what's claimed about containment in general has been measured, and the study couldn't count assertions outside those studies (interpretation). Of the 22 measured claims, 20 are in the field and 2 in a laboratory; 13 rest on companies' own data under the vendor rule in Method (5 on a vendor's customer or product telemetry or sample repository, 8 on passive DNS databases, 5 of them commercial and 3 one company's internal database); 10 rest on data that isn't public, 9 on partly public data and 3 on public data. What they report runs every way, each in its authors' terms (observations):

- lookups that stopped on the day of one botnet takedown, and mixed results at two others (Nadji and others, CCS 2013);
- domains that stayed down about two years on average, more than half later released for sale (Alowaisheq and others, NDSS 2019);
- 81 per cent of leaked secrets not removed by their repository owners within 16 days (Meli and others, NDSS 2019);
- abused code-signing certificates revoked 1 to 1,553 days after malware signed with them was first detected, 171.4 days on average, with some revocations not ending trust in malware signed earlier and some later removed from the lists (Kim and others, USENIX Security 2018);
- exposed credentials halved 14 days after notification, exposures rising again by day 172 while revocations kept falling (Demir and others, arXiv 2603.12498);
- one malware operation growing during a sinkhole operation and two falling after seizures (Ife and others, arXiv 2104.01631);
- and, in the laboratory test of recovery, permanent loss after AI agents' damaging database statements from none to 15 per cent by engine, figures the authors say their test harness weighs on (Kaya and others, arXiv 2611.30483). This measures undoing damage, which is recovery rather than stopping harm under way, and its two claims are the study's only measured recovery outcomes; it is counted in Part A because the brief named it there.

The measures, outcomes, populations and windows (14 days to about six years) are too different to set side by side, so each is reported alone, as the brief directs (interpretation). Nothing here says whether any containment measure works in general or now.

**Part B, recovery exercises.** Inconclusive under the brief on two counts: fewer than three documents were included, and none of their claims is a measured outcome. The two included reports, SIFMA's Quantum Dawn VII after-action report and Public Safety Canada's Cyber-Physical Capstone after-action report, carry 12 outcome claims: 8 self-reported and 4 asserted (derived, tally). Neither gives a measured time to restore a service, and neither reports a measured outcome (the Capstone report counts the exercise's delivery, such as scenario injects and active users, which isn't an outcome); Quantum Dawn VII says its design took in lessons from Quantum Dawn VI but reports no follow-up of their implementation, and the Capstone, a pilot, states none (observations). Both are organisers' own after-action reports, and the searches found no peer-reviewed evaluation of an exercise, so this is a result about two reports of that kind, not about exercise reporting in general, and the contrast with Part A is partly one of source type: Part A's documents are outcome studies admitted because they report outcomes (interpretation). An exercise's result is never evidence of real recovery. The study's only measured recovery outcomes come from the laboratory test in Part A (A08), which is not an exercise. Three of the five named series gave nothing to read: two couldn't be read at all (Cyber Storm, both editions walled; Cyber Europe, refused by robots.txt), and for the third no public report was located (Locked Shields, whose series page links none), so the readable set may lean towards openly published reports (the brief's statement, applied).

## Method

The brief's eight queries (QA1 to QA5, QB1 to QB3) were run once each, verbatim and in order, on 25 November 2026; none errored, none was reworded or rerun (searches.md). QA5 returned only public-health results, so isolation and quarantine are **not found by this search**, not untested. Candidates were screened on the title and URL alone, since the tool showed no venue or year. Part A had 9 candidates (7 known documents and 2 from searches); Part B had 11 slots (five named series by two editions, plus one admitted series, Public Safety Canada's Cyber-Physical exercise, the first other series in QB1's results). No other QB result met the admission rule.

Each document was read by one gated one-off GET through scripts/lh_fetch.py, with the robots gate applied before the request and any redirect; A03 took three (below). From fetchlog.csv (derived, tally): 15 document GETs were made (Part A 10, Part B 5), 12 answered HTTP 200 and 3 met walls (A04 with HTTP 301; B01 and B06 with HTTP 403); 3 documents were refused by robots.txt before any request (B02, B03, B08); 5 of the 20 GETs were unused. Five locator rows were logged: three requests with a status (two 200, one 403), one refused before any request (ENISA) and one request whose redirect to a disallowed path wasn't followed (Bank of England), within the brief's eight. No body was kept: no read document but A07 states a licence allowing redistribution, and A07's body wasn't kept because its PDF's compressed streams can hold email addresses the redaction can't reach. Each read document has a derived file with its URL, read time, status, SHA-256, the facts used and short quotations. Inclusion was decided on the document read, by each part's three conditions, and each claim was classed once, with setting and vendor labels, by the reading subagent. The evidence review (R-0158) checked every class against rereads of the same bytes; this version applies its corrections (A05-3 and A08-1 restated, A05-5's setting laboratory, A05-1, A05-3 and A05-4 labelled vendor). A03's four claims were classed by the revising subagent and haven't been checked against the paper by anyone else (the recheck, R-0161, compared them only with the derived file).

**The vendor rule.** The brief labels vendor "a claim resting on a vendor's own customers or product telemetry". From version 0.3 the label is applied by one stated rule: a claim is vendor when the observations it counts (its units, their dates and their outcomes) come in whole or in part from data a company collects and holds for itself or sells, that is, its customers' or products' telemetry, its sample repository, or a passive DNS database, commercial or internal. Who the authors work for is recorded as an interest beside the document and isn't part of the label. Data a company or project publishes openly (GitHub's search results in A02, HTTP Archive's crawl in A07) don't make a claim vendor, nor does a public document found by way of company data (A03-3's revocation lists, which their issuers publish). Under this rule A01's five claims, which rest in part on commercial passive DNS, are relabelled vendor; version 0.2 had labelled A05's claims on one company's internal passive DNS vendor and A01's on commercial passive DNS not, a difference the reader review (R-0160) found had no ground in the brief. The rule was stated after every claim had been read and labelled, so it is a post hoc reading of the brief's definition, applied to all 36 claims; no other label changes under it. Part A's vendor count becomes 14 of 24 claims, 13 of the 22 measured, which doesn't meet the brief's condition that all measured claims rest on vendor samples.

Version 0.1 excluded it as full text unavailable. The derived file of 25 November kept no link to the paper, so on 26 November 2026 the revising subagent made one more gated GET of the project page at 20:47:55 UTC (HTTP 200, the same 8,522 bytes, sha256 4546ba7e...203b4810, as on 25 November), only to find the URL of its "Paper" link, and then one gated GET of that URL at 20:48:06 UTC (HTTP 200, a PDF of 365,567 bytes, sha256 d6216cd4...98f73079).
