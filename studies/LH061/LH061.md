# LH061: What does published evidence measure about whether containment and recovery work, and what does it only assert?

**In short.** We asked how much of what's said about containing and recovering from internet incidents has actually been measured. For containment (taking down domains and botnets, sinkholing, removing or revoking leaked keys and certificates), seven documents were read and included, and 22 of their 24 outcome claims were measured by the authors, mostly in the field, though the measures differ too much to add up and the results run in every direction. For recovery exercises, only two reports could be read and included, and neither reports a measured outcome: their twelve claims are participants' own answers or the organisers' statements, and three of the five named exercise series couldn't be read at all. An evidence review checked the first version and this one corrects it and adds the paper it found still unread; a fresh agent's reading of the changes, a reader review and a recheck come before anything is said elsewhere.

## Answer

**Part A, containment.** Seven documents were included, and their authors' 24 outcome claims are 22 measured outcomes, 1 modelled and 1 asserted (derived, tally). Of the 22 measured claims, 20 are in the field and 2 in a laboratory; 8 rest on security companies' own data (5 on a vendor's customer or product telemetry or sample repository, 3 on a company's internal passive DNS database); 10 rest on data that isn't public, 9 on partly public data and 3 on public data. What they report runs every way, each in its authors' terms (observations): lookups that stopped on the day of one botnet takedown and mixed results at two others (Nadji and others, CCS 2013); domains that stayed down about two years on average, more than half later released for sale (Alowaisheq and others, NDSS 2019); 81 per cent of leaked secrets not removed within 16 days (Meli and others, NDSS 2019); abused code-signing certificates revoked 1 to 1,553 days after malware signed with them was first detected, 171.4 days on average, with some revocations not ending trust in malware signed earlier and some later removed from the lists (Kim and others, USENIX Security 2018); exposed credentials halved 14 days after notification, exposures rising again by day 172 while revocations kept falling (Demir and others, arXiv 2603.12498); one malware operation growing during a sinkhole operation and two falling after seizures (Ife and others, arXiv 2104.01631); and, in a laboratory test of recovery that the brief named for this study, permanent loss after AI agents' damaging database statements from none to 15 per cent by engine, figures the authors say their test harness weighs on (Kaya and others, arXiv 2611.30483). That last document measures undoing damage, which is recovery rather than stopping harm under way; it is counted here because the brief named it for Part A. The measures, outcomes, populations and windows (14 days to about six years) are too different to set side by side, so each is reported alone, as the brief directs (interpretation). Nothing here says whether any containment measure works in general or now.

**Part B, recovery exercises.** Inconclusive under the brief on two counts: fewer than three documents were included, and none of their claims is a measured outcome. The two included reports, SIFMA's Quantum Dawn VII after-action report and Public Safety Canada's Cyber-Physical Capstone after-action report, carry 12 outcome claims: 8 self-reported and 4 asserted (derived, tally). Neither gives a measured time to restore a service, and neither reports a measured outcome (the Capstone report counts the exercise's delivery, such as scenario injects and active users, which isn't an outcome); Quantum Dawn VII says its design took in lessons from Quantum Dawn VI but reports no follow-up of their implementation, and the Capstone, a pilot, states none (observations). This is a result about the two reports read, not about exercise reporting in general, and an exercise's result is never evidence of real recovery. Three of the five named series (Cyber Storm, Cyber Europe, Locked Shields) couldn't be read at all, so the readable set may lean towards openly published reports (the brief's statement, applied).

## Method

The brief's eight queries (QA1 to QA5, QB1 to QB3) were run once each, verbatim and in order, on 25 November 2026; none errored, none was reworded or rerun (searches.md). QA5 returned only public-health results, so isolation and quarantine are **not found by this search**, not untested. Candidates were screened on the title and URL alone, since the tool showed no venue or year. Part A had 9 candidates (7 known documents and 2 from searches); Part B had 11 slots (five named series by two editions, plus one admitted series, Public Safety Canada's Cyber-Physical exercise, the first other series in QB1's results). No other QB result met the admission rule.

Each document was read by one gated one-off GET through scripts/lh_fetch.py, with the robots gate applied before the request and any redirect; A03 took three (below). From fetchlog.csv (derived, tally): 15 document GETs were made (Part A 10, Part B 5), 12 answered HTTP 200 and 3 met walls (A04 with HTTP 301; B01 and B06 with HTTP 403); 3 documents were refused by robots.txt before any request (B02, B03, B08); 5 of the 20 GETs were unused. Five locator rows were logged: three requests with a status (two 200, one 403), one refused before any request (ENISA) and one request whose redirect to a disallowed path wasn't followed (Bank of England), within the brief's eight. No body was kept: no read document but A07 states a licence allowing redistribution, and A07's body wasn't kept because its PDF's compressed streams can hold email addresses the redaction can't reach. Each read document has a derived file with its URL, read time, status, SHA-256, the facts used and short quotations. Inclusion was decided on the document read, by each part's three conditions, and each claim was classed once, with setting and vendor labels, by the reading subagent. The evidence review (R-0158) checked every class against rereads of the same bytes; this version applies its corrections (A05-3 and A08-1 restated, A05-5's setting laboratory, A05-1, A05-3 and A05-4 labelled vendor). A03's four claims were classed by the revising subagent and haven't been checked by anyone else.

**A03.** Its first GET, on 25 November 2026, used a search result's URL, which redirected to the authors' project page: the title, authors, abstract and links, not the paper. Version 0.1 excluded it as full text unavailable. The evidence review ruled that a page answering 200 that isn't the document is not a wall, refusal or error, the brief's only grounds for that exclusion, and that a GET of the full text was still allowed within the twenty before 15 December 2026, counting the first GET as used. The derived file of 25 November kept no link to the paper, so on 26 November 2026 the revising subagent made one more gated GET of the project page at 20:47:55 UTC (HTTP 200, the same 8,522 bytes, sha256 4546ba7e...203b4810, as on 25 November), only to find the URL of its "Paper" link, and then one gated GET of that URL at 20:48:06 UTC (HTTP 200, a PDF of 365,567 bytes, sha256 d6216cd4...98f73079). Both used fetch.py through scripts/lh_fetch.py with access 'oneoff', redirects checked and provider anthropic, and both count against the twenty. The second page GET was a decision made after the first read's outcome was seen; the brief says the abstract page isn't fetched separately, and this fetch was made only because no link had been retained, not to read the abstract, from which nothing is claimed. The paper states no licence, so no body was kept, and keep.py wrote derived/A03-kim-usenix2018-page.json and derived/A03-kim-usenix2018-paper.json. The paper meets Part A's three conditions (revocation of abused credentials in the field; measured delay, persistence and reach of revocation; population, sources and periods stated), so it is included with four claims. The derived file of 25 November (derived/A03-kim-usenix2018.json) is left as read; its exclusion is superseded.

**The stop.** The brief stops reading when the eight searches are run and the twenty GETs are spent or both candidate lists are exhausted, or at the end of 15 December 2026. Version 0.1 judged the stop met; the evidence review found it wasn't, because A03's full text was still readable (above). Checked again on 26 November 2026, after A03's read, against the brief and fetchlog.csv: the eight searches are run, and both lists are now exhausted in the sense the brief allows. Every candidate was read, walled, refused by robots.txt, or not locatable within the rules (A06 had no URL and the brief allows locator reads only for exercise series; SIMEX's and Locked Shields' one locator read each was spent without finding a report). The order of reading never bound, since neither part's list outran its GETs. No candidate remains readable within the five unused GETs before 15 December: the brief allows a retry only on a timeout, connection error or HTTP 500, 502 or 504, so A04's 301 and the two 403s stand as walls; the robots refusals at ENISA and at Quantum Dawn VIII's host can't be read around, and fetching the same Quantum Dawn VIII file name from SIFMA's main host would route around a refusal. So the stop is met, and this record is version 0.2, not an interim record (derived from the brief and fetch log).

**Safety conditions as applied.** The research safety assessment's narrowing ([the keeper's decision of 24 November 2026](../../notes/keeper/2026-11-24-defensive-side.md)) was followed in the reads and in this record: outcomes and durations only; where authors give reasons a measure failed or an effect faded (A01-2, A03-2, A03-3, A03-4, A05-2, A07-3, A09-1, A09-2), the record says only that they give reasons and tallies none; no host, domain, credential or indicator was enumerated, resolved or visited, and no dataset, sinkhole or dashboard was fetched; no participant, operator or system is named beyond a report's own level (A01-5's "one technology company", B11-8's unnamed organisations, A07's organisations described generally); A07-4 names providers as its authors do; A03's certificate authorities, revocation list addresses and the problems it reports by authority are not named or restated, and its reasons for A03-2 to A03-4 are not restated; no dataset the authors released was fetched; no dependency map is compiled; A08 is recorded only for recoverability; the technical package the Capstone page mentions was not used; nobody was contacted; robots.txt was obeyed under the one-off rule; email addresses are withheld.

## Limits

- One run of eight fixed queries, with a search tool that showed no venue or year, is a narrow window on the literature; what it didn't surface is not found by this search, not absent (the brief's rule).
- Five search results showed no title (QA1 results 1 and 3, QA2 results 8 and 10, the last an arXiv preprint, and QA3 result 6) and one no venue (QA3 result 5), and were set aside on that metadata. The brief screens on title or snippet, and snippets weren't kept, so whether any concerned the question can't be checked.
- Part A's documents are few and varied: four peer-reviewed papers from 2013, 2018 and 2019 and three arXiv texts from 2021 and 2026, two of which state peer-reviewed publication. Their observation windows run from 14 days to about six years, from 2011 to 2026; most of their data aren't public, and the nine vendor-labelled claims rest on three documents' relationships with security companies.
- A03's paper gives two different counts for one finding (45 certificates and 250 samples in its text, 62 and 402 in its summary table) and doesn't reconcile them; A03-2 gives both.
- The reads are one agent's per document and no body is kept. Classes, directions and paraphrases were checked against rereads of the same bytes by the evidence review (R-0158), which corrected A05-3 and A08-1; some of the corrected wording (A05-3's spike, A05's data source, A08-1's added figures and the other notes marked so in claims.csv) rests on that reread alone, since the derived files of 25 November don't hold it. A03's claims are unchecked.
- Part B rests on two reports, both of 2023 exercises, both inconclusive by the brief's own conditions.
