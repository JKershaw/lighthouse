# Can public evidence establish that ARTEX operations used acquired AI credentials to sustain further credential hunting?

**In short.** We asked whether public reports can show that people running ARTEX, an open-source AI hacking tool, used stolen AI keys to pay for the computing that powered their hunt for more keys. In the six reports we could read on 17 November, only Amazon describes that loop for ARTEX, and every step of it rests on the attackers' own agent records, which Amazon says it cannot verify; the other ARTEX reports neither confirm nor contradict it, and a report on a different operation describes a similar loop, also mostly from the agent's own records. This is unreviewed working material, and for now the answer is that public evidence shows the loop was intended and recorded, but doesn't yet show, from anything beyond the attackers' own records, that it worked. Next, reviewers check this record, and a targeted search looks for the missing reports that could test each step.

## Answer

Unreviewed. All of it is drawn from six documents read on 17 November 2026; Check Point's report could not be read.

**What the sources establish about ARTEX (question 1).** Several actors have used ARTEX since at least August 2026, by the FBI's synthesis of other reports (C001). Two publishers describe their own observation: Microsoft says it watched one cluster, Storm-3358, use it from October (C060 to C062), and Amazon says its decoy sensors captured ARTEX agents' requests from 3 October to 5 November (C020). Every source that speaks to control describes people setting objectives, targets and limits, with agents doing the work inside them. The direct evidence for that is the operators' instructions Amazon's sensors captured (C027, C029, C032); "minimal human intervention" is each publisher's own reading, and none gives a measure of how often people stepped in (C002, C028, C045). Persistence is reported as shared task memory inside an operation (C033), infrastructure seen over days or weeks (C048, C065, C079, C080), and copies of the code spread by people (C007, C088, C124); no source reports running agents spreading themselves. Scale comes in units that can't be combined: hosts with an exposed console (Censys, 1,465 as of 3 or 4 November, C070, C071), console instances (the FBI, citing Check Point, over 1,600 in early November, C006), targets named in agent records ("hundreds", C034), tenants with attempted sign-ins (more than 2,250, one Microsoft origin repeated in three of our sources, C005, C086). Results that a publisher says it saw itself are few (Microsoft's RAT downloads, C062). Most results are relayed from reports we haven't read, and Amazon's are unverified agent records, including a deepfake campaign that failed in every attempt the agents recorded (C043).

**What supports each link of the loop (question 2, round 1 part).** Public evidence in this corpus does not establish the loop for ARTEX. That is inconclusive, not a finding that the loop didn't happen. Only Amazon describes it for ARTEX, and there all six links (acquiring credentials, showing they work, loading them into a gateway, using the inference, that use enabling further hunting, and more usable resources coming back) rest on agent records Amazon says it cannot verify, plus Amazon's own interpretation; it gives no count of keys, no spend or token figure, and no victim's or provider's confirmation (C023, C025, C036). We code all six as claimed without sufficient disclosed support. The four other ARTEX documents we read (FBI, Microsoft, Censys, StepSecurity) leave every link unknown, and none contradicts it (derived count of this record's codings: 6 claimed, 24 unknown, 0 supported, 0 contradicted over those five documents; scripts/r1_tables_output.txt). Three things bear on the loop without settling it: Censys's scans found LLM gateway software on 43 of the 1,465 hosts (C076), which fits the loop but is no evidence that stolen keys were loaded; CrowdStrike's finding, relayed by three of our sources, that one actor bought model access through a reseller (C009, C077, C078, C111), which is purchased inference in one campaign; and Amazon's and SANS's decoys receiving agents' operational prompts (C021, C091). SANS's September account of a different operation, not tied to ARTEX, describes the same shape of loop in more detail: the honeypot directly captured collected API keys, and the rest rests on captured agent-side records, with SANS saying a human still steered it (C093, C097 to C102). It shows the mechanism has been attempted elsewhere, not that ARTEX operations ran it.

Derived counts over the 42 codings: 1 supported, 11 claimed, 0 contradicted, 30 unknown; over the 36 ARTEX codings, 0 supported, 6 claimed, 30 unknown (scripts/r1_tables_output.txt). These count codings, not events, and say nothing about prevalence. Each coding's reason and claim ids are in [links.csv](links.csv).

**L1, acquiring credentials that give model access.** Amazon's sensors captured operators telling agents to hunt and grade AI credentials and a playbook mapping AI gateway and builder products to known attack chains (C029, C040 to C042). That supports intent. That keys were actually taken rests on agent records (C039; unverified, C023). SANS's honeypot received "collected API keys" inside the agent's own request, so possession is directly observed, though the means mix farming free trial accounts with abusing flaws and default credentials (C093, C094). StepSecurity relays Microsoft's 5 November report that agents searched GitHub for secrets alongside the names of cloud and AI providers (C116, C127), and GitGuardian's lab test in which ARTEX's model hunted planted secrets of its own accord (C113 to C115); neither says AI keys were obtained in the wild. The FBI and Microsoft describe credential theft in general, not AI keys (C008, C063).

**L2, showing the credentials work.** Amazon describes an instructed grading scheme, with L2 meaning "a confirmed working API call", but gives no count at any grade (C041). SANS's captured records show the agent's own test failing 341 of roughly 379 channels, and SANS warns that a working endpoint's model name is the reseller's claim, not a verified identity (C096, C098). The FBI and Unit 42 (via StepSecurity) describe testing cloud and application credentials, not AI keys (C008, C126).

**L3, loading them into an inference gateway.** Amazon's agent records are said to show stolen keys "fed into the framework's own model gateway" (C036). The other descriptions of ARTEX we hold say it has no model of its own and connects to endpoints its user configures (C083; FBI line 55; StepSecurity line 132), and none describes a built-in gateway or key pool, so what Amazon's phrase refers to is unresolved. Censys found gateway or reseller software (mainly CLIProxyAPI and New API) on 43 ARTEX hosts (C076); that is software presence, not evidence of whose keys it held. The FBI asks for reports of such software and presents none (C010). SANS's second capture describes a gateway being built in detail and SANS says it "watched" it come online (C097, C100), but the evidence is still the agent's side of things; SANS doesn't say it queried the gateway.

**L4, actually using the inference.** No source gives a token, spend or billing figure tied to acquired credentials. Two publishers' decoys received agents' operational prompts sent as inference requests to endpoints that weren't the operators' own (C021, C022, C091): direct telemetry of attempted use, though a decoy delivers no real inference and is not a stolen credential. Inference use by ARTEX operations is reported in other forms: DeepSeek Flash models dominating, partly for low cost (C003), one intrusion's roughly 1.4 billion tokens of model input with its source unstated (C112), and purchase through resellers (C009, C077, C078, C111).

**L5, that use enabling further hunting.** Only interpretation. Amazon says stolen keys "paid for the LLM inference powering further credential hunting" (C025); SANS says the pool is made available "to support further operations" (C101). Neither describes evidence that separates hunting powered by stolen inference from hunting powered by inference obtained some other way.

**L6, more usable resources coming back.** Amazon's grading scheme names "spendable balance" and agents "went after" managed AI services (C038, C041); no count is given, and that later hunting found more usable keys is implied by "self-sustaining" rather than shown. By SANS's captured records, about 38 channels survived the agent's test (derived: roughly 379 less 341; SANS doesn't state it) and five model names answered (C099). The FBI lists compute launched and volumes created inside victims' clouds, which isn't tied to inference (C013).

### 3. Where the evidence comes from

Observation of what the documents cite, with the dependence it implies.

- **The ARTEX loop has one origin in this corpus: Amazon's MadPot sensors.** The FBI's FLASH, issued the same day, cites eleven reports and not Amazon's (C014); StepSecurity, a day earlier, doesn't mention it (C128); Censys and Microsoft are earlier. So there is neither repetition nor independent corroboration of it here. Coverage after 13 November (a 16 November article in The Register, whose address names an FBI and AWS warning, appeared among Q1's results) was not opened, so whether later articles only repeat Amazon is unknown.
- **SANS is an independent origin for the mechanism, not for ARTEX.** Its honeypot, its operator and its date (11 September, before Amazon's window) are separate. But it rests on the same kind of evidence as Amazon: an agent's context captured at a decoy that presents as an LLM endpoint. Independence of origin doesn't remove that shared weakness.
- **The ConnectWise figure has one origin repeated three times**: Microsoft's 21 October report, relayed by the FBI (C005), Censys (C086) and StepSecurity.
- **Reseller purchase has one origin repeated three times**: CrowdStrike's 7 October report, relayed by the FBI's indicator table (C009), Censys (C077, C078) and StepSecurity, which adds unnamed "others" (C111).
- **Model prevalence** in the FBI rests on Zscaler and Check Point (C003).
- **Console counts come from different instruments.** Censys counted its own scans (C070); the FBI's "over 1,600" cites Check Point, not Censys (C006); StepSecurity lists four teams' counts and says each used a different method and date (C118 to C121). The leads relaying Check Point give its figures as 2,557 systems hosting panels and 1,608 live, which would fit the FBI's figure, but that is a lead, not evidence, until Check Point is read.
- **The developer's 8 October takedown** is relayed by the FBI (notes 3 and 6), Censys and StepSecurity, all from the developer's notice or the reports that cite it.

### 5. Competing explanations and what would tell them apart

The brief names four. They can coexist: human direction and resource feedback can hold together, and different campaigns, or one pool, can mix stolen and bought inference.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

Acquired resources sustained further operations | Amazon's agent records and Amazon's reading (C025, C036); SANS's analogous captured records, not ARTEX (C097 to C101) | Victim or provider records that keys taken from named exposed AI applications were later used from attacker infrastructure, timed against further hunting; billing anomalies on those keys; a gateway's channel list traced to victims | Not in this corpus. Four things that are easy to run together are kept apart here, and none of them establishes the next. Task persistence is reported (shared memory inside an operation, C033). Resource replenishment is claimed, by Amazon, from agent records (C025). Increased targeting ("hundreds of targets", C034) is reported but not shown to follow from replenishment. Self-propagation and reproduction are claimed by no source; copies of the code spread through people (C007, C124), and SANS says its data show no self-replicating system (C102). A self-sustaining credential loop, if one were shown, wouldn't by itself show self-replication, independence from people or profit.

### Readings made after the sources were read

Three readings in this record were made after the documents were read, and each is labelled post hoc wherever it appears.

1. **Decoy selection (interpretation, post hoc).** A decoy that poses as an exposed LLM API is reached mainly by operations that draw inference from endpoints they find. So both documents that describe the loop (Amazon, SANS) see it from a vantage that selects for it. These documents can't test this. Round 2 can test it in part by setting them against sources whose vantage isn't a decoy LLM endpoint (Check Point's console databases, CrowdStrike's open directories).
2. **Amazon's decoys as evidence for L3 and L4 (interpretation, post hoc).** That ARTEX agents sent operational prompts to Amazon's decoys may mean endpoints they found were placed in their inference supply. Amazon doesn't say so. One of round 2's queries looks for Amazon stating how the decoys came to be used; if none is found, nothing in this pilot can test it.
3. **Censys's hedge (observation of the text, post hoc).** Censys first says the actor "likely bought" access through a reseller, then that the actor "bought" it (C077, C078). CrowdStrike's own report, a round 2 candidate, says which.

## Method

This record reads only what the baseline retained on 17 November 2026; it fetched nothing.

1. **Reading.** The brief, the commissioning note, the baseline checkpoint, the source register, panel.json, the fetch log, the six queries and the six retained texts, each read in full. The redacted raw bodies were checked for article text the extraction might have missed (none of substance; Microsoft's Figure 1 is an image of commands) and for publication and modification dates in page metadata, which the clocks table uses. Leads L-0064 and L-0091 and the two horizon scout notes behind them were read as context.
2. **Integrity (derived).** The SHA-256 of each of the six retained bodies matches the fetch log; Check Point has no body.
3. **Claims.** 98 consequential claims, each with a short exact quotation and its line range in the retained text, campaign, event window, publication date, retrieval time, basis, statement, value and unit in the publisher's own terms, outcome and limitation. The script checks every quotation against its lines (whitespace normalised) and that no quotation carries a withheld indicator. In the FBI's PDF, table columns interleave in the extracted text, so claims from its tables quote single cells.
4. **Basis (rule-bound coding).** Direct telemetry described by the publisher: the publisher says its own sensors, scans or observation recorded it. That includes what a decoy received in a request, while what that content says happened elsewhere is agent-reported. Agent-reported action or outcome: the agents' own records. Operator instruction or intent: instructions as captured. Publisher interpretation: the publisher's reading, and every relay or synthesis of someone else's report. Later independent corroboration: used by no claim, because none of the claims has any. A claim carries several bases where it needs them. Counts of basis labels are in the output (63 publisher interpretation, 27 direct telemetry, 14 agent-reported, 8 operator instruction; a claim may carry several).
5. **Link coding (rule-bound, with judgement).** Supported: the publisher's own telemetry, or evidence independent of the agents' and operators' own records, is described as showing the link happening. Claimed without sufficient disclosed support: the link is asserted, but rests on agent- or operator-side records, the publisher's inference, or reports not retained here. Contradicted: a retained source's evidence shows the link did not happen for the operation in question. Unknown: the source doesn't address the link for acquired AI credentials, or wasn't read. The same rule was applied to Amazon and to SANS, although SANS's account is more specific; that choice matters most for SANS's L3 and is flagged for review.
6. **Calculations.** Status counts and the few derived figures (Censys's 815, 44% and 10.6% recomputed from its own numbers; the 38 SANS channels; indicator date ranges) are in the script. Replay: `python3 -I studies/LH055/scripts/r1_tables.py > studies/LH055/scripts/r1_tables_output.txt`, which ends "all checks passed".

## Limits

- **Unreviewed.** No second agent has checked this record. The status codings, the basis labels and the three post hoc readings are this writer's.
- **A small, chosen corpus.** Selection is purposive and outcome-informed. Five documents were fixed by the brief, and two (SANS, StepSecurity) were added after the retrospective outcomes were known. Nothing here estimates prevalence, cost or cause.
- **Check Point unread.** It is the panel source most likely to bear on human involvement, hallucinated successes and where inference came from.
- **No primary behind a relay was read.** CrowdStrike (7 October and VAPOR PANDA), Microsoft (21 October and 5 November), Google's threat intelligence group, Zscaler, Unit 42, GitGuardian, BadByte, WorkOS, CERT-EU and CERT-In are known here only through the FBI, Censys, Microsoft's links or StepSecurity.
- **Versions.** Three pages carry modification times after their stated dates, the FBI's PDF on the day we read it, and StepSecurity's server reports one; our copies are as of 17 November, and earlier versions weren't seen.
- **Withheld indicators.** Addresses and domains are withheld from the retained copies by design and weren't checked; no listed host was visited.
- **Search coverage.** Six queries through a tool that returns titles and a model-written summary; unopened results weren't read, and coverage can't be measured.
- **Not claims in Lighthouse's voice.** Every report of harm or compromise here is the publisher's, with what it rests on; this record makes no claim that harm is happening now or that a named system is compromised.
- **Cost.** The baseline's collection overhead is in its checkpoint; this record's effort and model cost are unknown, not zero.
