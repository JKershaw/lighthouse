## What Check Point's report says (observation, attributed; unreviewed)

Relevant to the record's open links, as Check Point reports them from ten exposed console databases and a scan of exposed panels:

- Scale in its own units: "2,557 systems hosting or having hosted ARTEX panels, with 1,608 live and publicly available instances at the time of the research"; key points say "more than 1,800 publicly exposed instances" (the two figures are not reconciled on the page).
- Models: operators configured mostly DeepSeek Flash models; guardrails were hit only with Anthropic's Opus 5.5 on one instance, after which the actor switched models. One campaign ran "on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash behind a third-party relay". Costs are Check Point's estimates from token counts at official DeepSeek prices (for example "between $700 and $1500" for 36 billion tokens); the page does not say who paid or with what credentials. The words "key" and "stolen" do not occur in the extracted text in connection with AI access; the page describes no stolen AI credentials feeding operations.
- False success: in the crypto campaign, "some agents claimed to have found vulnerabilities that allow USDT cashouts", but "the attacker's wallet shows a balance of $0 and no recorded transactions"; "not one cent moved". This is the case leads L-0091 and Equixly (S11) relayed, now read at source.
- Credentials of other kinds were acquired and validated: about 300 signing certificates with three passing the agents' checks; about 190,000 game-account credentials with "roughly 3,3%" still valid by the agents' sampling.
- Human involvement: operators set goals and "barely interacted"; two campaigns had no prompts beyond the initial goals.
- Dates: the page says the developer made ARTEX closed-source on "October 8th" and, later, refers to "the deletion of the original ARTEX project on September 8"; the inconsistency is the page's, not resolved here.
