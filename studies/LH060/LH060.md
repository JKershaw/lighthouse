# LH060: What do published studies that measure deployment of available security fixes or features by operator size or capacity find, and on what data?

**In short.** We asked what published research has actually measured about whether bigger or smaller operators deploy available security fixes or features more, less or faster. Six fixed searches and fifteen fetches found three documents that meet the brief's rule, and two of those got in on judgement calls the evidence review has to check. What they report doesn't line up: one company's own scan data shows no difference by revenue or staff for one new SSH feature, more uptake by one size measure and a thinner share by another; a small survey finds no association it could detect; and one study of rescans finds remediation differed by type of network. This record is unreviewed. It waits for the evidence review, the reader review and a recheck before anything reaches a page.

## Answer

In six searches and fifteen documents (the brief's stop), three documents were included and they report six claims about deployment broken down by size, capacity or type (observation, from the register). On size, the directions disagree: no difference (two claims, one measured from a vendor's scans, one self-reported with a test of little power), larger more (one, vendor) and larger less (one, vendor, on a different measure of the same organisations). On type, both claims report a difference. None rests on public data as far as the documents state. This is what this read found; it is not a picture of the literature, says nothing about whether size causes any difference, and sits exactly at the brief's minimum of three inclusions, two of them on judgement calls flagged for review (interpretation).

The structure check found one field in LH033's retained Censys data that could one day stand in for operator size, a per-network list, and none in LH039's files; no value was read, and proposal P-0050 carries it to a gate (observation, from structure.md).

### What each document reports

**C07, Haque and others (authors employed by Bitsight, whose scans are the data).** The outcome is whether an organisation offers a post-quantum key exchange on at least one SSH server, and the share of its addresses that do. The authors report 45.2 per cent of hosts and 39.9 per cent of entities (10,788 of 27,012) offering one. By revenue and staff they write that "Both small-scale organizations and multi-billion dollar enterprises show similar median distributions", read from box plots with no test stated. By SSL active count, "entities with more mature web presences (i.e., higher SSL active counts) show a slightly higher propensity to initiate PQC deployment", with no test stated. By IPv4 count, "adoption density thins as network size increases"; the authors call this a "complexity penalty", an explanation they assert rather than measure. By sector, adoption is "driven by research-oriented and infrastructure-heavy sectors like 'Education' and 'Telecommunications'". The firmographic data come "from our service providers", and the data are not stated as public. The authors suggest many entities are "unintentionally PQC-ready" through an SSH upgrade with the algorithm on by default, so the outcome may not be a decision at all (interpretation). The four claims are one origin.

**C08, Deng and Inthiran.** Respondents reported their organisation's security model: perimeter, zero trust or hybrid. The authors find "no statistically significant association (χ² = 4.27, df = 6, p = .64)" between model and organisation size. Descriptively, the zero trust shares fall with size: 0.500 (under 50, n = 10), 0.417 (50 to 250, n = 24), 0.250 (250 to 500, n = 12), 0.231 (over 500, n = 13). The cells are small, so the test has little power, and a test that finds nothing has not found an absence (derived reading of the authors' table). The PDF names the Pacific-Asia Conference on Information Systems 2026, which is not a listed venue; the document entered as an arXiv preprint.

**C10, Nosyk and others (academic; one author at RIPE NCC).** Remediation on rescans after notification through CSIRTs, as Kaplan-Meier survival. The authors write: "Military CSIRTs are particularly fast and efficient in remediation, contrary to research and educational entities that exhibit the slowest and worst patching rates", with a Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test, p = 0.00076 (their figure 5). The derived file also records that notified CSIRTs' networks remediated 20.4 per cent of nameservers and 14.24 per cent of domains in Phase 1, similar to those not notified, with an average per-CSIRT rate of 26.2 per cent. The numbers of nameservers per type were not checked.

## Method

The brief fixed six queries, run once each and verbatim on 25 November 2026 between 14:14:50Z and 14:15:42Z; none failed or was reworded (searches.md). They showed 73 results, many duplicates. Screening on metadata, plus the known documents the brief names, gave 15 candidates: 10 from results and 5 known documents no query returned (C01, C03, C04, C11, C14). Each got one gated one-off GET, in the brief's order of tier, then year, then first appearance; the fetch script ran C12 before C13, which changed nothing with every candidate read (register.md). No retries, no robots refusals.

Inclusion was decided on the document read, against the brief's three criteria: a measured deployment or remediation outcome of an available fix or feature by operators; a breakdown by size, capacity or type; and a stated method. Claims were classed as the brief defines.

**Stop.** The brief stops "when the six searches are run and fifteen GETs are spent or the candidate list is exhausted, or at the end of 11 December, whichever comes first". The six searches were run and all fifteen GETs were spent (fetchlog.csv has fifteen rows, one per candidate), and the candidate list was exhausted as well, so the stop was met on 25 November 2026. No reading is due before 11 December under this protocol, and any further GET would exceed the brief's ceiling, which allows no extension without a keeper line.

## Limits

- Three inclusions describe this read, not the literature, and are no evidence that measurements by size are rare. All three are arXiv preprints by metadata, none stated as peer reviewed.
- No figure has yet been checked against the documents by a second reader.
- C07 is one origin, one scan day and one feature, on a company's own data, with no test stated. C08 is a convenience sample of 64. C10's remediation follows a notification, grouped by intermediary.
- Directions are sorted by Lighthouse from the authors' words; "larger more" and "larger less" in C07 use different size measures and different outcomes on the same organisations, and are not in conflict by necessity.
- Nothing here says how fast any operator or class of operator deploys fixes now, why, or whether size causes it.
- The structure check settles only that a field exists; whether it could measure operator size is unknown.
- The derived files carry a stock "body_removed" sentence citing robots or page reservation, while their "body_kept" field and fetchlog.csv give the actual reason, no redistribution licence. The reviewer may want that wording corrected; it changes no figure.
