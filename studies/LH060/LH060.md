# LH060: What do published studies that measure deployment of available security fixes or features by operator size or capacity find, and on what data?

**In short.** We asked what published research has actually measured about whether bigger or smaller operators deploy available security fixes or features more, less or faster. Six fixed searches and fifteen fetches found two documents that meet the brief's rule, fewer than the three it needs to describe the literature, so on this read the question stays open. The only breakdown by size comes from a paper by a security-ratings company's staff, on their own scans from one day: they report no difference by revenue or staff for one new SSH feature, slightly more organisations starting with it among those with bigger web footprints, and, read from a scatter with no test, fewer large networks with it on every address; the other, a study of rescans after notifications, reports that remediation differed by the type of incident-response team notified. This version was revised after the evidence review and is unreviewed beyond it: a fresh agent reads what the revision wrote, then the reader review and a recheck come before anything reaches a page.

## Answer

In six searches and fifteen fetches (twelve documents read in full; the brief's stop), two documents were included, below the brief's minimum of three. Under the brief's first inconclusive condition, the six searches found too little to describe the literature, and the question stays open on this read (observation, from the register and R-0150). The two report five claims, all measured, four of them from one vendor's own scans of one day. **Every claim by size comes from that one vendor paper on one scan day:** no difference by revenue or staff, read from box plots (C07-a); more organisations starting deployment with more SSL evidence and, in the paper's key findings, more IPv4 addresses (C07-b); and fewer organisations with full coverage as IPv4 count rises, read from a scatter's density with no rate or test (C07-c). By type, both report a difference: C07-d by sector, and C10-a by the type of incident-response team notified, which its authors read as the sector the team serves. None rests on public data as far as the documents state. A survey of the security model organisations use (C08), included in version 0.1, was excluded at the evidence review, because a self-applied label for an architecture isn't the deployment of a published fix or feature as the brief defines one. This is what this read found. It isn't a picture of the literature, and says nothing about whether size causes any difference (interpretation).

The structure check found one field in LH033's retained Censys data that could one day stand in for operator size, a per-network list, and none in LH039's files; no value was read, and proposal P-0050 carries it to a gate (observation, from structure.md; R-0150 reproduced the check byte for byte).

### What each document reports

**C07, Haque and others (authors employed by Bitsight, whose scans are the data).** The outcome is whether an organisation offers a post-quantum key exchange on at least one SSH server, and the share of its addresses that do. The authors report 45.2 per cent of hosts and 39.9 per cent of entities (10,788 of 27,012) offering one (both reproduced in R-0150). By revenue and staff they write that "Both small-scale organizations and multi-billion dollar enterprises show similar median distributions", read from box plots with no test stated. By SSL active count, "entities with more mature web presences (i.e., higher SSL active counts) show a slightly higher propensity to initiate PQC deployment", with no test stated. By IPv4 count, the paper says two things (quotations from R-0150's reread). Section IV.C says adoption is "widespread across organizational sizes when measured by digital size metrics (SSL active and IPv4 counts)" and that "As the number of IPv4 addresses increases, the density of entities achieving 100% PQC support thins out", read from a scatter of the share of each entity's addresses against IPv4 count, with no rate or test; fewer points at the top of such a scatter can mean fewer large entities overall, not a lower share among them (R-0150's reading, interpretation). The key findings say "While higher SSL and IPv4 counts correlate with PQC support, adoption density thins as network size increases, reflecting the complexity of migrating legacy infrastructure." So the two sections differ on whether IPv4 count goes with starting deployment; the key findings say it does, as SSL active count does. The authors call the thinning a "complexity penalty", an explanation they assert rather than measure. By sector, adoption is "driven by research-oriented and infrastructure-heavy sectors like 'Education' and 'Telecommunications'". The firmographic data come "from our service providers", and the paper doesn't say how many of the 27,012 entities have revenue, employee, SSL or IPv4 data (region is present for 15,820, so coverage can't be assumed; R-0150). SSL active count is "the number of unique SSL evidence keys ... graded over the past 60 days", so that measure has a 60-day window, not the scan day (R-0150). The data are not stated as public. The authors suggest many entities are "unintentionally PQC-ready" through an SSH upgrade with the algorithm on by default, so the outcome may not be a decision at all (interpretation). The four claims are one origin.

**C10, Nosyk and others (academic; one author at RIPE NCC).** Remediation on rescans after notification through CSIRTs, as Kaplan-Meier survival. The authors write (as R-0150 quotes): "There exist different types of CSIRTs, e.g., governmental, national, military, research and education, non-commercial, and critical information infrastructure (CIIP). We wonder if the sector in which a CSIRT operates impacts the remediation rate." Their figure 5 is titled "Server remediation rates of different CSIRT constituency types". They report: "Military CSIRTs are particularly fast and efficient in remediation, contrary to research and educational entities that exhibit the slowest and worst patching rates", with a Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test, p = 0.00076 (their figure 5). The derived file also records that notified CSIRTs' networks remediated 20.4 per cent of nameservers and 14.24 per cent of domains in Phase 1, similar to those not notified, with an average per-CSIRT rate of 26.2 per cent. The paper doesn't give the number of CSIRTs or nameservers of each type, as far as R-0150 found. "National" is a jurisdiction rather than a sector, and since remediation follows notification through a CSIRT, a difference by type may reflect the CSIRTs' handling of the notice as much as the operators' (interpretation). Five of the six types are sectors of the networks served, which meets the brief's "type (for example ... sector, ownership)", so C10 stays included (R-0150's ruling, which this revision accepts).

## Method

The brief fixed six queries, run once each and verbatim on 25 November 2026 between 14:14:50Z and 14:15:42Z; none failed or was reworded (searches.md). They showed 73 results, many duplicates. Screening on metadata, plus the known documents the brief names, gave 15 candidates: 10 from results and 5 known documents no query returned (C01, C03, C04, C11, C14). Each got one gated one-off GET, in the brief's order of tier, then year, then first appearance; the fetch script ran C12 before C13, which changed nothing with every candidate read (register.md). No retries, no robots refusals.

Two results in listed venues showed no title or snippet and so weren't candidates under the screening rule: Q1 #8 (an ACM DOI, 10.1145/3613904.3642456, possibly CHI 2024, unverified) and Q1 #12 (an IEEE S&P 2015 PDF). Had both entered, there would have been 17 candidates for 15 GETs; both would have been read first as tier 1, leaving C14 and C15 unread (both excluded anyway). What the two documents say is unknown (R-0150, N6).

Inclusion was decided on the document read, against the brief's three criteria: a measured deployment or remediation outcome of an available fix or feature by operators; a breakdown by size, capacity or type; and a stated method. Claims were classed as the brief defines.

**Stop.** The brief stops "when the six searches are run and fifteen GETs are spent or the candidate list is exhausted, or at the end of 11 December, whichever comes first". The six searches were run and all fifteen GETs were spent (fetchlog.csv has fifteen rows, one per candidate), and the candidate list was exhausted as well, so the stop was met on 25 November 2026. No reading is due before 11 December under this protocol, and any further GET would exceed the brief's ceiling, which allows no extension without a keeper line.

### Judgement calls, as resolved at the evidence review

1. **C08:** whether a reported "security model in use" (zero trust against perimeter) is the deployment of an available security feature. R-0150 ruled it isn't: the brief defines an available fix or feature as "a patch, fixed release or configuration that was published and obtainable at the time measured (a software update, a fixed version, or a security feature such as a protocol or setting the operator could enable)", and C08's labels were "harmonized to a consistent taxonomy" by the authors without saying what a respondent had to have deployed. The reviser checked the ruling against the brief's text and agrees. C08 is excluded, inclusions fall to two, and the brief's first inconclusive condition applies.
2. **C10:** whether CSIRT constituency type is a type of operator. R-0150 ruled it is, as the sector of the constituency, with the caveats in C10's paragraph. Kept.
3. **C02 and C15,** excluded on criterion 2: upheld by R-0150 on rereads.
4. **The known NDSS 2018 study (C01):** unresolved; see its row. R-0150 notes, from its own prior knowledge and not read, that the NDSS 2018 notification paper may have Stock, Pellegrino, Li, Backes and Rossow as authors, in which case "Stock and others" and "Li and others" would be the same paper. No further GET is allowed under the brief.

## Limits

- Two inclusions describe this read, not the literature; the brief's minimum of three was not met, and they are no evidence that measurements by size are rare. Both are arXiv preprints by metadata, neither stated as peer reviewed.
- Figures for C07, C08 and C10 were checked against rereads of the same bytes by the evidence review (R-0150); the reviser of 0.2 made no network read, so the quotations added in 0.2 rest on R-0150's reading alone.
- Every size claim (C07-a to c) is one origin, one scan day and one feature, on a company's own data, with no test stated, and with coverage of the size data among the 27,012 entities not stated. C10's remediation follows a notification, grouped by intermediary, with numbers per type not stated.
- Directions are sorted by Lighthouse from the authors' words; "larger more" and "larger less" in C07 use different size measures and different outcomes on the same organisations, are not in conflict by necessity, and C07's sections differ on IPv4 count.
- Nothing here says how fast any operator or class of operator deploys fixes now, why, or whether size causes it.
- The structure check settles only that a field exists; whether it could measure operator size is unknown.
- The derived files carry scripts/lh_fetch.py's stock "body_removed" sentence citing robots or page reservation, while their "body_kept" field and fetchlog.csv give the actual reason, no redistribution licence found. register.md now says so; the derived files are not rewritten, and the fix belongs in `write_derived` (R-0150, N8). It changes no figure.
