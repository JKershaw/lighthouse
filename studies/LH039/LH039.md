# After WordPress fixed CVE-2026-119977 on 28 October 2026, how fast did the public views of WordPress's installed base move to the fixed releases?

**In short.** We asked how far the websites and the builds that run WordPress had moved to its fix of 28 October 2026 for a flaw in its own updater. W3Techs's survey of websites is a lagged view, visiting each site about once a month by our reading of its documentation: its share of the newest branch, 7.1, on the fixed release climbed steadily, from about 20% twelve days after the fix to 38% at day 24 (43% counting a further security release of 18 November), and to just under a quarter on 7.0 and 6.9, but a steady climb is what those visits would show even if every site that updated had done so at once, so it doesn't tell us how fast sites moved. Composer downloads, a different thing counted differently (fetches by developers and build systems), went to the fix within days on 7.1 and much more slowly on the older branches. Beside PHP's fix in the same survey at about the same ages, WordPress 7.1 stayed below PHP's newest branch while passing its older ones, and the older WordPress branches were below all of PHP's (a branch-by-branch reading we chose after seeing the figures); that comparison only describes, it can't say whether automatic updates help, and none of this is released yet.

## Answer

Unreviewed. Day 0 is 28 October 2026 (E0, "WordPress 7.1.4" in WordPress's release feed, 17:13:02Z). "Fixed" is 7.1.4, 7.0.8 or 6.9.11, or any later release on that branch (brief, Resources and stop).

1. **W3Techs, fixed share within each branch (derived measurement, measure 1).** These are lagged views of the sites, not a record of when they updated: W3Techs visits each site about once a month (LH033's reading), so a share that climbs steadily for weeks is what its visits would produce even if sites had updated at once, and a slower spread of updates would look much the same. Two observations, both post hoc, suggest the lag matters (Findings 7): the 7.1.4 series is close to a straight line that reaches zero at about day 1 (on 7.0 and 6.9 the lines reach zero nearer day 6.5, which fits that picture less well), and releases the fix had replaced, 7.0.7 and 6.9.10, kept gaining share in November. The day before the last checkpoint, 20 November, isn't in Packagist's history as read, and 18 and 19 November are lower bounds because the releases of 18 November weren't read, so whether the fixed releases took half on those days is unknown.
4. **Supply (observation, measure 5).** WordPress published a further security release on all three watched branches on 18 November 2026: "WordPress 7.1.5 Release" in its release feed at 17:08:59 UTC, with 7.0.9 and 6.9.12 dated 18 November on its release archive (day 21; E3). 7. **Evidence that the survey's lag shapes the curve (post hoc, chosen after the reads were seen; record-1.2-output.txt, Parts G1 and G2).** A straight line through the E0 release's own share of its branch, as W3Techs gives it, at the ten reads before the second release (days 12 to 21): 7.1.4 rises 1.83 points a day, the largest residual is 0.44 points, and the line reaches zero at about day 1.2; at day 4 it gives 5.0%, against the 6.6% on W3Techs's 1 November history point. A survey that revisits each site about once a month would draw a line like that, through about day 0, if every site that updated had done so on day 0 or 1 (interpretation). The same fit for 7.0.8 and 6.9.11 reaches zero at about day 6.7 and 6.4 (largest residuals 0.47 and 0.73), which fits that picture less well, so the line alone doesn't decide it. The history pages add a second sign: releases the fix replaced kept gaining share in November, 7.0.7 from 20.0% to 28.4% and 6.9.10 from 23.8% to 28.4% of their branches between 1 and 21 November, both first listed on 1 November. **The one-off read (after collection stopped).** The daily collector never read WordPress's release feed: it isn't in collect.py's list, and the feed was read on 7 November, before the brief, and not again until this read. With api.wordpress.org's stable-check and version-check refused from 9 November, nothing read during collection could have caught E3. The decision to make this read was taken on 22 November, after every outcome had been seen. That afternoon, scripts/oneoff_release_dates.py read WordPress's release feed (https://wordpress.org/news/category/releases/feed/, the brief's named instrument for E0 and E3, 15:14:54 UTC, HTTP 200) and its release archive (https://wordpress.org/download/releases/, 15:14:57 UTC, HTTP 200), once each, through scripts/lh_fetch.py with access='oneoff'. wordpress.org's robots.txt allowed both, and keep_decision allowed both bodies to be kept (no training, archiving or `*` refusal at those paths; no reservation on the pages), so the feed's body is kept in data/oneoff-2026-11-22T1514Z/ with the read log and the facts used (feed.facts.json, archive.facts.json); the archive page's body (1.3 MB) was not committed, for size alone, since archive.facts.json holds the facts used and the read log its hash (the read log's "yes" for the archive is keep_decision's answer, not what the repository holds; the folder's README.md says so); email addresses are withheld from the kept body. The read was made for the release dates only, which are supply metadata, not an outcome; it changes no measured share, only which label the brief's rule counts as fixed. api.wordpress.org's stable-check and version-check weren't attempted: its robots.txt refuses `*`, which the one-off rule also obeys.

## Limits

- **W3Techs's pace is partly its own, perhaps mostly.** Each site is visited about once a month, by LH033's reading of W3Techs's documentation (not re-checked here), so a day's figure blends a month of visits, and a share that rose doesn't show when the sites updated. Findings 7 gives post hoc evidence that the lag matters; nothing here measures how much.
- **A within-branch share moves when sites join or leave the branch,** as well as when they update within it, so a release's share can rise or fall without any of its own sites changing release. On 7.0, for example, 7.0.4 went from 26.4% to 10.0% between 9 and 21 November while 7.0.7 went from 22.6% to 28.4% (record-1.2-output.txt, Part G4); what moved them isn't known.
- **What W3Techs's labels mean isn't known.** It listed a 7.1.5 before WordPress published one, and a 7.1.6 that WordPress hasn't published; how it detects a release isn't described on the pages read.
- **After 18 November two fixes are mixed.** From the read of 19 November, the fixed share counts a second security release whose post lists, among other fixes, an unauthenticated privilege escalation "that could lead to remote code execution" (release feed, read 22 November). Uptake from then on isn't uptake of the first fix alone.
- **Packagist** counts fetches by developers and build systems, weighing each by how often it fetches; it says nothing about sites. 20 November is missing, and 18 and 19 November are lower bounds.
- **Measure 2** sets two kinds of software side by side in one survey with overlapping populations, from PHP reads two days off the matching day, and from LH033's interim, unreviewed record. Whether W3Techs detects a WordPress release the way it detects a PHP version isn't known (both studies list its detection as unknown), and the branches play different roles in the two programs, so the comparison can't test whether automatic updates help. A difference in timing is a description.
- **Missing, not negative:** api.wordpress.org's branch shares, version-check and stable-check at all 13 reads from 9 November; the brief's E3 rests on the release feed alone, read after the stop. W3Techs's 1 December monthly point falls after the window.
- **Not checked:** whether any update completed, or any site was exposed or attacked during an update (by the CVE record the update itself is the exposure window); what WordPress.org's download counters count; whether the robots.txt of W3Techs, Packagist and wordpress.org allowed the reads before 9 November (for the rules-of-the-road re-audit, row LH047-reread); why 7.0's counter rose before the second release; how many sites skipped 7.1.4.
