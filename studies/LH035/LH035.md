## Answer

By what was found, no layer of the ten is unobserved: each has at least one public instrument whose own pages were read on 2 November 2026 and whose data can, by those pages, be read without payment (observation). The survey has 40 included rows. Counted by the layer each sits on first, they fall 4 on physical and interconnection, 4 on routing, 2 on addresses and reachability, 1 on names, 1 on certificates, 2 on running services, 5 on web content, 8 on software supply, 10 on vulnerability and exploitation records and 3 on traffic; counted on every layer they touch, the thinnest layers are names, certificates and running services, with 3 rows each. Twelve further candidates have no row: 8 whose pages did not establish free access and 4 whose pages could not be read. Six candidate entries were excluded with reasons, one of them covering four of LH003's AI-subject sources.

What no included instrument observes is narrower than a layer, and the instruments' own pages and the papers read say where (observations, each attributed in Findings). On routing, the public collectors peer with a small minority of networks: a 2024 preprint counts vantage points in 1.3 per cent of 74,000 networks. On addresses, no included instrument's pages state an IPv6-wide view, and Censys's own 2025 paper says it does not scan IPv6 comprehensively. On names, the included instruments rank popularity; beyond what certificate logs and crawls reveal, the record of which names exist, the zone files, sits behind accounts or requests whose terms the pages read did not give. On running services, the fullest scanning data are paid or restricted, and a version is what a service reports. On traffic, each view is one company's network, one academic link in Japan, the dark space of one telescope, sensor fleets, or four US cable operators' interconnects in aggregate to 2024; none read publishes traffic across networks generally. On software supply, each registry counts a "download" its own way, and none of their pages says who fetched. On exploitation, the evidence behind the scores is private: FIRST's page says its partners' telemetry "cannot be released".

Many rows are not independent views. By their own pages, 8 of the 40 draw on another included instrument: CAIDA's AS relationships and Cloudflare Radar's routing views on RouteViews and RIPE RIS; Tranco on CrUX and Radar; HTTP Archive and W3Techs on CrUX (W3Techs also on Tranco); pypistats on PyPI's BigQuery table; GitHub's unreviewed advisories on NVD; OSV on GitHub's advisories (observation). Forty rows are therefore fewer vantage points than forty. This is a first sketch of a map from documentation, not a census: it measures nothing of the internet itself, and every count here counts instruments, not what they see.

## Findings

**Every layer has at least one included instrument; the thinnest are names, certificates and running services.** Any-layer counts (sources.md, Counts by layer): physical and interconnection 4, routing 6, addresses and reachability 5, names 3, certificates 3, running services 3, web content 5, software supply 9, vulnerability and exploitation 12, traffic 7 (observation, from the rows). The three on names are Cloudflare Radar's rankings, Tranco and CrUX, all measures of popularity; the three on certificates are Certificate Transparency logs, Censys and Radar's CT view, and Radar's comes "from the CT logs currently monitored by Cloudflare". The three on running services are Censys, the Shadowserver Foundation and W3Techs, the last measuring websites rather than hosts. A count of rows says how many instruments were found, not how much of a layer they see.

**Physical and interconnection: registers and estimates, not observation of what flows.** PeeringDB's records are self-reported ("can be a carrier, a facility, an exchange, or a network"); TeleGeography's map gathers cable systems from operators and says "The cable routes on our map are stylized and do not reflect the precise geolocation of systems", with capacity "only available as part of our subscription-based research services"; Epoch AI's data-centre dataset covers 96 AI data centres and estimates its own coverage of deployed AI compute at 45 per cent (90 per cent interval 30 to 78); the Interconnection Measurement Project reports aggregate utilisation of four US cable ISPs' interconnects for January 2018 to December 2024 (observations, read 2 November 2026). Land routes are not covered by any included row: the one candidate for them, Internet Atlas, did not resolve from this container, and CAIDA's AS Facilities dataset, measured in March to April 2017, is behind a request form (sources.md, Candidates without a row). A blog post on a 2026 study of 472 exchanges' public traffic graphs says "Public IXP data does not include private interconnections" (observation; the study is excluded as one-off).

**Routing: the public archives are long and open, and see from few networks.** RouteViews holds data "from the 1990s to today" under CC BY 4.0; RIPE RIS says "Currently dumps are created every 8 hours, and updates are created every 5 minutes"; CAIDA's monthly AS relationships run from 1998 (observations). Neither archive's pages read state a coverage limit. The literature does: Alfroy et al. (L06, preprint, 2024) count vantage points in "1.3%" of 74,000 networks and 8.4 per cent of transit networks, and report a private collector seeing 192,000 links that no RIS or RouteViews point saw; Sermpezis et al. (L05, preprint, 2023) find RIS "heavily biased towards larger networks and IXPs" (bias score 0.16 against 0.00 to 0.01 for a random sample) and RIPE Atlas much less so (0.06). These are those papers' measurements, cited, not reproduced here.

**Addresses and reachability: IPv4 views, with IPv6 stated by none of the included pages.** CAIDA's Ark topology dataset is IPv4 by its name and says "a random address in each prefix is probed approximately every 24 hours", since 13 September 2007, its latest year restricted to academic and US government users; RIPE Atlas has "over 14,000" volunteer probes and says "The vast majority of data... is freely available"; the UCSD telescope watches "approximately 1/256th of all IPv4 addresses" (observations). Censys's documentation names "public IPv4 and IPv6 hosts", but its own SIGCOMM 2025 paper (L01, by Censys staff) says "we do not conduct comprehensive IPv6 scans" and estimates it sees 98 per cent of IPv4 services on the top 10 ports and 62 per cent across all 65K ports (observation of the paper's claim). USC/ISI's address censuses, which "ping every allocated IP address one time over about two months", are behind a request page (undetermined).

**Names: the included instruments rank popularity; which names exist is behind accounts.** Tranco averages provider lists "over the past 30 days"; CrUX includes a page only above a threshold that "is not disclosed"; Radar ranks by "estimated number of unique users" (observations). ICANN's CZDS needs an account and covers "participating generic Top-Level Domains"; DNS Coffee's API is "private, authenticated" and on request; neither page states the cost (undetermined). Sommese et al. (L07, preprint, 2023) found public sources, CT logs and Common Crawl, covering 43 to 80 per cent of 19 country-code zones in 2023. Le Pochat et al. (L03, NDSS 2019) found the four lists then behind Tranco agreed on about 70,000 of 2.82 million sites and could all be manipulated.

**Certificates: one public record, whose page states no limit.** The CT project's page says logs are "append-only ledgers of certificates", mostly "submitted by CAs", queryable by "anyone", with a merge delay "usually 24 hours"; it states no limit (observation). The papers read give partial views of coverage: CT logs held 52 per cent of the names in those 19 zones in 2023 (L07, names rather than certificates), and to December 2015 almost every domain seen on one campus had a valid certificate in some log (L11). A third paper on coverage (L04) returned HTTP 503 and was not read.

**Running services: the open view is thin, and a version is what a service says.** Censys's free account gives "basic visibility into standard ports and services", with history from "1+ month" on paid plans; Rapid7's Sonar data are "granted on a commercial basis to qualified organizations" (excluded); Shodan's pricing page returned 403 and Netlas's policy page gives no terms (undetermined); Shadowserver gives its reports free to "vetted subscribers" and its public dashboard returned 503 twice (observations). The Censys tracker Lighthouse used counts "PHP responses Censys scanners receive per minute, by the version they report"; its "Notes & limitations" heading had no text under it. Censys's paper says it restricts "data that is ripe for abuse" (L01).

**Web content and technology: landing pages, from a few places.** HTTP Archive tests one page per site, "does not crawl the website's other pages", from "various datacenters in the US"; Common Crawl takes "a randomly selected subset" of a site and "JavaScript is not executed"; W3Techs investigates "technologies of websites, not of individual web pages" and calls its results possibly "incomplete and inaccurate" (observations). Aqeel et al. (L08, IMC 2020) found internal pages of 1,000 sites differ substantially from landing pages, and that nearly two thirds of 119 web-performance studies would need revising to apply to them.

**Software supply: each registry counts a download its own way, and none says who fetched.** npm's page leaves a download undefined and keeps per-version counts for "the previous 7 days"; Packagist counts "installs" without defining one; nodejs.org reports downloads matched in its access logs with a gap from 1 to 21 September 2015; Hugging Face counts "Every HTTP request to these files, including GET and HEAD" (its counts' publication not established); Docker counts a multi-architecture pull "as one pull for each different architecture" (access not established) (observations). That none says who fetched is Lighthouse's reading of what the records hold (interpretation, as in LH003 and LH011 to LH013). Software Heritage's pages returned 503 or a bot-protection block three times.

**Vulnerability and exploitation records: the most instruments, with the evidence for exploitation private.** CISA's catalogue page showed "1766 results" on 2 November 2026, with CSV and JSON downloads, and requires "a clear remediation action" before listing; the CVE List updates "about every 7 minutes"; OSV keeps sources "no more than 15 minutes stale, 99.5% of the time"; EPSS publishes a daily probability for every CVE from partners' sensors and says "the training data cannot be released"; Exploit-DB holds "exploits and proof-of-concepts rather than advisories"; the Atlantic Council's dataset was last updated in 2023 (observations). Parla (L10, a single-author preprint on a purposive sample, 2024) found 42 of 57 catalogue entries from 2023 or later had low EPSS scores before listing, and calls the catalogue "a subset of all exploited CVEs". NVD's pages returned only their title, as in LH003.

**Traffic: views from one place each.** Cloudflare Radar's views come from "Cloudflare's global network" and its resolver; MAWI records "the transit link of WIDE to the upstream ISP" since 1 July 2006, with data missing from 4 November to 15 December 2022; the UCSD telescope sees unsolicited traffic only; DShield and GreyNoise see what reaches volunteers' logs or "thousands of sensors"; M-Lab says "All data collected via M-Lab is made publicly available", from tests that measure paths rather than count traffic (placed on this layer as the nearest, a classification made here) (observations). Degen et al. (L09, 2026) found larger telescopes "detect many attacks missed by smaller ones".

**What the instruments say of themselves.** For 13 of the 40 rows, the pages read state no limit of the instrument's own; for 21 they give no history depth; for 27 no publication or update date (observation, from the fields left "not stated"). A missing statement is not an absence of limits. Of the 40, only Epoch AI's pages give a number for their own coverage (45 per cent of deployed AI compute); others state kinds of gap, such as MAWI's dated outages or EPSS's "does not assume we are seeing all of the exploitation activity".

**Lighthouse's reading of the blind spots (interpretation).** Each row's last-but-one column gives a characteristic blind spot, as the brief asks, labelled as Lighthouse's reading; one is blank (PCH, whose page was too thin to judge). Read together, after the rows were filled, the blind spots group by where an instrument stands: those that observe from inside a network or service see only what crosses it (Radar, MAWI, the registries' logs, the interconnect project); those that observe from outside by asking see only what answers or declares itself (scanners, Atlas, PeeringDB, W3Techs, TeleGeography); those that collect reports see only what someone reported (CVE, the catalogue, Exploit-DB, Breaking Trust, urlscan.io). This grouping is a post hoc reading made from 40 rows by the writer who wrote them, with no test; it bears on, but does not test, the reading proposed in essays/seen-from-outside-seen-from-inside.md (unreleased when the brief was fixed, released on 30 October 2026), that each instrument's blind spot sits on a characteristic side. Nothing here measures it; Next says what could.

**The draft diagram.** studies/LH035/figure.svg, drawn by scripts/diagram.py, places each included instrument on every layer it sits on, filled on its primary layer, and shows the twelve candidates without a row dashed. It is a draft that may seed a map page; it was looked at in light and dark.

## Method

Followed studies/LH035/brief.md (protocol 0.1) without amendment. Read AGENTS.md, the brief and studies/LH003 first.

Search plan: the twenty fixed searches, two per layer of the forms "<layer> internet measurement dataset" and "<layer> public data internet-wide", using the brief's layer names, were all run before any result was opened, and every result's title and URL was written to data/search_raw.md; the eight literature searches likewise to data/lit_raw.md before any paper was opened. searchlog.md marks each result opened or not. Most candidates from the plan were named in the search tool's model-written summaries rather than its result lists; for those, the instrument's own documentation was read directly, and the row says which search named it. A summary was never used as a source.

Candidates: (a) instruments Lighthouse's studies used; (b) LH003's rows touching a layer; (c) the search plan. For each, the instrument's own documentation was fetched with WebFetch, asking for the brief's fields and quotations, and filled into data/instruments.json; a field the pages did not fill says "not stated". Inclusion test, a decision made in this run before any table was built and applied to every candidate: a row is included when the pages read show data, link to data files, or describe the data as public, published, free or openly licensed; "undetermined" when they put data behind an account, request, credentials or subscription without saying any is free, or say nothing of its availability. Lighthouse's own earlier reads of an instrument (Docker Hub's tags, for example) were not counted as evidence of its terms. The cap of 45 was not reached (40 included), so the cap's ranking rule was not needed. Layers were assigned from what the pages say each records; an instrument on several layers has a primary one, chosen here; M-Lab's placement on traffic is the weakest of these. Lighthouse's reading of each blind spot was written after each row's fields.

Literature: eight searches, then eleven papers opened (twelve allowed), read at least to method and limits; one failed with HTTP 503. Five PDFs the fetch tool could not parse were saved by the tool, copied into their own directories and read with pdftotext, grep and sed; the passages used are quoted in data/literature.json. A paper's evaluation is cited beside the row it concerns.

Reads: 28 searches and 100 page fetches (87 for instruments, 13 for papers, counting redirects and failures; 75 returned readable content), against a ceiling of about 120 and twenty-eight (sources.md, Every fetch). No scan, account, paid service, bulk download or contact.

Replay (offline): `python3 studies/LH035/scripts/searchlog.py && python3 studies/LH035/scripts/build.py && python3 studies/LH035/scripts/diagram.py` regenerates searchlog.md, sources.md (and prints every count quoted here) and figure.svg from studies/LH035/data/.

## Limits

Every row rests on a model-written extract of a page, not the raw page: WebFetch summarises what it fetches, and the quotations in the table are the extract's quotations. LH003's review found an extract misquoting a page (notes/R-0001.md); no raw read was made here, and the brief's sample check of rows against their pages, by the driving session, has not happened at the time of writing.

Several instruments were read through one page, sometimes a thin one (PCH's directory listing, CZDS's sign-in page, GreyNoise's home page after two documentation 404s, M-Lab's two overview pages). Their blank fields may be filled by pages not read. Twenty-seven rows give no page date, so most rows say what a page said on the read date, not since when.

The inclusion test is this run's operational reading of "some of its data can be read without payment". It counts a linked data file or an open licence as free reading without trying the data, which this reading-only study did not do; and it leaves out instruments whose free access may be well known (Docker Hub, Hugging Face, Shodan) because the pages read did not say so. A different reading would move rows between the table and the undetermined list without changing which layers are covered.

The candidate list is not a census. It comes from three fixed places, and the search tool's results were often poor for the brief's fixed phrasings (search L2b returned mostly 1990s IETF documents; L4b mixed personal names with domain names). Instruments the writer knows of but which none of the three places produced were not read and have no row: from the writer's knowledge, not checked, among them IODA, crt.sh, OpenINTEL, the regional registries' delegation statistics, the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine and Internet Health Report. Twenty leads from the searches were not reached within the ceiling (sources.md, Leads not reached). "Unobserved by what was found" means just that.

The literature is eleven papers chosen by eight searches; four are preprints, one is by an instrument's own staff (L01), one is a single-author preprint on a purposive sample (L10), and one paper on CT coverage (L04) could not be read. Their figures are theirs, not reproduced.

The layer of each instrument, its primary layer, the independence count (8 of 40, from what pages say they draw on; others may draw on unstated sources, ClickPy's page for one names none) and the blind-spot column are Lighthouse's classifications. The grouping of blind spots by where an instrument stands is post hoc and untested.

The spend of this run is recorded by the driving session in its close-out, not here.
