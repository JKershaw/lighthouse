## Answer

By what was found, no layer of the ten is unobserved: each has at least one public instrument whose own pages were read on 2 or 5 November 2026 and whose data can, by those pages, be read without payment (observation). The survey has 48 included rows: the 40 of version 0.1 and eight added on 5 November, when the twelve candidates then without a row were read again, raw. Counted by the layer each sits on first, they fall 5 on physical and interconnection, 5 on routing, 2 on addresses and reachability, 2 on names, 1 on certificates, 3 on running services, 5 on web content, 11 on software supply, 11 on vulnerability and exploitation records and 3 on traffic; counted on every layer they touch, the thinnest layer is certificates, with 3 rows, then names and running services with 4 each. Four candidates still have no row: 3 whose pages did not establish free access (USC/ISI's datasets, ICANN's CZDS, Shodan) and 1 whose pages could not be read (Internet Atlas). Six candidate entries were excluded with reasons, one of them covering four of LH003's AI-subject sources. What the Answer says of where the instruments stop did not change with the eight new rows; two of its sentences gained a qualification, on names and on exploitation, given below.

What no included instrument observes is narrower than a layer, and the instruments' own pages and the papers read say where (observations, each attributed in Findings). On routing, the public collectors peer with a small minority of networks: a 2024 preprint counts vantage points in 1.3 per cent of 74,000 networks. On addresses, no included instrument's pages state an IPv6-wide view, and Censys's own 2025 paper says it does not scan IPv6 comprehensively. On names, the included instruments mostly rank popularity; one, DNS Coffee, shows which names its imported zone files hold, page by page, with bulk access through a paid or discretionary API; the zone files themselves, through ICANN's CZDS, sit behind an account and each registry's approval, at a cost the pages read do not state. On running services, the fullest scanning data are paid or restricted, and a version is what a service reports. On traffic, each view is one company's network, one academic link in Japan, the dark space of one telescope, sensor fleets, or four US cable operators' interconnects in aggregate to 2024; none read publishes traffic across networks generally. On software supply, each registry counts a "download" its own way, and none of their pages says who fetched. On exploitation, the evidence behind the scores is private: FIRST's page says its partners' telemetry "cannot be released". On vulnerability records, NIST says the NVD has had since early 2024 "a significant backlog of unenriched CVEs" that it has been "unable to clear".

Many rows are not independent views. By their own pages, 11 of the 48 draw on another included instrument: CAIDA's AS relationships and Cloudflare Radar's routing views on RouteViews and RIPE RIS; Tranco on CrUX and Radar; HTTP Archive and W3Techs on CrUX (W3Techs also on Tranco); pypistats on PyPI's BigQuery table; GitHub's unreviewed advisories on NVD; OSV on GitHub's advisories; CAIDA's AS Facilities on PeeringDB and CAIDA's AS relationships; RIPEstat on RIPE RIS and RIPE Atlas (by its endpoints' names); NVD on the CVE List (observation). Forty-eight rows are therefore fewer vantage points than forty-eight. This is a first sketch of a map from documentation, not a census: it measures nothing of the internet itself, and every count here counts instruments, not what they see.

## Findings

**Every layer has at least one included instrument; the thinnest are certificates, then names and running services.** Any-layer counts (sources.md, Counts by layer): physical and interconnection 5, routing 7, addresses and reachability 6, names 4, certificates 3, running services 4, web content 5, software supply 12, vulnerability and exploitation 13, traffic 7 (observation, from the rows; version 0.1's counts, on 40 rows, were 4, 6, 5, 3, 3, 3, 5, 9, 12 and 7). The four on names are Cloudflare Radar's rankings, Tranco and CrUX, all measures of popularity, and DNS Coffee, added on 5 November, which shows names from zone files; the three on certificates are Certificate Transparency logs, Censys and Radar's CT view, and Radar's comes "from the CT logs currently monitored by Cloudflare". The four on running services are Censys, the Shadowserver Foundation, W3Techs, the last measuring websites rather than hosts, and Netlas, added on 5 November on a free plan that needs an account. A count of rows says how many instruments were found, not how much of a layer they see.

**Physical and interconnection: registers and estimates, not observation of what flows.** PeeringDB's records are self-reported ("can be a carrier, a facility, an exchange, or a network"); TeleGeography's map gathers cable systems from operators and says "The cable routes on our map are stylized and do not reflect the precise geolocation of systems", with capacity "only available as part of our subscription-based research services"; Epoch AI's data-centre dataset covers 96 AI data centres and estimates its own coverage of deployed AI compute at 45 per cent (90 per cent interval 30 to 78); the Interconnection Measurement Project reports aggregate utilisation of four US cable ISPs' interconnects for January 2018 to December 2024 (observations, read 2 November 2026). Land routes are not covered by any included row: the one candidate for them, Internet Atlas, did not resolve from this container on 2 or 5 November (sources.md, Candidates without a row). CAIDA's AS Facilities dataset, version 0.1's other candidate here, is now a row (I41): its catalogue page links a public directory of data files under CAIDA's acceptable use agreement, but it is one snapshot, "derived using measurement data collected in March-April 2017", and inferred from traceroutes and PeeringDB's declared facilities (observations, raw read 5 November 2026). A blog post on a 2026 study of 472 exchanges' public traffic graphs says "Public IXP data does not include private interconnections" (observation; the study is excluded as one-off).

**Routing: the public archives are long and open, and see from few networks.** RouteViews holds data "from the 1990s to today" under CC BY 4.0; RIPE RIS says "Currently dumps are created every 8 hours, and updates are created every 5 minutes"; CAIDA's monthly AS relationships run from 1998; RIPEstat, added on 5 November (I48), calls its API "the public data interface for RIPEstat", an interface over RIS, Atlas and registry data rather than a further vantage point, with terms that forbid redistribution and commercial use without permission (observations). Neither archive's pages read state a coverage limit. The literature does: Alfroy et al. (L06, preprint, 2024) count vantage points in "1.3%" of 74,000 networks and 8.4 per cent of transit networks, and report a private collector seeing 192,000 links that no RIS or RouteViews point saw; Sermpezis et al. (L05, preprint, 2023) find RIS "heavily biased towards larger networks and IXPs" (bias score 0.16 against 0.00 to 0.01 for a random sample) and RIPE Atlas much less so (0.06). These are those papers' measurements, cited, not reproduced here.

**Addresses and reachability: IPv4 views, with IPv6 stated by none of the included pages.** CAIDA's Ark topology dataset is IPv4 by its name and says "a random address in each prefix is probed approximately every 24 hours", since 13 September 2007, its latest year restricted to academic and US government users; RIPE Atlas has "over 14,000" volunteer probes and says "The vast majority of data... is freely available"; the UCSD telescope watches "approximately 1/256th of all IPv4 addresses" (observations). Censys's documentation names "public IPv4 and IPv6 hosts", but its own SIGCOMM 2025 paper (L01, by Censys staff) says "we do not conduct comprehensive IPv6 scans" and estimates it sees 98 per cent of IPv4 services on the top 10 ports and 62 per cent across all 65K ports (observation of the paper's claim). USC/ISI's address censuses, which "ping every allocated IP address one time over about two months", are behind a request process asking for a research project, "e-mail sent from your institution" and a signed data use agreement, at a cost no page read states (undetermined, read raw again on 5 November).

**Names: the included instruments mostly rank popularity; which names exist is seen through one site's pages or behind accounts.** Tranco averages provider lists "over the past 30 days"; CrUX includes a page only above a threshold that "is not disclosed"; Radar ranks by "estimated number of unique users" (observations). DNS Coffee (I42, added on 5 November) shows, without an account, what the zone files it imports hold: its com page showed "168,656,928 Domains", first imported on 11 April 2011, and its FAQ says "Zone files are periodic snapshots, so a name that appears and disappears between two imports may not be recorded at all"; its API is "private, authenticated", its terms mention paid plans and discretionary academic access, and they forbid automated access to the website (observations, raw read 5 November 2026). ICANN's CZDS needs an account and each registry's approval ("approved requesters (e.g. law enforcement agents, IP attorneys, researchers)"), covers "participating generic Top-Level Domains", and its terms of 2013 state no fee either way (undetermined). Sommese et al. (L07, preprint, 2023) found public sources, CT logs and Common Crawl, covering 43 to 80 per cent of 19 country-code zones in 2023. Le Pochat et al. (L03, NDSS 2019) found the four lists then behind Tranco agreed on about 70,000 of 2.82 million sites and could all be manipulated.

**Certificates: one public record, whose page states no limit.** The CT project's page says logs are "append-only ledgers of certificates", mostly "submitted by CAs", queryable by "anyone", with a merge delay "usually 24 hours"; it states no limit (observation). The papers read give partial views of coverage: CT logs held 52 per cent of the names in those 19 zones in 2023 (L07, names rather than certificates), and to December 2015 almost every domain seen on one campus had a valid certificate in some log (L11). A third paper on coverage (L04) returned HTTP 503 and was not read.

**Running services: the open view is thin, and a version is what a service says.** Censys's free account gives "basic visibility into standard ports and services", with history from "1+ month" on paid plans; Rapid7's Sonar data are "granted on a commercial basis to qualified organizations" (excluded); Shodan's two pricing pages returned 403 behind a browser challenge on 2 and 5 November (undetermined); Netlas (I45, added on 5 November) offers "Community Free Forever", "50 requests / day" for "non-commercial use only" on an account; Shadowserver gives its reports free to "vetted subscribers" and its public dashboard returned 503 twice (observations). The Censys tracker Lighthouse used counts "PHP responses Censys scanners receive per minute, by the version they report"; its "Notes & limitations" heading had no text under it. Censys's paper says it restricts "data that is ripe for abuse" (L01).

**Web content and technology: landing pages, from a few places.** HTTP Archive tests one page per site, "does not crawl the website's other pages", from "various datacenters in the US"; Common Crawl takes "a randomly selected subset" of a site and "JavaScript is not executed"; W3Techs investigates "technologies of websites, not of individual web pages" and calls its results possibly "incomplete and inaccurate" (observations). Aqeel et al. (L08, IMC 2020) found internal pages of 1,000 sites differ substantially from landing pages, and that nearly two thirds of 119 web-performance studies would need revising to apply to them.

**Software supply: each registry counts a download its own way, and none says who fetched.** npm's page leaves a download undefined and keeps per-version counts for "the previous 7 days"; Packagist counts "installs" without defining one; nodejs.org reports downloads matched in its access logs with a gap from 1 to 21 September 2015; Hugging Face counts "Every HTTP request to these files, including GET and HEAD", and a model page shows "Downloads last month" without an account (I44); Docker counts a multi-architecture pull "as one pull for each different architecture", and its Hub API says "Public repositories are accessible to everyone", a cumulative pull count per repository (I43); Software Heritage's graph exports, January 2019 to June 2026, "are available publicly and with no login required" (I46), an archive of source code rather than of fetches (observations; Hugging Face, Docker and Software Heritage were settled by raw reads on 5 November 2026, the Docker pull definition resting on the 2 November extract). That none says who fetched is Lighthouse's reading of what the records hold (interpretation, as in LH003 and LH011 to LH013).

**Vulnerability and exploitation records: the most instruments, with the evidence for exploitation private.** CISA's catalogue page showed "1766 results" on 2 November 2026, with CSV and JSON downloads, and requires "a clear remediation action" before listing; the CVE List updates "about every 7 minutes"; OSV keeps sources "no more than 15 minutes stale, 99.5% of the time"; EPSS publishes a daily probability for every CVE from partners' sensors and says "the training data cannot be released"; Exploit-DB holds "exploits and proof-of-concepts rather than advisories"; the Atlantic Council's dataset was last updated in 2023 (observations). Parla (L10, a single-author preprint on a purposive sample, 2024) found 42 of 57 catalogue entries from 2023 or later had low EPSS scores before listing, and calls the catalogue "a subset of all exploited CVEs". NVD (I47, added on 5 November) was read through NIST's own page, which calls it "a reliable, sustainable and publicly available source of information about cybersecurity vulnerabilities" and says that since early 2024 it has had "a significant backlog of unenriched CVEs" it has been "unable to clear", with backlogged records published before 1 March 2026 moved to "Not Scheduled" (update of 15 April 2026; observation, raw read 5 November 2026); nvd.nist.gov itself returned only a script shell, as in LH003.

**Traffic: views from one place each.** Cloudflare Radar's views come from "Cloudflare's global network" and its resolver; MAWI records "the transit link of WIDE to the upstream ISP" since 1 July 2006, with data missing from 4 November to 15 December 2022; the UCSD telescope sees unsolicited traffic only; DShield and GreyNoise see what reaches volunteers' logs or "thousands of sensors"; M-Lab says "All data collected via M-Lab is made publicly available", from tests that measure paths rather than count traffic (placed on this layer as the nearest, a classification made here) (observations). Degen et al. (L09, 2026) found larger telescopes "detect many attacks missed by smaller ones".

**What the instruments say of themselves.** Of the 40 rows of version 0.1, the pages read state no limit of the instrument's own for 13, no history depth for 21 and no publication or update date for 26 (observation, from the fields left "not stated"; 27 before the raw re-read of 6 November found CrUX's date); of the eight added on 5 November, the pages read state no limit of the instrument's own for 4 (Docker Hub, Hugging Face, beyond its note on double counting, Netlas and Software Heritage, beyond its errata), no history depth for 5 and no page date for 4. A missing statement is not an absence of limits. Of the 48, only Epoch AI's pages give a number for their own coverage (45 per cent of deployed AI compute); others state kinds of gap, such as MAWI's dated outages or EPSS's "does not assume we are seeing all of the exploitation activity".

**Lighthouse's reading of the blind spots (interpretation).** Each row's last-but-one column gives a characteristic blind spot, as the brief asks, labelled as Lighthouse's reading; one is blank (PCH, whose page was too thin to judge). Read together, after the first 40 rows were filled, the blind spots group by where an instrument stands: those that observe from inside a network or service see only what crosses it (Radar, MAWI, the registries' logs, the interconnect project); those that observe from outside by asking see only what answers or declares itself (scanners, Atlas, PeeringDB, W3Techs, TeleGeography); those that collect reports see only what someone reported (CVE, the catalogue, Exploit-DB, Breaking Trust, urlscan.io). This grouping is a post hoc reading made from 40 rows by the writer who wrote them, with no test, and it was not revisited for the eight rows of 5 November, though the reading subagent notes that RIPEstat and DNS Coffee observe through other instruments' or registries' records; it bears on, but does not test, the reading proposed in essays/seen-from-outside-seen-from-inside.md (unreleased when the brief was fixed, released on 30 October 2026), that each instrument's blind spot sits on a characteristic side. Nothing here measures it; Next says what could.

**The draft diagram.** studies/LH035/figure.svg, drawn by scripts/diagram.py, places each included instrument on every layer it sits on, filled on its primary layer, and shows the four candidates still without a row dashed (twelve in version 0.1). It is a draft that may seed a map page; it was looked at in light and dark on 2 November and again, redrawn, on 5 November.

## Method

Followed studies/LH035/brief.md (protocol 0.1) without amendment. Read AGENTS.md, the brief and studies/LH003 first.

Search plan: the twenty fixed searches, two per layer of the forms "<layer> internet measurement dataset" and "<layer> public data internet-wide", using the brief's layer names, were all run before any result was opened, and every result's title and URL was written to data/search_raw.md; the eight literature searches likewise to data/lit_raw.md before any paper was opened. searchlog.md marks each result opened or not. Most candidates from the plan were named in the search tool's model-written summaries rather than its result lists; for those, the instrument's own documentation was read directly, and the row says which search named it. A summary was never used as a source.

Candidates: (a) instruments Lighthouse's studies used; (b) LH003's rows touching a layer; (c) the search plan. For each, the instrument's own documentation was fetched with WebFetch, asking for the brief's fields and quotations, and filled into data/instruments.json; a field the pages did not fill says "not stated". Inclusion test, a decision made in this run before any table was built and applied to every candidate: a row is included when the pages read show data, link to data files, or describe the data as public, published, free or openly licensed; "undetermined" when they put data behind an account, request, credentials or subscription without saying any is free, or say nothing of its availability. Lighthouse's own earlier reads of an instrument (Docker Hub's tags, for example) were not counted as evidence of its terms. The cap of 45 was not reached (40 included), so the cap's ranking rule was not needed. Layers were assigned from what the pages say each records; an instrument on several layers has a primary one, chosen here; M-Lab's placement on traffic is the weakest of these. Lighthouse's reading of each blind spot was written after each row's fields.

Literature: eight searches, then eleven papers opened (twelve allowed), read at least to method and limits; one failed with HTTP 503. Five PDFs the fetch tool could not parse were saved by the tool, copied into their own directories and read with pdftotext, grep and sed; the passages used are quoted in data/literature.json. A paper's evaluation is cited beside the row it concerns.

Reads, 2 November: 28 searches and 100 page fetches (87 for instruments, 13 for papers, counting redirects and failures; 75 returned readable content), against a ceiling of about 120 and twenty-eight (sources.md, Every fetch). No scan, account, paid service, bulk download or contact.

Replay (offline): `python3 studies/LH035/scripts/searchlog.py && python3 studies/LH035/scripts/build.py && python3 studies/LH035/scripts/diagram.py` regenerates searchlog.md, sources.md (and prints every count quoted here) and figure.svg from studies/LH035/data/.

## Limits

Rows I01 to I40 were written from model-written extracts of pages and checked against the raw pages on 6 November, four days later, not on the day they were written: a field a page changed in those four days and the check did not catch is the 2 November extract's. Row I30 (CISA's catalogue) rests on its extract alone, since its pages refused the research client. The quotation check is mechanical and exact; the field check was made by two Sonnet subagents reading plain-text dumps, with the driving session rechecking only what they flagged, so a claim they wrongly passed as supported was not rechecked. The blind-spot and literature columns were not part of the check. Rows I41 to I48 rest on raw reads of 5 November, except the two fields that rest on 2 November extracts.

Several instruments were read through one page, sometimes a thin one (PCH's directory listing, GreyNoise's home page after two documentation 404s, M-Lab's two overview pages; on 5 November, Software Heritage's dataset page and Netlas's pricing page). Their blank fields may be filled by pages not read. Thirty rows give no page date (26 of rows I01 to I40 and 4 of I41 to I48), so most rows say what a page said on the read date, not since when.

The inclusion test is this run's operational reading of "some of its data can be read without payment". It counts a linked data file or an open licence as free reading without trying the data, which this reading-only study did not do; and on 2 November it left out instruments whose free access may be well known (Docker Hub, Hugging Face, Shodan) because the pages then read did not say so. The reads of 5 November show how much that depended on which pages were read: eight of twelve moved into the table when a terms page, an API specification or a page showing data was read, and Shodan stays out only because its pricing pages answered with a browser challenge. A different reading would move rows between the table and the undetermined list without changing which layers are covered; the eight moves of 5 November changed no layer's coverage either. The test counts a free plan behind an account (Netlas) and public pages whose bulk use is restricted (DNS Coffee, RIPEstat) as free reading, which a stricter reading of the brief would not.

The candidate list is not a census.
