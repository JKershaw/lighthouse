# LH062: In published measurements, how long do exposed credentials stay publicly exposed, and stay valid, unprompted and after notification?

**In short.** We asked what published research says about how long a leaked password, key or token stays in public view, and how long it keeps working, with and without anyone telling its owner. Six fixed searches and two papers we already knew gave us three research papers and two security companies' blog posts that followed leaked credentials over time. In the papers, most leaked secrets in one sample of public code were still in place after 16 days, and keys exposed on websites stayed up for about a year on average, as far as nine archive snapshots could show; after the researchers emailed the sites' owners, half the exposed keys came down within two weeks while about three in four of the keys that had worked at the start still did, and exposures had crept back up nearly six months on. None of them compared notified owners with owners left untold, so none can say what notification itself changed, and no two of them counted the same thing in the same way, so their figures are reported one at a time. This version has had an evidence review, whose fixes are applied here, but not yet its reader review, so it's still unreviewed in part and nothing from it is on a public page.

## Answer

**The stop gate is met** (derived, tally): searches.md holds the six query entries, all run, none failed; fetchlog.csv shows six document GETs of the twelve allowed, all HTTP 200, and three robots refusals before any request, which use no GET; every one of the nine candidates in the register was read or is unavailable, so both candidate lists are exhausted; no fetchlog row is dated after 14 December 2026. This is therefore a record of a completed read, not an interim one.

**Partly inconclusive under the brief.** Two of its conditions apply:

- **No included after-notification claim rests on a randomised design or an unnotified comparison** (A07's are before and after only; W6's state none). So no effect of notification can be stated, only descriptions of what was seen after it.
- **No group of claims sharing a unit of count and an outcome spans two documents** (derived, tally: 0 of 9 unit and outcome groups). Claims within one study share units, so A07's rows are set side by side within that study, but no figure from one study is set against another's. A02 counts secrets that passed its filters, not checked with providers and not all sensitive, while A07 counts credentials its authors verified with providers, so they are different counts and stay apart.

The other conditions don't apply: three venue documents were included, two of them with claims kept, so even without the third there are two; A07 and W6 have after-notification arms; A07 checks validity (as do W4 and W6); the seven venue claims are not vendor; walls and refusals number three, not more than four.

**B1 is included but gives no figure here.** It studies two super-app platforms' mini-apps and meets the brief's three inclusion conditions, but its follow-up of validity and presence over time covers one named platform's apps only, a group defined by naming a platform, so its three claims are withheld under the brief's Never done rule (claims.csv marks them `kept: no`; the tally counts 12 claims, not 15). Version 0.1 kept them; see Corrections.

**What changed with version 0.2** (Corrections): the conclusion didn't. The stop gate is met, the same two inconclusive conditions apply and no new one does, and the record still describes what each study saw rather than an effect of notification or a comparison across studies. What changed is narrower: B1's figures are withdrawn, so there is no "mixed" notification class, and the unit question between A02 and A07 is settled as different counts rather than left open.

**What the studies report, each in its authors' terms (observations, all measured, all field):**

- **Still exposed, unprompted (venue).** About 6 per cent of leaked secrets found in public GitHub files were removed from the current branch within an hour, over 12 per cent within a day and 19 per cent within 16 days, leaving 81 per cent; and no monitored repository whose owner removed a secret rewrote its history, so removed secrets stayed reachable there (A02, 2018, secrets; known case). Credentials exposed on websites' landing pages stayed public for an average of 12 months (1 to 60), measured across nine archive snapshots, which bound it, with about 10 per cent fewer in the first month (A07, to September 2025, 1,748 credentials; known case).
- **Still valid, unprompted (venue): not answered.** No kept venue claim measures validity without notification. A02 didn't check whether secrets worked: its "valid secrets" are candidates that passed the authors' filters, which they note aren't always sensitive. A07's validity checks are those after its emails.
- **After notification (venue).** After the authors emailed website owners and third parties (at least one email reached 68.5 per cent of sites), exposed credentials fell from 1,405 at day 0 to 706 at day 14 (the authors' "50%"), and those still valid as checked from 1,175 to 865 (about 26 per cent fewer); by day 172 exposures not removed had risen to 1,019 while those still valid had fallen to 660 (A07, known case). In Lighthouse's arithmetic, within A07 only, that is 50.2 and 72.5 per cent of day 0's exposures at days 14 and 172, and 73.6 and 56.2 per cent of day 0's valid credentials (derived, tally).
- **Vendor stream, reported only as such.** One vendor tracked 10,000 live secrets in 9,399 public GitHub files daily for 31 days in late 2023 and reports 74 per cent still valid at day 31, 63 per cent of files exposing live secrets still public, and more than half of keys whose file was taken down still valid; whether owners were told is not stated (W4). Another reports, relaying its own 2024 report, which robots.txt kept us from reading, that over 91 per cent of the leaks it alerted owners to were still valid five days after the alert, and that of 5,000 repositories where "the secret was gone within 5 days", 28.2 per cent were still public, without saying whether the secret stayed reachable (W6). The vendor table is not compared row for row with the venue table.

Read together (interpretation, labelled so): in the documents read, removal from public view and loss of validity are separate outcomes that can move apart. In the venue stream, A07's day-172 rows show it within one study: exposures not removed rose while those still valid fell. In the vendor stream, W4 reports it within its own data: more than half of keys whose file was taken down still worked. That is a description of two papers' kept figures and two posts, from six searches and two known documents, not of the literature, and nothing here says whether notification works, how any provider or platform compares with another, or why credentials stay exposed or valid.

## Method

The brief's six queries (Q1 to Q6) were run once each, verbatim and in order, on 27 November 2026, 14:14:18 to 14:15:20 UTC, after the brief and its schedule rows were committed at 12:32:36 UTC; none errored and none was reworded or rerun (searches.md). Results were screened on title, URL, venue and year as shown, into the venue stream (NDSS, USENIX Security, IEEE S&P, CCS, IMC, arXiv cs.CR), the vendor stream (a security company's own site and its own measurement) or neither (locators, never fetched). A07 was returned by Q1 and entered as a candidate in its own right; A02 was not returned under its own title and entered as a known document, as the brief requires. The register, fixed before the first GET, held three venue candidates (A07 and A02 in the conference tier, the super-app paper, B1, on arXiv) and six vendor candidates, so six of the venue stream's nine GETs passed to the vendor stream, and the order of reading never bound.

Each candidate was read by one gated one-off GET through scripts/lh_fetch.py, robots applied before the request and any redirect (one vendor page redirected to a trailing-slash form, robots-checked). Three vendor URLs were refused by robots.txt before any request and use no GET under the brief. No retry was needed. A07's and A02's SHA-256 values match LH061's derived files, so the same bytes were read. No body was kept: PDFs never are under the brief, and the HTML pages state no licence allowing redistribution; each read has a derived file with URL, read time, status, SHA-256, inclusion decision, the facts used and short quotations. Inclusion was decided on the document read by the brief's three conditions, and each claim was classed once, with stream, vendor label and setting, by the reading subagent. Version 0.1 was written from those retained files only; no document was reread for it. Version 0.2 also uses the passages the evidence review quotes from its five rereads (R-0162; same bytes by SHA-256), and no request was made for it. fetch.py wrote "pending (keep.py)" into fetchlog.csv's body_kept column; that column and the note column were completed by hand after reading.

**Judgement calls, as the evidence review left them.** B1 stays included (it meets the three conditions) but gives no figure, under the Never done rule (above); that makes 0.1's "mixed" notification label for it moot, and the label is gone. W6's narrow inclusion stands, classed measured on the post's description of the vendor's own checks, post and report being one origin. W4's clock is the day a commit appeared in the feed the vendor watched, which the post treats as both exposure and discovery. A02's secrets and A07's credentials are different counts (Answer).

**Safety conditions as applied.** The research safety assessment's narrowing to published aggregate durations and shares was followed in the reads and in this record: no per-provider or per-platform figure is kept, and A07's statement that one AI developer's keys are in its population is recorded without naming it; no reason why credentials stay exposed or valid, and no method of finding or testing them, is restated or tallied; no credential, repository, host, account or domain was looked up or copied; no dataset or tool was fetched; nobody was contacted; robots.txt was obeyed under the one-off rule; email addresses are withheld. **The check repeated for version 0.2** (27 November 2026), as the brief asks when a document contains per-provider weakness: B1's follow-up figures are one named platform's, framed by its authors as a judgement of that platform's enforcement, so they are no longer kept, quoted or tallied, the record no longer attaches the platform's name to any figure, and derived/arxiv2307.09317v1.json was rewritten without them. Outcome: proceed, narrowed as before; nothing else read needed withholding, and no other work stopped. The 0.1 figures remain in git history (commit 82e159c), as nothing is rewritten on a shared branch.

## Limits

- Six fixed queries, run once, are a narrow window; what they didn't surface is not found by this search, not absent. Two venue results shown with no title (a USENIX Security 2019 PDF and an arXiv PDF) and several untitled repository links were set aside on that metadata, and since snippets weren't kept, whether any concerned the question can't be checked.
- Three venue documents and two vendor posts, with observation from 2018 to at least late 2025 (A07's day-172 date isn't stated), on two kinds of place with figures kept (public code on one platform, websites), with windows from five days to five years; the third venue document, on super-apps' mini-apps, gives none here. Two of the three venue documents are known cases whose outcomes were seen before the brief, and the third, the only venue document the searches surfaced on their own beyond A07, keeps no figure; the venue table is the two documents the study started from.
- No study had an unnotified comparison, so every after-notification figure is a description. A07's comparison with its own archival rate is the authors', and rests on different windows and clocks.
- The vendor stream's three highest-ranked candidates were one publisher's annual reports, all refused by robots.txt, and both of that publisher's posts read relay them; W6's figures rest on a report not read. The readable vendor set is blog posts.
- "Unprompted" is clean only where a study says no one was told: A02 states no notification during monitoring but not whether the platform's own scanning told owners; A07's archival measure states no intervention; W4 doesn't say. The brief's writer expected platform forwarding to mix some populations; the documents read don't settle it.
- Each document was read once by one agent for the study, and no body is kept. The evidence review reread five of the six and checked Findings against them; it is a second agent's contribution, not independent confirmation, and W5 (excluded) was not reread.
- The derived files' "body_removed" text is a fixed sentence from the shared helper giving robots or page reservation as the reason, while their "body_kept" field gives the real one (a PDF, or no licence); a neutral note there would be a change to the shared helper, outside this study. It changes no figure.
- Nothing here says whether removal, revocation or notification works in general or now, why credentials stay exposed or valid, how any provider or platform compares with another, or whether any named system is compromised.
