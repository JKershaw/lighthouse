**In short.** We followed how much of what people downloaded from nodejs.org, in each Node.js release line fixed on 21 October, was the fixed release or a newer one. Thirty days on (20 November) it was 65.5 per cent of line 22's downloads, 74.3 per cent of line 24's and 96.3 per cent of line 26's, counting three newer versions first seen on 11 November as fixed, a rule chosen after those counts were seen that assumes, without checking, that they are releases carrying the fix; left out, as the count was first written, the figures are 39.8, 60.6 and 85.8 per cent. Most of the rise in those shares from 11 November came from more downloads at or above the fix, arriving with the newer versions, while downloads below the fix fell only a little, by 4 to 7 per cent on average (worked out after the counts were seen); a download is a request, not a machine, from one channel of several. The record has had its evidence review, its reader's review and a check of the changes those made; the revision of the week-one piece comes next.

## Answer

Thirty days after Node.js's security releases (day 30, Friday 20 November 2026), the fixed release or a newer one in its line made up 65.5 per cent of line 22's downloads from nodejs.org, 74.3 per cent of line 24's and 96.3 per cent of line 26's, counting the three in-line versions first seen on 11 November (v22.23.5, v24.22.3, v26.13.1) as at or above the fix (derived; post hoc rule, below, which assumes, unchecked, that those versions carry the fix). By the measure as the brief wrote it, which leaves those versions out of numerator and denominator alike, the same day reads 39.8, 60.6 and 85.8 per cent. By day 30 those versions were 42.6, 34.8 and 74.2 per cent of each line's downloads, so the two readings are far apart. All three lines first passed half on day 2 under either rule; line 22 dipped below half again on days 3 and 4, a weekend, and lines 24 and 26 stayed at or above half from day 2. One minus the day-30 share, the part of each line's downloads below the fix, is 34.5, 25.7 and 3.7 per cent by the amended rule (60.2, 39.4 and 14.2 as written). In line 22, most of that part is five or more releases behind the fix. Lines the release does not fix took 11.5 per cent of version-attributed downloads on day 30.

**What the late rise is.** By the amended rule each line's share was a few points higher on days 21 to 30 than on days 13 to 20 (daily ranges in Findings). That is mostly more downloads at or above the fix, arriving with the newer versions; downloads below the fix fell only a little between the two periods (by 4.0 to 6.5 per cent of their mean), so the smaller part of the rise is fewer requests for old versions (derived, post hoc, worked out after every outcome was seen; Findings). Downloads below the fix fell mainly between the first week and day 12.

**Which rule this record leads with, and why.** It leads with the amended rule and gives the rule as written beside it, every time. The question asks what share is "at or above the fixed release", and under the brief's own wording a later release in the line already counts, as the listed v24.22.2 and v26.13.0 did from their first day. The rule as written stopped counting newer releases only because nodejs.org's robots.txt has refused its release list since 9 November, so the three versions that appeared on 11 November never entered the list. From day 22 its share measures a shrinking, older remainder of each line, and it falls because, as the newer versions appear, downloads of listed versions at or above the fix fall far more than downloads below it (derived from totals; checkpoints/2026-11-16.md; in line 22, 5,350,355 and 3,119,227 on day 20, 1,829,805 and 2,764,140 on day 30, from notes/R-0128-lh034-v1-evidence.md). Daily counts can't show whether the same requesters switched. The amended rule has two weaknesses, both stated wherever it is used. It was made after the day-21 counts were seen (one post hoc rule; it changes 30 of measure 1's 93 line-days, days 21 to 30 in three lines, measure 3's denominators, and both of amendment 1's tests, two comparisons under each rule). And it assumes that each of the three keys is a real published release that carries the October fix; that wasn't read, because the list that would date them is refused and no other source was opened. The choice to lead with it was itself made after every outcome was seen.

Set beside the comparators named in the brief, as descriptions in their units. On day 1 the three Node.js lines were below all four Python security fixes' download shares (the same unit, from a different index) and above all four PHP branches' Censys patched shares (a different unit). On day 14, when the two rules agree, line 22 was within the Python range and below every PHP branch, line 24 above the Python range and below every PHP branch, and line 26 above both. On day 30, by the amended rule (post hoc), the same placements held; by the rule as written, line 22 was below both ranges, line 24 within the Python range and below every PHP branch, and line 26 above the Python range and within the PHP range. Against the July security release on the same instrument, October's share was higher in every line on day 14, and on day 30 higher by the amended rule (post hoc) and lower by the rule as written. None of this says one ecosystem or one release is faster, or why a curve has its shape.

On 30 October, after the first week was seen, two predictions were written down for day 30, shared with the PHP study. Both passed under both rules, the amended one made after day 21 was seen. Test A asked whether less of Node.js's day-30 share came after the first week than of PHP's, comparing medians over lines and branches. It passed, but it adds little. It sets a share of a day's downloads, which can settle as soon as what is fetched changes, against a share of standing services, which moves as services are rescanned, rebuilt or drop out of the count, so the difference in units could produce the result whatever happened (interpretation). And neither side is plain uptake. Under the rule as written, Node.js's side passes because its share falls after day 21, the counting artefact above. Under the amended rule it mixes fewer downloads below the fix, mostly by day 12, with added downloads at or above the fix (in line 22, the median line, from day 21, as the newer versions arrived). PHP's side rests mostly on days 7 to 14, when patched counts rose in every branch, but Censys's "patched" probably includes services first seen already fixed, and its rise after day 14 came mostly from fewer services counted in three branches (LH033's record, version 1.2). Test B means what it says: on days 28 to 30, line 22 sat more than five points below the lower of lines 24 and 26, by 8.04 points by the amended rule and 18.83 as written. They are two comparisons under each of the two rules, testing readings made after the first week was seen, and they say nothing about cause.

## Method

## Limits

- **A download is a request**, not a machine, an install or a user; a machine that builds often is counted often. Header tarballs fetched by node-gyp are among the requests and can't be separated by version (nodejs.org/metrics, written for the historic logs and assumed to hold for the current summaries, not checked against the build code).
- **nodejs.org is one channel of several.** Distribution packages, Homebrew, built container images, corporate mirrors and caches, and runners that keep their own copy aren't counted. A machine installed long ago that never fetches again is invisible here.
- **The two rules.** The rule as written understates each line's share from day 22 because it drops the newer releases; the amended rule is post hoc and assumes those three keys are published releases that carry the October fix, which wasn't read. Every day of both is in Findings.
- **A share mixes volume and uptake.** Under the amended rule, most of the rise on days 21 to 30 is more downloads at or above the fix, arriving with the newer versions, rather than fewer below it, which fell only a little (post hoc, Findings). A higher share is not, by itself, more of a line moving.
- **The release list stops on 5 November.** Release order for measure 3 and the set of listed versions come from that list. A release published after it in a fixed line could only be counted by the amended rule, and only if it is a plain version above the fix.
- **The unfixed tail includes line 27**, a line that appeared after the fix and whose status wasn't read (post hoc note, Findings).
- **The file's day boundary** is taken as the UTC day of the downloads; the metrics page says only that a file is named for its last entry.
- **Different units and indexes.** The Python shares come from a different index and population; the PHP shares are services as last scanned by Censys, floors on protection. The comparisons describe shapes; they don't rank the ecosystems or attribute a difference to severity, publicity, exploitation reports, AI or any cause. Tests A and B test readings made after the first week was seen.
- **The July comparison's day 0** is the list date of v26.5.1, a day before the July files were posted; one post hoc alternative alignment is shown.
- **Review status.** Version 1.0 was cleared by the evidence review (notes/R-0128-lh034-v1-evidence.md), whose three wording fixes are in. The reader-and-inference review (notes/R-0131-lh034-v1-reader.md) is resolved in version 1.1, and a fresh agent's recheck of what version 1.1 changed (notes/R-0133-lh034-v1-recheck.md) cleared it after small wording corrections. A second agent's agreement is a review contribution, not independent confirmation.
- Not checked: what v22.23.5, v24.22.3, v26.13.1 and v27.0.0 fix or when they were published; whether the summaries' build matches the metrics page's description; whether nodejs.org's robots.txt allowed this study's requests before 9 November (left to studies/LH047).
