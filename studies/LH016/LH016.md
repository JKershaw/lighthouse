## Answer

**Who changed what a claim means.** In the six rounds of 28 and 29 September 2026 (LH011 to LH015), 125 changes were made to the pieces, their public surfaces and their records while a step of the workflow ran, and 66 of them changed what a careful reader of a public surface would believe (tier A in the codebook the brief fixed before any change was coded; derived measurement). The evidence review made the most, 23 in six rounds, and 13 of those were figures or how sure a sentence sounded. Changes to what a claim meant, what a count counts, what caused what, or a new reading, came mostly from the keeper's reading of two released pieces (5 of its 8) and from the reader-and-inference review's second stage (8 of its 17), and seldom from the evidence review (4 of its 23); this grouping of the codebook's types was made after the counts. All 8 of the keeper's changes, and 15 of stage 2's 17, altered text that an evidence review had read and passed.

**Fixes need checking.** Text written to resolve a check carried problems of its own. 8 of the recheck's 10 public-claim changes corrected text that stage 2's resolution had written minutes before, most often a new reading stated more surely than its evidence or without its label, and 6 of the 7 changes the evidence review made to LH011's revision corrected text written after the keeper's reading (derived; a reading made after the counts, from a literal search of each round's starting text).

**What reached release.** In both frames, every correction to a released piece's claims came from the keeper's reading of the released piece or from work the keeper commissioned, directly (18 of the 35 public-claim corrections after release) or in the rounds those readings opened (the other 17). Of the twelve pieces released before the reader review existed, three had their main claims corrected after release, LH010's, LH011's and LH012's, with the notices that carried their findings onto the synthesis; the replays below found two more problems still standing in released pieces. The two pieces released since the reader review joined, LH014's and LH015's, have had hours, which says nothing yet.

**The replay.** Three fresh reader reviews were run on LH011's and LH012's pieces exactly as released, before the keeper's corrections. Each found the keeper's central point: that a low share of downloads was read as a slow spread (both runs on LH011's piece, one with the review's list of prompts and one without), and that an old Python does not say which older boto3 was fetched (the run on LH012's). Of 16 known answers scored, 6 were found, 6 partly and 4 missed. Neither run on LH011's piece raised the first-day reading's missing label, and on that piece the list of prompts made no difference to what was found. The replays also found material problems still standing in released pieces, which the driving session checked on the retained data. All six litellm releases that LH011's piece says reached half, "the typical one after 25 days", first did so on one day, 1 May 2026, and none held it. And a new version's share rose after day 2 in 339 of the 355 releases LH012 read, by a median 7.2 points, which "holds for the month" does not say. Both are filed for correction.

**Cost.** At list rates, estimated from transcripts: the evidence review $30.41 over six rounds, $1.32 per public-claim change it made, and $1.94 in the three rounds where the reader review also ran; the reader review with its recheck $22.47 over three rounds, $0.83; the blind check, run once, $7.24 for 3 public-claim changes and 5 to the record; the keeper's reading no model cost. The steps read different texts in a fixed order, so a later step's count is what it added after the others, not what it would find alone.

**The research steps** leave no before and after to count. The briefs' conventions set the headline label in four records and withheld one (LH015's "as a rule"); six literature checks changed designs, and none is recorded as changing an outcome. Of 23 readings made after the counts that entered LH010 to LH015, one has since been tested on data not read (it held), ten have a planned test and twelve none, six of those twelve from the reader review's second stage.

**How far the coding holds.** A second coder on another model coded 30 changes drawn at random, without seeing which step made them. It agreed with the first on whether each was material for all 30, and on the tier for 28 (Cohen's kappa 0.88), above the bar the brief set; but only one of the 30 was not material, so the line between material and not was tested on little. Types agreed for 21 of 29 and directions for 26 of 29, so the grouping by type is the looser reading. The second coder's types would move two more of stage 2's changes, and none of the evidence review's, into the group that changes what a claim means.

**What Lighthouse should learn** (interpretation; the section of that name gives each lesson's rows). Keep both reviews in their order: the evidence review for figures, calculations and consistency, the reader review for what the figures mean. Treat the recheck as part of the reader review, not polish, and give any text written to resolve a review, the keeper's included, a fresh reader before release. When a reading made after the counts reaches a public surface, name its test or say that none is planned. Keep the blind check for studies whose outcome rests on a classifier the writer built. Correct AGENTS.md's account of where the first drafts' errors were caught.

## What Lighthouse should learn

Interpretation throughout, each point tied to the findings it rests on. Six rounds are cases, not a rate, and the steps ran in one order.

1. **Keep both reviews, in their order, for different work.** The evidence review corrects figures and how sure a sentence sounds, and keeps surfaces consistent (Findings 2 and 3: 13 of its 23 public-claim changes). It passed every problem of meaning that the keeper and stage 2 later changed (Finding 4). The reader review's stage 2 and its recheck made most of the public-claim changes before release where they ran (22 of 35 in P5 and P6) at lower cost per change (Finding 6). Neither does the other's work, so neither replaces the other. The replays support the split: on two texts, the reader review found the point of meaning the keeper later caught, and only in part the problems of window and label that the evidence review's rules have targeted since the keeper's reading of 28 September (Finding 12).
2. **The recheck is part of the reader review, and any text written to resolve a review needs a fresh reader.** Most of the recheck's changes, and most of the evidence review's changes to LH011's revision, corrected text written minutes before in response to a review (Finding 5). The text a round writes last is the least read. The rule that a fresh agent rechecks the reader review's resolution should hold for every resolution before release: of the keeper's reading, of the evidence review's own edits where no later step reads them, and of the reader review.
3. **A reading made after the counts that reaches a public surface should name its test.** Such readings are how the pieces' main claims changed meaning in these rounds (Findings 3 and 8), and they are the least tested: one of 23 tested, twelve with no plan. A round that adds one should say in the record which brief, amendment or issue will test it, or that none can, and the programme should list those without a test.
4. **Keep the blind check where an outcome rests on a classifier the writer built.** Its one run moved three public figures by correcting two rules, at the highest cost per public-claim change (Findings 2 and 6). One case says it can find what a reading of the text cannot; it does not say how often.
5. **The keeper's reading has been the only check after release**, and every correction of a released claim in the frames traces to it (Finding 7). On the two texts where the keeper's catches are known, a reader review as it now stands found the central one, so the keeper's reading need no longer be the only check of meaning. But each fresh reader found material problems that every earlier one had passed, two of them still standing in released pieces (Finding 12), so no single reading exhausts a piece. A reader review of the pieces released before 29 September, which never had one, is the cheapest next check: the replays cost $3.59 to $4.98 each.
6. **The research steps work before the drafts and cannot be credited here.** The briefs' conventions set every headline label in the rounds read and withheld one (Finding 10). Nothing in the history says what they prevented, and no change is proposed.
7. **AGENTS.md's account of the first drafts is too simple.** Three successive readings, the first relayed by the keeper, each found problems the one before had passed (Finding 13), which is the pattern of the later rounds too.

What this session changed on the strength of these (programme.md, Next, says the same): AGENTS.md step 2 now says what each check found, with the first drafts' three readings; step 4, that a fresh agent reads whatever a round writes to resolve a review, the keeper's reading included; and the Evidence paragraph, that a reading made after the counts that reaches a public surface names its test or says that none can. notes/reader-review.md says the recheck is the first reader of the resolution's text, and records the replay, with the list of prompts kept since it made no difference either way. The About page describes the second review, the recheck and the keeper's reading. Issues #21 and #22 were filed for the two corrections the replays found, and #23 for a reader review of the pieces that never had one.

## Method

The brief (fixed at cb97e4e before any diff or finding was read) set two frames. The primary frame is 19 commit windows in six rounds, each window the diff while one step ran, taken from the commit subjects alone; the first coder read each window's diff on the coded surfaces with `git diff --word-diff`, attributed each change to the step whose note, commit message or close-out named it, and coded it by the brief's codebook (tier, type, direction, improved, what earlier steps had passed, post release, post hoc), writing one row per change to data/changes.csv (data/coding_log.md has its commands and hard calls). The secondary frame, the pieces released on 26 and 27 September, was read for changes after release by a second research agent, which also listed the readings made after the counts in LH010 to LH015 and the research steps (data/secondary_log.md). The spend table (data/costs.csv) was filled from the close-outs and release commits before the brief was committed. scripts/tally.py, committed before any coded row existed, computes the measures M1 to M7; its A-tier breakdowns by type, by improvement and before release in P5 and P6 were added after the first run and say so in the code. The second coder's sample was drawn with seed 16 from the table sorted by identifier (scripts/draw_sample.py) before any tally was read, and scripts/compare_blind.py compares the codes. scripts/post_hoc_origin.py, written after the counts, searches each A-tier change's quoted text in its round's starting commit. The replays (amendment 2, fixed before any run) gave three fresh Fable 5.1 reviewers the reader review's task texts, verbatim or without the stage 2 list of prompts, on git worktrees of the two release commits, and the driving session scored their notes against the known answers the amendment fixed (review/replay_scores.csv). Replay: `bash studies/LH016/replay.sh`.

## Limits

- **Changes, not truth.** The study counts what each step changed and whether the change moved the text towards the retained evidence, as the first coder judged it; it does not say whether any claim is now right, and it cannot see an error no step changed.
- **Order and credit.** The steps ran in one order on texts earlier steps had left, so a step's count is what it added in its place. No step ran without the others, and nothing here measures what the workflow would have released without a given step, except the replays and the corrections after release.
- **Few rounds.** Six rounds in two days, the reader review in three of them (one a rehearsal on text the keeper had already corrected), the blind check in one. Every comparison is a description of these rounds; costs are estimates from transcripts at list rates, with output tokens estimated from characters.
- **One coder's judgement.** Tier, type and attribution are the first coder's, checked on a sample of 30 by a second coder who saw no step; see Finding 11. Where a window mixed a step's edits with the driving session's, the attribution rule credited the step the notes name, so D is a floor on the driving session's own changes.
- **What the history holds.** The clone starts at db300e2, so the first drafts of 26 September are read only through the notes. The keeper's own words are not in the repository; what the keeper asked is taken from the revision commits and the close-outs.
- **The corrections after release** were made while the keeper was reading; pieces the keeper did not read, or read later, may hold problems no step has changed. LH014's and LH015's pieces had been out for hours.
- **The replays** are three draws of a stochastic reviewer on two texts; see Finding 12.
- **The origin reading** (Finding 5) was made after the counts, by a literal search that can miss a passage the coder shortened.
