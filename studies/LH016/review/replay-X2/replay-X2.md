# What a reader would believe, written before reading the record

**The finding, said back.** Of the 37 most-downloaded Python libraries with releases from April to August 2026, 26 usually had a new version at half or more of their downloads within two days, usually the next, and that share then barely moved for a month. That is 70 per cent: most, but under the authors' pre-set line for a rule. For eleven, boto3 and three relatives, numpy, pandas, pytest, protobuf, grpcio-status, fsspec and litellm, no release reached half within two days, and, litellm's six late releases aside, older versions kept most downloads all month. The pace belongs to the library, not the release.

**Observed.** PyPI's download log, via ClickHouse's copy, checked against pypistats; 424 releases; the daily share that was the new version or later, for 30 days. For 104 releases, the pip/uv build-server flag over three days: 32 per cent of the new version's downloads flagged against 41 of the replaced version's, about seven points, in 96 of 104 releases and every library.

**Explained, and causes.** Little, by its own account. Why the eleven lag was not measured; the candidates, other libraries' version limits or unsupported Pythons, are next. The flag gap is guessed to be builds pinning versions while fresh setups take the newest. Only arithmetic is settled: by release it is 30 per cent, since three daily releasers dominate.

**Limits noticed.** Downloads are not installs or runs; mirrors, caches and scanners distort; the flag is a floor; days not hours, so day 0 says little; big libraries only; resampling spans 54 to 84 per cent, straddling the line; nothing about AI.

## What the round establishes

The counts stand. R-0014 recomputed every headline figure and re-read eight live; my four reads agree in kind, and litellm's 52 in-window pre-releases, folded into "~other", are 0.1 to 0.5 per cent of its downloads, so "~other" is older versions. What the profile shows is a mix, not a speed. Every one of the 37 libraries rises from day 0 to day 1 (review/figure_checks.txt) and then runs flat, so the day-1 share measures how much of a library's download stream re-resolves to whatever is newest; the rest sits on older versions. Both components exist in every library: versions older than the first April release held a median third of the 26 fast libraries' downloads on day 1 and three quarters of the eleven's, and thinned by a median 6 points over 82 days in the 26 and 10 points over 121 days in the eleven (§1). At the end of the window, 102 to 179 days on, pre-April versions still held 46 to 95 per cent of the downloads of ten of the eleven (litellm 30; §1, §2). The sharp split is in the proportions; the slow component moves at the scale of months in both groups. The CI gap says builds are over-represented in the slow component, as the record reads it. The eleven are not one kind: botocore's old-version downloads concentrate (top 20 versions 46 per cent, one version 13), boto3's spread over 2,069 versions (top 20 hold 12 per cent; half needs 198), and litellm's daily total swung from 10 to 22 to 3 million within the window (x2_requery.txt; §7).

## Material claim problems

**1. Pace for mix.** articles/two-days-for-most.md, "What this does to what we said before": "software taking a range of versions picks up a new release within days ... For most of the most downloaded libraries, that holds ... For nearly a third of them it does not, and the older versions go on being fetched for weeks"; and articles/short/two-days-for-most.md: "ordinary updates already move at two speeds". Evidence: review/figure_checks.txt (all 37 rise from day 0 to day 1); review/piece_checks.txt (the eleven's day-1 and day-2 medians within a point or two); §1 and §2 (both components in every library; pre-April versions still 46 to 95 per cent of ten of the eleven after 102 to 179 days: months, not weeks). A reader takes away that range-taking software in the eleven does not pick up releases and that libraries fall into two speed classes; the log shows range-taking software taking every release the next day in all 37, and the eleven differing in how little of their stream is of that kind. Resolution: say the two-day pace holds wherever software re-resolves, in all 37, and what differs by library is the share of downloads that do, above half for 26 and between 2 and 34 per cent for eleven; "weeks" to "months"; "two speeds" to "two components, mixed differently".

**2. The family explanation for boto3.** Full piece, "What holds the eleven back": "Amazon's boto3, botocore and s3transfer and the aiobotocore library built on them restrict one another's versions ... so software that installs one of them can be held back by what another allows." Evidence: §3: boto3 requires botocore, and their downloads of versions at most 30 days old match (1.1 to 1.4 to one), but boto3's downloads of older versions are about three times botocore's (91 to 105 million a day against 27 to 36), so at least two-thirds of boto3's old-version downloads come without a botocore download and are not installations of a pinned pair into an empty environment; x2_requery.txt: on 12 August boto3's old-version downloads spread over 2,069 versions with a recency gradient (uploads of 2024 to 2026: 85 per cent), while botocore's concentrate (1.42.97 alone 13 per cent). A bound holds boto3 and botocore together, so it fits botocore and cannot make most of boto3's plateau. Resolution: keep the guess for botocore, s3transfer and aiobotocore; for boto3 say that something fetches old boto3 wheels alone, across thousands of versions, and that its nature is unknown; carry the pairing test into the next brief.

**3. litellm's late reachers.** Full piece, "Most, not all": "For litellm, six of its 46 releases eventually did, the typical one after 25 days." Evidence: §6 and §7: the six are litellm's first six releases (3 to 13 April) and all crossed on the same day, 1 May, when the old-version share dipped; it was back at 73 to 78 per cent by mid-May, fell to 18 by August, and litellm's total went from 21 million a day in August to 2.9 million in September. That is a stream switching, not uptake over 25 days. Resolution: say the six crossed together on one day when litellm's older-version downloads briefly dipped, and that litellm's volume swung sevenfold within the window, so its figures describe automation as much as users.

**4. The unit behind "not as a rule".** Full piece, "Most, not all": "26 of the 37 libraries reached half within two days: 70 per cent. That is below our line for a rule". Evidence: §4: with the four AWS libraries, which the piece itself says release and constrain one another together, as one unit, 26 of 34 is 76.5 per cent; with protobuf and grpcio-status as one too, 26 of 33 is 78.8; both above the 75 per cent line. The brief fixed the project as the unit, so the record's verdict follows its rule; the piece should say the verdict hangs on counting a family as four. Resolution: one sentence beside the resampling interval.

## Readability

- The chart's title, "The day after a release set its share for the month", is the finding; the headline could carry it.
- "Who stays behind" and "What holds the eleven back" presume delay; "Who sits on older versions" and "Where the eleven's downloads sit" match the evidence.

## Next step

Continue with issue #14, adjusted. Put the question as composition, not delay: what the slow component of a library's downloads consists of, and why it is large for eleven. The programme's two explanations leave different traces and the log can tell them apart, but only if the brief names four and their signatures before any count: (A) dependents' bounds: concentration on the versions named dependents pin (botocore-like); (B) Python support windows: old versions on old `python_minor`, at the last version each supports; (C) diffuse pinning: a long tail with a recency gradient on current Pythons (boto3-like); (D) fetches that are not installations: unpaired with a required dependency, or a stream that switches on and off (litellm). A study built for (A) and (B) alone would read boto3's long tail as inconclusive or misread it. The evidence is available now, anonymous and cheap: the by-version table gives the spread for all 37 in tiny reads (versions needed for half of the old downloads, and their ages); `pypi.pypi` gives `python_minor`, installer, `ci`, system and country for a project-day within the billion-row limit (boto3's 12 August is 125 million rows); PyPI's JSON gives `requires_python` per version and `requires_dist` for a list of dependents fixed in the brief (PyPI has no reverse index; the top-80 list supplies s3fs, google-auth and googleapis-common-protos, general knowledge the rest; GitHub's pages are refused and not needed). ClickPy holds every day since 2016, so waiting loses nothing and gains nothing. First probes: old-version downloads of boto3 and botocore on one day by installer, flag and Python (two reads), and the weeks of 10 and 17 August, when botocore's pre-April share fell from 59 to 49 per cent (§6), matched against dependents' releases. The comparison set is not optional: the 26's slow component is the baseline, and the retained counts already give five months of its thinning offline (§1), which the brief should state as what it knows. Rank bands, LH011's second Next, would widen the population without explaining the plateau; the CI-share composition candidate rides along in the same per-download read.
