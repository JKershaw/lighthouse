# What a reader would believe, written before reading the record

*Replay X1, stage one, 29 September 2026. Read: the full piece, its short form and the README's "Current focus"; nothing else.*

**The finding, said back.** Of the 37 most-downloaded Python libraries that released between April and August 2026, most (26, or 70 per cent) had a new version at half of their downloads within two days, usually by the next day, though short of the 75 per cent set beforehand for calling it a rule. For eleven, including boto3, numpy, pandas and pytest, no release reached half in two days, and for ten not even in a month. Whatever share a release held the day after, it kept all month. It qualifies an earlier one-library reading.

**Observed.** Daily downloads by version from the public PyPI log, 424 releases, 30 days each; and, for 104 releases, build-server-flagged downloads were a smaller share of a new version's first three days than of the replaced version's, about seven points.

**Explained.** Nothing, the pieces say, yet a reader leaves with a probable story: pace belongs to the library, not the release; the eleven are held back by other packages' version limits or dropped Python versions, which the README names as the next question; builds lag because they pin versions while fresh installs take the newest.

**Limits noticed.** A download is not an install or a run. The flag is a floor on builds. Days, not hours, with release days mostly spent. Only top libraries, five months. By release, only 30 per cent reached half; resampling puts the 70 between 54 and 84. The month-long flatness was noticed, not pre-set. The short form drops the "not a rule" verdict, the uncertainty and the by-release count.

# Stage two, after reading the record

*Read: studies/LH011/ entire (record 0.2, brief with amendment 1, sources, data, scripts, the earlier review's calculation files), investigations/software-updates.md and programme.md's Next. My calculations: studies/LH011/review/replay_x1_checks.py, output replay_x1_checks.txt, sections cited below by number.*

**What the round establishes.** The figures stand. From the retained tables I recounted the headline (26 of 37 libraries, 127 of 424 releases), the opening example (requests 2.34.0 at 54.8 per cent of 64.3 million on 12 May, pandas 3.0.3 at 27.7 of 25.7 million; 60.7 and 32.3 on day 30), the eleven's day-1 and day-30 medians, the flag figures (96 of 104; −6.6 points; 32.2 against 40.7) and the figure's description (sections 1, 2, 4, 6). The at-or-newer measure does not carry the result: by each release's own share 111 of the 127 still reach half and no library changes group (section 3). The frame, the flag's rule in pip's and uv's source, the cross-check and the one-day resolution are as the pieces say. The stage-one picture is what the record holds, with one exception.

**Material claim problems.**

1. A level presented as a speed. *Passages.* articles/short/two-days-for-most.md: "So a new version's pace is set by the library, not only by the release. That matters to anyone watching for something that spreads unusually fast: ordinary updates already move at two speeds." articles/two-days-for-most.md: "it turns out to be the usual pace for these libraries, though not the only one" and "because the ordinary pace differs by library, not only by release"; programme.md's Next carries it on as "a second, slow pace". *Evidence.* Section 4, from data/analysis/release_days.csv: both groups reach their share on day 1 and then drift by the same few points a month, median +3.8 for the 26 and +4.3 for the eleven (day 2 minus day 1: +0.1 and −0.3); on day 1 a library already holds a median 0.94 (the 26) and 0.83 (the eleven) of its day-30 level, with litellm at 0.45 and boto3 at 0.61 because their numerator accumulates a month of later releases. What differs is the level: older versions are 18 to 47 per cent of downloads at day 30 for the 26 and 63 to 97 for the eleven. The five "mixed" libraries are exactly the five whose level sits at 50 to 57 per cent, and at a threshold of a quarter the verdict would read "as a rule", 30 of 37 (section 9): the pre-fixed measure answers "how large is the part of a library's downloads that moves with a release", and the pieces present it as "how fast does a release move". Nothing in the record shows the eleven moving more slowly; it shows a larger part of their downloads not moving in a month, and a part of every library's doing the same. So the measurements can be right while "two speeds" and "held back" are wrong: the reading the pieces do not raise is that each library's downloads are a stable mixture of traffic that takes the newest version overnight and traffic fixed to older versions, in proportions set by who generates the downloads, and boto3's count shows the stream is no count of installs (2.75 times botocore's downloads in August, though every boto3 install needs botocore; section 8). A reader who believes "slow" expects arrival; the evidence supports none. *Resolution.* In "The day after decides" and the closing section say that the speed was the same everywhere, one night, and that what differs by library is the share that moves at all; in the short form replace the "two speeds" sentence with "a release moves overnight through the part of a library's downloads that moves at all, most of them for 26 libraries and a fifth or less for eleven; the rest did not move in a month"; the README's and the investigation page's "what holds those eleven back" reads better as "what the older-version downloads of those eleven are". No figure changes. Not material: pandas 3.0.4's "no" is its yank (22.7 to 0.2 per cent on day 2, section 7), flagged in the record and not changing pandas' verdict.

**Readability.** (i) Put the five mixed libraries' levels, 50 to 57 per cent, beside "The split was sharp"; it explains the sharpness. (ii) "Who stays behind" names people; the section is about a flag on downloads: "Which downloads carry the build flag". (iii) The opening mixes "55 in every hundred" with "61 per cent"; one form.

**Next step.** Continue with the selected question (issue #14), adjusted. Its evidence can distinguish the two readings in play: dependents' bounds put the older downloads on the few versions the bounds allow, and PyPI's requires_dist names them (the programme cites aiobotocore's bound on botocore; s3fs, rank 59 in the record's top-80 read, is by general knowledge, not read here, the dependent to read first for fsspec and aiobotocore); a support window puts them on the last version for an older Python, which `python_minor` shows. Both are anonymous reads in ClickPy and PyPI JSON, available now and cheap (a project-day of pypi.pypi is well under the per-query cap; the flag subsample read 5.8 billion rows in 104 queries). But neither would see the third reading above, fixed automation or preinstalled runtimes, and the retained data lean that way for the eleven: for the 26 the at-or-newer share is a median 5.4 points higher on weekends, every library positive, and for the eleven a median −0.4; weekend downloads are 0.65 of weekday for the 26 and 0.71 for the eleven, litellm 0.89; and the eleven's replaced release is less flagged CI than the 26's, 32 against 42 per cent (sections 5 and 6; a reading made after the fact, for the brief to fix as a measure, not a finding). So: frame the question as what the traffic that does not move is, not what holds it back; read the older versions' spread, `python_minor`, installer, flag and weekday shape for the eleven and the comparison set on fixed days across several months, so "does it move at all" is answered with "which versions"; fix beforehand what counts as concentrated, and read Requires-Python and the dependents' bounds before any count; and treat boto3's ratio to botocore as a check on what a boto3 download is. Waiting gains nothing, since ClickPy holds the days, and the rank-band question should follow, as the programme says, once the plateau is understood.
