## Answer

In this sample, pinned dependents moved to an announced fix before its advisory at about 1.7 times the pace they moved to a silent one, but the same share of their moves came before the advisory, and the difference rests on few moves in few libraries. Of the 37 fixed releases behind LH008's 68 advisories, 19 said in their own changelog, tag or PyPI description, at or before the release, that they fixed a security flaw, and 18 did not (observation; data/fix_classes.csv). Taking each of LH008's 16 libraries' one event (eight announced, eight silent; seven of each had pinned dependents to follow), 257 repository and event pairs held an affected version when the fix came out, and 173 moved to it by 27 September (derived; data/summary_by_class.csv).

- **Share of moves before the advisory** (the question's measure; derived). Announced: 29 of 105 moves (28 per cent). Silent: 17 of 68 (25 per cent). The announced fixes' advisories came sooner after their releases (median 3.9 days against 10.6), so they had less time in which to draw a move before the advisory; the share is not a fair comparison between the classes by itself.
- **Rate before the advisory** (derived; data/hazard_by_class.csv). From the release to the advisory, 2.36 moves per 100 repository-days waiting for announced fixes (29 moves over 1,228.8 repository-days) against 1.36 for silent ones (17 over 1,246.1). Leaving out any one event, the announced rate stays between 1.84 and 2.84 and the silent between 1.13 and 1.68. The difference is not in the first two days after the release, when the classes ran at the same pace (3.77 on 11 moves, 3.99 on 8), and bots wrote 7 of the 11 and 4 of the 8; it is in days two to seven, stopping at the advisory where it came first: 3.22 on 13 moves against 1.45 on 5, and people wrote 9 of the 13. From day seven to day thirty before an advisory the two were alike again (0.94 on 5 moves, all pyjwt's, against 0.87 on 4).
- **After the advisory** (derived). In its first two days, 5.56 per 100 repository-days for announced fixes (13 moves) against 3.63 for silent ones (6); from day two to seven, 2.71 (14) against 2.75 (10).
- **What the moving commits said** (observation of commit messages). Of the 46 moves before an advisory, one message has a security word, and it cites another library's advisories. No commit here says it moved because a release announced a fix; bots' messages are their usual "Bump" and "Update" lines in both classes.

What this does not say (interpretation). With one release per library, class and library cannot be separated: the announced class's lead before the advisory comes mostly from gitpython (8 moves, 6 of them in its first two days, four of those Renovate's) and pyjwt (10 moves over a 25-day gap), and the days-two-to-seven difference is 13 moves against 5, three of the 13 in one organisation's repositories on one morning. The pattern fits people reading an announced fix's notes and moving within the week, while bots move both classes on the same schedule; it fits equally a difference between the libraries' users. "Silent" means silent in the three sources the rule reads: GitHub's release pages, where litellm, mcp and langchain may put their notes, were refused, and those three releases had no notes in any source read. A move is a request in a repository, not an installation or a run, and no project here is said to have been exposed.

## Findings

**The classes** (observation; data/fix_classes.csv, data/fix_texts.csv, data/flaw_judgements.csv; brief.md amendment 1). Of the 37 fixed releases, 19 are announced at the release: 16 by a security word (in every case in the changelog at the tag; lxml's also in its tag annotation and PyPI description) and 3 by the flaw named alone (gitpython 3.1.49; mako 1.3.11 and 1.3.12); 18 are silent. None is announced late. Nine of the silent entries come near: they name the change in the affected function ("Reject absolute paths in `StaticFiles.lookup_path`", "Add multipart header limits", "Enforce `max_fields` and `max_part_size` in `FormParser`", cryptography's "Updated ... wheels to be compiled with OpenSSL 4.0.1") without saying anything was wrong; data/flaw_judgements.csv quotes each. Five releases (litellm's five), two (mcp's) and one (langchain's) have no notes in the changelog, the description or the tag. By library the classes are mixed in two: gitpython has nine announced fixes and two silent ones (3.1.48, 3.1.50), cryptography one announced (50.0.0) and two silent (48.0.1, 49.0.0). Every announced entry's deciding text is dated before the release's first upload, by between under a minute and 9.7 hours (the tag's commit or annotation; data/fix_classes.csv, hours_after_upload). Eleven of the 19 announced entries name GHSA identifiers, four of them among the events (urllib3, pyjwt, pyasn1, msgpack), before GitHub published the advisories, 1.2 to 25 days later; the notes pointed at advisories nobody outside the project could yet read. The one change found between the entry at the tag and at the head is pillow 12.2.0's, whose headings gained their CVE numbers after the release; the words at the tag already said "Security" and "vulnerable to GZIP decompression bombs".

## Method

The brief (brief.md) was written before any note, tag, dependents list or repository was read, with the classification rule, the library rule (all sixteen of LH008's events), the frame, pin and move rules (LH008's), the measures and the ceiling. classify.py read the three sources for each fixed release and flagged security words; the "flaw named" judgement was made by this agent against each advisory's summary and written to data/flaw_judgements.csv with the words; classes.py combined them with the dates. Amendment 1 records the classes and two changes to how the rule was carried out (a tag form for mako; what "flaw named" was taken to mean), before any dependent was read. Then, in order: osv_ranges.py (the twelve advisories' ranges and release lists), collect.py frame, screen and moves (LH008's, with the kept rule read from the OSV ranges), trailers.py, analyse.py (lags, summaries by event and class, rates, authorship; it reads LH008's four fix frames from studies/LH008/data), summarise_reads.py. Every table in data/ is written by them; classes.py and analyse.py need no network, trailers.py needs the clones in /tmp/claude-0/lh009-work, which are not kept. analyse.py reproduces LH008's two-day counts for its four frames (8 moves over 194.4 repository-days after the release; 8 over 156.8 after the advisory).

Choices made where the brief left room: the 24-hour allowance for a tag's time was applied to every dated source; an entry was compared at the tag and at the head, and the file's history between them was read only where they differed; LH008's "shortest tag" fallback picked litellm's nightly and release-candidate tags (for example `v1.83.0-nightly`), which changes nothing since litellm has no changelog at any tag or at the head; the rates are pooled over pairs, and each class's rate leaving out one event at a time is given as a check on one library carrying a class.

## Limits

- **Class is library.** Each library gives one release, so every difference between the classes is also a difference between seven libraries and seven others: their users, their bots, how often their dependents update. gitpython's dependents moved fastest of all (median 5.3 days) and carry 8 of the announced class's 29 moves before the advisory; pyjwt's 25-day gap carries 10. Leaving out any one event keeps the announced class ahead, but not by much when gitpython is left out (1.84 against 1.13 to 1.68).
- **Small counts.** The days-two-to-seven difference is 13 moves against 5; three of the 13 are one organisation's repositories within an hour. No significance test was run, as the brief says; rates on counts this small carry Poisson noise of their own size.
- **Silent in the sources read.** GitHub's release pages were refused (HTTP 403, every one), and three silent events (litellm, mcp, langchain) have no notes in the changelog, the description or the tag, so they may have announced the fix on GitHub or elsewhere. Security mailing lists, blogs and social media were not read. litellm's repository held a release-notes page for v1.83.0 for three weeks after the release, not at the tag or the head; its security words concern the March supply-chain incident.
- **Judgement.** "Flaw named" is this agent's judgement; it decides one event (mako), and the strict class without it gives the same pattern. Nine silent entries name the fix's change without the flaw; a reader who counts them as announced would move five silent events (cryptography, starlette, aiohttp, python-multipart and soupsieve) into the announced class, leaving litellm and mcp as the only silent events with dependents; the direction then holds on too few moves to mean anything (from the release to the advisory, 2.02 per 100 repository-days on 40 moves against 1.22 on 6; data/hazard_by_class.csv, grouping "near misses counted as announced").
- **The gap.** The announced advisories came sooner after their releases (median 3.9 days against 10.6), so windows long after the release are filled mostly by silent events. The time-matched windows (0 to 2, 2 to 7, 7 to 30 days from the release) are the fair comparison; the share of moves before the advisory is not.
- **Frames.** Open Source Insights' list for the release before the fix is a frame of pinners of that release, as in LH008; where the release before came days before the fix, it is nearly empty (aiohttp, msgpack, langchain). 69 of 332 new packages named no repository; 27 clones were refused.
- **Two readings a little over an hour apart.** LH008's four frames were cloned at 07:24 to 07:26 UTC and LH009's twelve at 08:49 to 08:51 UTC; each pair's end of observation is its own clone time.
- **When an entry was written.** An entry read at the tag is dated at the tag's commit; git does not record when a commit or tag was pushed, so an entry could have become public later than its date, and the changelog file's history between the tag and the head was read only by comparing the two ends.
- **Authorship from git alone**, with LH008's limits: a squash merge of a bot's pull request counts as the bot's; pull-request bodies and labels, where Dependabot marks a security update, were not read.
- **A move is a request.** Nothing here says what was installed or run, or whether any project was exposed.

## Next

Reading GitHub's release pages from an address that is served them would settle whether litellm's, mcp's and langchain's fixes were silent or announced somewhere this rule could not see. The comparison would be cleaner within a library: gitpython published nine announced and two silent fixes between April and August, and cryptography one announced and two silent, so the dependents of one library could be followed through both kinds of release. And whether the people who moved in the week after an announced release had read its notes is a question for their pull requests, which need GitHub's API.
