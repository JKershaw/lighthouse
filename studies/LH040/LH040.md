**In short.** We asked how often PyPI's public download logs, as ClickPy holds them, recorded the poisoned LiteLLM 1.107.0 (malicious by its maintainers' and several security firms' accounts) being fetched on 21 October 2026, when it was up for about an hour and three quarters by its maintainers' times, and in the month after. They record 49,043 fetches that day, including any after it was pulled; per hour as the newest release, a rule fixed in advance calls that ordinary against 17 LiteLLM releases of the same weeks, but under the maintainers' end time it ranks 17th of 18 and the rule's band is wide (0.59 to 1.39 times the median), so our reading, made after the counts, is no faster than usual and probably at the low end. After it was pulled, the logs record 1,502 fetches on the 28 of the next 30 days that have a record, nearly all with no installer name or a browser's: 33 by uv, none by pip. A download is a fetch, not an install or an infection; the record has had an evidence review, a reader-and-inference review and a recheck, and no piece is planned.

## Answer

Evidence review cleared; reader-and-inference review done and resolved here; recheck cleared this version. PyPI's download logs, as ClickPy held them on 23 November, record 49,043 fetches of litellm 1.107.0 on its upload day, 21 October 2026 (observation), which is 1.42 per cent of litellm's 3,465,646 fetches that day (derived). ClickPy held exactly the same day-0 figures on 9 November: no cell both reads held, on any day to 8 November, changed between them (derived). Per hour as the newest release, 1.107.0's index lies inside the range of all 17 comparison releases under each of the three reported window ends, so the brief's pre-set reading applies: it "travelled as an ordinary newest release did in its first hours" (derived, by the rule fixed before any count was read). The range is wide: it admits any index from 0.59 to 1.39 times the comparison median. Within it 1.107.0 sits low under the maintainers' end of 13:23 UTC (17th of 18, second lowest, at 0.68 times the comparison median, above only 1.108.1) and under Datadog's 13:20 (17th, 0.70 times), and near the middle only under Aikido's 12:47 (8th of 18, 1.04 times). Read with those numbers (interpretation, post hoc: made after the counts were seen, and not part of the brief), the pre-set "ordinary" means no faster than an ordinary newest release and, under the two later ends, probably at the low end; it does not mean it spread just like one. The rule can't tell ordinary uptake from uptake held down by up to about 40 per cent (a release at the median could lose 41 per cent of its index and still read as ordinary), whether by time of day, by caches slow to see a new release, or by something in the path treating it differently; the record describes the low position and doesn't explain it.

After it was pulled, the logs record fetches of 1.107.0 on 28 of the 30 days from 22 October to 20 November (day 30), 1,502 in all (observation); for the other two days, 16 and 19 November, ClickPy holds no row for 1.107.0, which the brief classes as no record, not zero, so the 30-day figure is a sum over the days with a record. To the last day held, 22 November, the sum is 1,541. Of the 1,502, most carry no installer name (954) or the name "Browser" (489), together 96.1 per cent; 33 were made by uv, 16 by requests, 9 by Nexus and 1 by devpi, and none by pip (observation). Whether any of these requests was served a file isn't in the logs. ClickPy's rows for the March 2026 versions, 120,633 fetches from 24 March to 30 April, are within a factor of two of PyPI's own "over 119k" (R5), so the brief's calibration holds (derived), and ClickPy's daily totals agree with pypistats.org's within 5 per cent on every day the brief checks (derived).

A download is one fetch of one file. It is not an install, a running copy, a victim or an infection, and nothing here says any system fetched, ran or was compromised by 1.107.0. That the release was malicious is the account of LiteLLM's maintainers (R1, R2), Cloudsmith (R6), Aikido (R7), Datadog (R8), StepSecurity (R9) and OSV's record (L7); this study quotes it and doesn't test it. StepSecurity's count of "about 49,000" (R9) is theirs, unchecked, and set beside ours below rather than merged with it. deps.dev, the brief's one view of what depends on the release, refused automated readers in its robots.txt at both reads, so that view is missing, not empty.

## Limits

- A download is a fetch. It counts machines that fetch often more than once, counts mirrors and caches that fetch for others, and misses copies installed from caches, mirrors or container images (including LiteLLM's Docker images, R1). Nothing here measures installs, running copies, victims, credentials taken or who fetched.
- ClickPy keeps the day, not the time: no fetch is placed inside W, and the per-hour index rests on an even-spread assumption that is false in its details.
- One release is one case; nothing generalises to other compromised packages.
