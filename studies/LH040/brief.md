# LH040: How far did the poisoned LiteLLM 1.107.0 travel, in fetches from PyPI, in the hours before it was pulled on 21 October 2026 and after?

**In short** (added 8 November 2026; not part of the protocol). On 21 October 2026 a release of LiteLLM, a popular Python library that sits between applications and AI model providers, was uploaded to PyPI with a credential stealer inside, as its maintainers and several security firms report, and was pulled after about an hour and three quarters. We'll count how often PyPI's public download logs recorded it being fetched that day and afterwards, and set that beside ordinary LiteLLM releases of the same weeks and the version it displaced. One security firm has already put the count at about 49,000, which we have read but not checked. A download is a fetch, not an install or an infection. Reads from 9 November; the record by 4 December.

**Protocol 0.1, fixed 8 November 2026, before this study read any download count for litellm, and before any count for litellm from 12 October 2026 on was read by any Lighthouse study, as far as the writer knows.** Writer: a research subagent in Claude Code (Claude Opus 5.5) for the driving session, from proposals.md P-0015, which the horizon gate started on 7 November 2026 under conditions that bind this brief (notes/horizon/2026-11-07T0853Z.md, Gate). Investigation: [Where software updates go](../../investigations/software-updates.md). The event's own records were read first, on 8 November between 14:14 and 14:16 UTC, and are kept raw under [data/](data/) with their [collector](scripts/collect_prebrief.py) and [sources](sources.md); Lighthouse measured no count from them, and the counts some of them state are listed under "What is already known of the outcome". The outcome [collector](scripts/collect.py) is committed with this brief and has not been run.

## Question

LiteLLM 1.107.0 was on PyPI for about 1.7 hours on Wednesday 21 October 2026, by the reports below, and every one of its wheels carried, by those reports, a credential stealer and a remote code loader that run whenever Python starts. From public data only: how many fetches of 1.107.0 did PyPI's download logs record, as ClickPy holds them, on 21 October and on each later day; what share of all litellm fetches that day was that, and how does it compare, per hour it was the newest release, with the share ordinary newest litellm releases took on their first day in October and November 2026; which installers, systems and file types made those fetches, beside the same for its neighbours; and do the logs record fetches of 1.107.0 after it was pulled? The unit is the fetch, counted by UTC day, and the comparison unit is the release. One release is a case: nothing here generalises to other compromised packages.

What a count counts: a download is one fetch of one file from PyPI's CDN, logged with the installer's self-reported fields. It is not an install, a running copy, a victim or an infection. It counts machines that fetch often more than once, counts mirrors and caches that fetch on others' behalf, and misses copies installed from caches, mirrors or container images. This study says so wherever a count appears, and makes no claim, in Lighthouse's voice, that any system fetched, ran or was compromised by 1.107.0.

## The event, as the records give it

Each statement is labelled with its source (ids from [sources.md](sources.md)) and is as reported unless it says otherwise.

- **Registry today** (observation, L1 to L5, read 8 November 14:14 UTC). PyPI's JSON API answers 404 for 1.107.0, its project JSON lists 1.107.0.dev1 to dev3 and rc1 to rc3 but no 1.107.0, and its simple index lists no 1.107.0 file. PyPI's documentation says a quarantined release is excluded from the index APIs (L10), so these reads cannot tell quarantine from deletion, and PyPI's records no longer give 1.107.0's upload time or the time it was pulled.
- **Upload.** The OSV record MAL-2026-2144 (L7, modified 2026-10-29T14:30:33Z) says 1.107.0 was "uploaded to PyPI on 2026-10-21 between 11:39 and 11:40 UTC". LiteLLM's own reports (R1, R2) and StepSecurity, citing them (R9), give the seven wheels' uploads as 11:39:51 to 11:40:13 UTC. Cloudsmith gives 11:39 UTC (R6). The upload is taken as **11:39:51 UTC** (the maintainers' report, within OSV's minute). No PyPI record of it was readable.
- **Pulled.** No registry or OSV record gives the time. As reported: by 13:23 UTC, by LiteLLM (R1: "live from 11:39:51 to 13:23 UTC"; R2: "frozen by ... 13:23 UTC", "PyPI quarantines" by 13:23) and StepSecurity citing LiteLLM (R9); 13:20 UTC by Datadog (R8, "PyPI quarantines affected version 1.107.0"); Aikido's page says PyPI "quarantined and removed" it and gives an install window ending 12:47 UTC, without saying what 12:47 marks (R7). PyPI has published nothing about it that was found (R10, R11). The gate's "about 13:20 UTC" from a search summary is borne out by Datadog's page, as reported. The study's primary edge is **13:23 UTC** (the maintainers' report), with 13:20 and 12:47 carried as alternatives wherever a measure depends on the edge.
- **The newest release.** From L1's upload times, 1.106.2 (10:44:22 UTC that day) was the newest final release before 1.107.0, and no final release higher than 1.106.2 was uploaded until 1.106.3 on 24 October at 09:14:52 UTC (derived from L1). So during its window 1.107.0 was the highest final release, the one an unconstrained installer would choose, and after it was pulled 1.106.2 was again.
- **What the payload was said to do** (all reported, not Lighthouse's finding). OSV's record (L7): three extra files in every wheel (`cook.pth`, `cook.py`, `sitecustomize.py`) that run at every interpreter start "without importing litellm"; a stealer that collects cloud, SSH, CI, Kubernetes, browser and AI developer tool credentials and posts them, encrypted, to a lookalike domain; and an hourly loader that fetches and runs remote code. Aikido (R7) calls them an infostealer and a remote access trojan; Cloudsmith (R6) and Datadog (R8) describe the same files; Cloudsmith says "active abuse was observed within hours" and StepSecurity (R9) that stolen AWS credentials were used between 16:20 and 17:01 UTC. The record's only 1.107.0 origin, Amazon Inspector's report as kept in the record, says in its own text that no malicious code path was found; the record's consolidated text says the opposite. That the release was malicious is LiteLLM's, Cloudsmith's, Aikido's, Datadog's, StepSecurity's and OSV's account, and PyPI's action as they report it; the study quotes it and does not test it.
- **How it got there** (reported, not used). Cloudsmith: stolen GitHub CLI OAuth tokens. LiteLLM (R2) and StepSecurity (R9): a leaked, unrevoked internal key that returned an organisation owner's token, then stolen maintainer tokens that ran the release workflow from a lookalike repository. The study claims no propagation and no link to any worm or campaign.
- **"The second compromise of 2026"** (checked, with a limit). OSV holds 85 records for litellm (L9), of which three concern malicious code: MAL-2026-2144, covering 1.82.7, 1.82.8 and 1.107.0, and PYSEC-2026-2 and GHSA-5mg7-485q-xm76, both about 1.82.7 and 1.82.8, published 24 and 25 March 2026. PyPI's incident report of 2 April 2026 (R5) describes the March versions. So in OSV's records, 1.107.0 is the second episode of malicious litellm releases in 2026, as Cloudsmith and Datadog say. An episode OSV does not record cannot be ruled out, and the brief says "the second in OSV's records", not "the second".

## Measures

1. **Day-0 count**, C0: fetches of 1.107.0 on 21 October, in all and by file type (wheel, source distribution); and its share S0 of all litellm fetches that day (ClickPy's daily total). Set beside StepSecurity's "about 49,000" as a difference, without judging which is right.
2. **Fetches after it was pulled:** fetches of 1.107.0 on each day from 22 October to the last day held, and their sum, by installer and file type. A fetch logged after 21 October is a fetch PyPI's logs recorded of a file that was no longer listed; by what route (a pinned URL, a cache, a re-run) is not observable here.
3. **Per-hour share index**, I(R) = S0(R) / (h(R)/24), for 1.107.0 and each comparison release, where S0(R) is R's share of litellm's fetches on R's day 0. Reported: every I(R), 1.107.0's rank among the 18, and its ratio to the comparison releases' median. **The pre-set reading:** 1.107.0 "travelled as an ordinary newest release did in its first hours" if its I lies within the range of the 17 comparison releases' I under all three window ends; "faster" or "slower" if outside the range under all three; "depends on the window's end" otherwise. The index assumes fetches spread evenly over each day, which they do not (W fell in the early afternoon in central Europe and the early morning on the American east coast); it is a description, and no hourly data can test it from public sources without an account.
4. **The displaced version:** 1.106.2's daily fetches on 20 to 23 October, and 1.106.1's, described beside 1.107.0's; no rate is attributed to the removal.
5. **Who fetched:** the shares of 1.107.0's day-0 fetches by installer (pip, uv, poetry, pdm, bandersnatch and other mirror tools named by ClickPy, other names, none), by system, and the CI share under LH011's rule, each beside the median and range of the comparison releases' day-0 shares. A mirror's fetch is a copy for others; a CI fetch is a build, not a deployment.
6. **Calibration on March:** ClickPy's fetches of 1.82.7 and 1.82.8 on 24 March 2026 and each later day held, set beside PyPI's "over 119k" (R5). If ClickPy holds roughly what PyPI reported, that supports reading ClickPy's 1.107.0 rows as PyPI's own logs of a pulled release; if it holds none or far fewer, see "Missing evidence". "Roughly" is fixed as within a factor of two, a loose test because PyPI's interval is not stated.
7. **deps.dev:** whether it holds 1.107.0, and each dependents count read, reported as numbers with the read time.

## The comparison that matters

Measure 3: 1.107.0's per-hour share of litellm's fetches on its day 0 against the 17 ordinary newest releases of the same weeks, with the unit the release. The question it answers is whether, in its 1.7 hours, the poisoned release was fetched the way any new litellm release is, which is what one would expect if nothing in the path from upload to installer told them apart before it was pulled. Measures 1, 2, 5 and 6 describe the case; none is a test. A difference in rate is a description; a cause needs its own evidence, which this study does not collect.

## Missing evidence

- A read that errs, times out, returns a ClickHouse exception, a refusal or a challenge is **unavailable** for that read; one retry is allowed per query within the read and logged.
- A day is a **gap** if ClickPy holds no litellm total for it, or the total is below half the median of the seven days around it (LH011's rule); every measure on a gap day is unknown.
- On a day that is not a gap, no row for a comparison release is zero fetches logged. For **1.107.0 and the March versions, no row is "no record", not zero**, because whether PyPI's logs or ClickPy keep rows for a release that was pulled is exactly what is not known; measure 6 is the check. If ClickPy holds no 1.107.0 row on 21 October, measures 1 to 5 for 1.107.0 are unknown and the study says so; StepSecurity's figure then stands as reported and unchecked.
- A deps.dev 404 is **no record**, not "no dependents".
- Unknown is never no.

## Resource ceiling

Two reads. No account, key, credential, BigQuery or paid service; no contact with anyone. Model spend: about $15 at list rates for both reads, tabulation and the record, before reviews.

## What would leave it inconclusive

- ClickPy holds no 1.107.0 row (measures 1 to 5 unknown), or 21 October is a gap day.
- Measure 6 fails (ClickPy's March rows are absent or outside a factor of two of PyPI's figure): the 1.107.0 counts are then reported as ClickPy's, with that disagreement, and not read as PyPI's full record.
- ClickPy's daily totals differ from pypistats's "with mirrors" by more than five per cent on more than a fifth of the days 14 October to 4 November (LH011's rule): counts reported as ClickPy's, with the disagreement.
- Fewer than ten comparison releases have a known day-0 share: measure 3 is reported as a list, without the pre-set reading.

It cannot say, in any case, how many machines installed or ran 1.107.0, who did, whether any credential was taken, how many copies came from mirrors, caches or container images after the fetch, or which repositories or lockfiles held the version. Those would need data that are private, or a repository census that the gate's aggregate-only condition would bind; a later study could take a census of lockfiles in aggregate, naming none.
