# Pinned software moved to security fixes over weeks, a little faster after the release and again after the advisory, and neither drew most of the moves

Between 19:28 and 20:16 UTC on Monday 15 June 2026, security advisories rated high were published against three of the most downloaded libraries in Python: first pyjwt, which handles the signed tokens many websites use to keep people logged in, then cryptography, then starlette, a toolkit for building web services. None of them brought a new fix. Each named a version that was already out: cryptography's had been published almost six days before, starlette's 23 days before and pyjwt's 25. They were not alone that evening: GitHub published ten advisories against the three libraries between 17:28 and 20:40, most rated lower, one more against starlette rated high and fixed in a later release, and every one of the ten named a fix that was already out. We followed the three. Of the projects we were following that pinned one of the three libraries, five moved to its fix in the two days after that evening. Most of the rest had moved already, took weeks, or had not moved by late September.

We had expected more. In the one library we had followed this way before, a similar evening of advisories was followed within 28 hours by moves in six of the thirteen pinned projects whose move we found. So this time we took four widely used libraries and 102 pins held by 88 projects, and set every move to a fix against two moments: when the fix was released and when its advisory was published. The pins moved after both. The two days after the release and the week after the advisory each brought a short spell of quicker moves, at about the same pace, and neither drew most of them. The typical move came about nineteen days after the release, at an ordinary slow pace, and about half were written by a bot or merged from one.

*27 September 2026 · Lighthouse*

## Two moments to move

Software that uses a library says which versions of it will do. It can give a range and take each new release as it comes, or it can hold still: name one exact version, which is called pinning it, or keep a lockfile, a file where a project writes down the exact versions it has settled on. A pin stays where it is until someone, or something, moves it.

A security advisory is a public notice that some versions of a library have a flaw, with the version that fixes it. GitHub keeps a database of them, and OSV, an open database, gathers them with others. What we had not expected is how reliably the fix comes first. Of the 200 most downloaded Python projects in August 2026, 16 had advisories from GitHub's database, rated high or critical, published between late March and late August with a fixed version: 68 advisories in all. In every one of the 68, the fixed version had been published on the Python Package Index, where Python's libraries are kept, on an earlier day than the advisory. So a project that pins a library has two moments at which it might move to a fix: when the fix is released, and when the advisory says why it matters.

[Last time](what-moves-a-pin.md), in one library, the second moment looked like the one that counted. We ended by asking whether pinned software in general moves on advisories rather than on releases.

## Four libraries, chosen by a rule

We took the four most downloaded of those 16: urllib3, which much Python software uses to talk to web servers, sixth by downloads in August; cryptography, tenth; pyjwt, 45th; and starlette, 46th. urllib3's fix came out on 7 May and its advisory four days later. cryptography's came out on 9 June and its advisory six days later, on the evening of 15 June. pyjwt's and starlette's came out on 21 and 23 May, and their advisories on that same evening, more than three weeks later.

For each library, a public index called Open Source Insights lists the packages that depend on it, by version. We took its lists for the version just before each fix, put them in an order set by a scrambled form of each name, which nobody could steer, and screened up to 60 from each list. We kept those whose public repository, on the day the fix came out, pinned or locked a version below it: 102 pairs of a repository and a library, from 88 repositories. Then we read each repository's history forward, to the first change that took the library to the fix or later, up to 27 September. To see what a fix adds, we did the same for an earlier release of each library that no advisory named: 91 more pairs.

## Two short spells, and a long slow middle

By 27 September, 73 of the 102 had moved to the fix, 28 had not, and one had stopped pinning the library. The typical move, the median, came 18.7 days after the fix was released, and half of them fell between one and five weeks after it. Of the 73, 19 came before the advisory and 54 after it. For pyjwt and starlette, whose advisories came weeks after their fixes, 13 of the 30 moves came before the advisory.

To compare the two moments fairly, we counted how many of the projects still waiting moved on each day. In the two days after a fix was released, about four in every hundred waiting projects moved each day (4.1). From the third day to the advisory it was about one and a half (1.4), the pace of the quiet weeks. In the two days after the advisory it was about five (5.1), and a little over four (4.3) in the five days after that; in the three weeks after the advisory's first week, 1.7. So each moment was followed by a spell of quicker moves: two days for the release, about a week for the advisory. Both spells rest on small counts: eight moves in the two days after the release and eight in the two days after the advisory. At counts that small, the difference between four and five a day is noise.

There was no burst. Four of the 102 moved within a day after the advisory. pyjwt's and starlette's advisories, like the ones we saw before, came weeks after their fixes, and in the day after them one pinned project moved.

## A release nobody warned about

The earlier releases, the ones no advisory named, moved pins more slowly. Within 30 days, 21 of the 91 pairs had moved to them, 23 per cent, against 50 of the 102 for the fixes, 49 per cent. From the end of their first week to the thirtieth day, they moved at 0.6 in every hundred waiting projects a day.

Part of that gap is quiet projects rather than waiting ones: 28 of the 43 repositories that had not moved to those releases by 27 September made no commit to their main branch more than 30 days after the release. And the comparison is not clean. Of the 45 moves to those earlier releases, 21 came after the next advisory against the library, and for cryptography, whose next advisory came 13 days after its comparison release, 14 of its 20.

So a fix, with its advisory, moved about twice as many pins in its first month as a release no advisory named. We cannot say what did it. A release can say in its own notes that it fixes a security flaw before any advisory is published, and we did not read the release notes, so a move "before the advisory" is not a move before anyone knew. For pyjwt, the US National Vulnerability Database published records of the flaw on 28 May, a week after the fix and 18 days before GitHub's advisory. Measured from those, 5 of its 25 moves came before and none in the two days after.

## Who moved them

Of the 73 moves to a fix, 22 were written by a bot, a program that proposes updates to a project's dependencies: 17 by Dependabot, GitHub's own, and 5 by Renovate, another. Another 16 were a person merging a branch whose last change a bot had made, 13 of them Dependabot's, though two of the 16 were a project's own release or sync branches rather than an update a bot proposed. The other 35 were a person's. So 38 of the 73, a little over half, were a bot's work or merged from it. For the releases no advisory named, it was 19 of 45.

In the two days after the advisory, three moves were a bot's commits, two were merges of a bot's branch and three were a person's. The bots' messages in those days say only that they bump or update the library from one version to another, in the same form as their moves at other times, so they are silent on whether a move was a security update. Two of the three people's changes in those days cite an advisory, or "dependabot security alerts". Three of urllib3's moves, two Dependabot commits and one merge of its branch, reached their projects 16 to 23 hours after its advisory. One of the three was written by Dependabot at three minutes past midnight on the Monday, fifteen hours before the advisory, and merged the next day, so it was already open when the advisory came, whatever moved the person to merge it. Of the other two, one was written two hours after the advisory, and the third was squashed on merging, which leaves no record of when the bot proposed it. Those two fit an update Dependabot opens when an advisory is published, and they fit a weekly schedule of updates equally well; the pull requests would say which, and we did not read them.

## What this does to what we said before

Last time, in one library, we wrote that if the pattern held more widely, a release would reach pinned software mostly when there was a security reason to take it. Across these four libraries it did not hold in that form. The advisory brought about a week of quicker moves, no bigger than the release's two days, and most moves came at neither moment. We think the burst we saw after that library's advisories belonged to that library, or to that evening, and was not the rule. What survives is smaller: a fix, with its advisory, moved about twice as many pins in its first month as a release no advisory named, and about half of those moves were a bot's work.

## Where the reading runs thin

These are four libraries with one advisory each, and three of the four advisories came on one evening, so that evening weighs heavily. The projects come from what Open Source Insights lists, at most 100 dependents for each version, and a quarter of the packages we screened named no repository at all. The pairs are not independent: 102 of them come from 88 repositories, and a project that updates several libraries in one change counts once for each. We read authorship from the history alone, so a person's merge of a bot's change can look like a bot's, and the times are when a change was committed, not when it reached the public repository.

And a move is a request. It changes what the project's next build or installation would take; it says nothing about what was installed or run. We did not check whether any project used the parts of these libraries the advisories concern, and nothing here says that any project was exposed.

## The next question

How much of a fix's early uptake comes because the release itself says it is a security fix? Reading the release notes and changelogs of these fixes, and a few more, for security language, and setting the moves before each advisory against them, would tell whether "release or advisory" is really "announced or unannounced". Reading Dependabot's pull requests, where they are public, would separate its security updates from its routine ones, which the history alone cannot.
