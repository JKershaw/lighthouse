# Pinned software leaned towards security fixes that said so before the advisory, but across thirty libraries the lean is too uncertain to call

On 10 March 2026, tornado, a Python library for building web servers, released version 6.5.5 with notes that opened under the heading "Security fixes" and named three flaws. GitHub published advisories for two of them within two days, and for the third 23 days later, by which time five of the six projects we followed that pinned tornado had moved to the fix, one of them before any advisory. Nine days before tornado's release, python-dotenv, a small library that reads settings from a file, had released 1.2.2. Its notes said that two of its functions "used to follow symlinks in some situations", with no word about security. The advisory, about overwriting files through such links, came seven weeks later, and two of the eight pinned projects we followed had moved before it.

Counted as our rule counts them, to the first advisory in our window, those two tell the story [our previous piece](fixes-that-said-so.md) suggested: that software pinned to a library moves sooner to a security fix when the fix says so in its own notes. We had found that across fourteen libraries, one fix each, at about 1.7 times the pace before the advisory, and warned that two libraries carried much of it. So this time we counted many more libraries, each once. Counted that way, we could not tell the libraries whose fix said so from those whose fix did not by how fast their pinned software moved before the advisory; added up across every project, the fixes that said so still came out ahead, by a margin too uncertain to settle the question. This piece revises what the last one suggested.

*27 September 2026 · Lighthouse*

## Casting the net wider

Software that uses a library can pin one exact version of it, or keep a lockfile, a file where a project writes down the exact versions it has settled on; either way it holds still until someone, or something, moves it. A security advisory is a public notice that some versions of a library have a flaw, with the version that fixes it. Last time we took only advisories rated high or critical, against the 200 most downloaded Python projects. This time we took every advisory GitHub had reviewed, of any severity, published between 27 March and 27 August 2026 against the 500 most downloaded, where the fix was out at least a day before the advisory. There were 215 such advisories, against 43 libraries. For each library we took one fix, the one whose advisory came first, and sorted it by the same rule as last time, fixed before we read a note: *announced* if its changelog, tag message or package description said at the release that it mended a security flaw, and *silent* otherwise. That gave 22 announced and 21 silent.

Then we followed the software that pins them, as before. A public index called Open Source Insights lists, for each version of a library, the packages that depend on it. For each fix we took up to 20 packages from its list for the version just before, in an order nobody could steer, kept those whose public repository pinned or locked an affected version on the day the fix came out, and read each one's history forward to its first change to the fix, up to 27 September. Thirteen libraries gave us nothing to follow, because the index listed few or no dependents against the version before their fix; in twelve of them that version had come out 16 days or less before the fix. That left 30 libraries, 18 announced and 12 silent, and 209 pairs of a repository and a library. By 27 September, 142 of those pairs had moved.

We had hoped for twenty libraries of each kind with pinned software to follow. The 500 most downloaded projects and five months of advisories did not hold them.

## One library, one count

As last time, the fair measure is the pace: of the projects still waiting, how many moved each day, counted from the fix's release until its advisory. We worked that out for each library on its own, so that a library counts once, however many projects pin it.

The typical library whose fix said so, the median of the 18, saw about 3.2 of every hundred waiting projects move each day before the advisory. The typical silent library, of the 12, saw about 2.7. That gap is small, and it is the kind of gap chance gives. If we shuffle the two labels at random among the 30 libraries, 10,000 times over, a gap at least as large turns up in 89 shuffles in every hundred.

The week after the release, where our last piece found its difference, does not help the announced fixes either. From the third day to the seventh, stopping at the advisory where it came first, the typical announced library had no move at all: nine of the 16 with time in that window had none. The typical silent library, of 8, had about one and a half a day.

## Where the old lead came from

Pool every repository together instead, as our last piece did, and the announced fixes still come out ahead. From the release to the advisory, 35 moves over 1,522 days of waiting, added up across the waiting projects, gave 2.3 a day for every hundred projects to announced fixes, and 20 moves over 1,273 days gave 1.6 to silent ones: about one and a half times, against 1.7 last time. Resample the libraries themselves, to see how much that depends on which ones we happened to have, and the ratio could as well be anywhere from about half to about four times.

The pooled count and the library count differ because pooling lets a few libraries speak for many. The figure shows how. In both rows the big dots, where many projects waited a long time, sit at the slow end: nltk, whose advisory by our rule came five months after its fix, holds almost half the announced fixes' waiting days on its own. And the fastest libraries of both kinds are small ones, with few projects or little time before the advisory: tornado, gitpython, poetry and pypdf among the announced, pydantic-ai-slim, pip, awscli and fastmcp among the silent, several on between one and five moves. A pooled rate is at the mercy of which libraries happen to be large and which happen to be quick. It is not at the mercy of any one of them: leave any single library out and the announced fixes still lead when pooled, and leaving out the three quickest, tornado, gitpython and aiohttp, brings the lead down to 1.7 against 1.6.

The week after the release shows it most plainly. Pooled, announced fixes drew 3.3 moves a day for every hundred waiting in days three to seven, on 11 moves, against 2.3 on 5 for silent ones. Three of those 11 were to one fix, aiohttp's, a library for making and serving web requests, and the only security words in its notes were about an earlier release's fix. Count that fix as silent, and the pooled lead in that week turns the other way, 2.6 against 3.3.

Nor did a larger share of the moves come before the advisory: 35 of the 96 moves to announced fixes, 36 per cent, and 20 of the 46 to silent ones, 43 per cent.

## Where the reading runs thin

The test cannot see much. Thirty libraries is a small number, most with a handful of pinned projects and many with no move at all in a window. A shuffle that matches the data 89 times in a hundred says the typical libraries cannot be told apart here; it does not show that a modest real difference is absent.

How the notes are read changes the lean, and not always in one direction. If only fixes whose notes used a word such as security, vulnerability or CVE count as announced, so that the three counted announced only because they described the flaw itself become silent, the typical announced library moved faster, 3.7 against 0.8, though shuffling still matches that gap 28 times in a hundred. If fixes whose only security words were about an earlier release count as silent, which among these thirty moves aiohttp, the typical silent library moved faster, 4.2 against 3.2. Neither reading is far from chance, and they point opposite ways.

Our rule for the advisory has a gap as well, and it runs through the tornado we opened with. We took each fix's first advisory published in our window, from 27 March, and only where the fix had come out at least a day before. Three libraries had an earlier advisory naming the same fix that the rule could not see: tornado's two, within two days of its release; nltk's, a critical one published six days before its release, which is why nltk's pinned projects spent five months "before the advisory" by our count; and pip's, six days before its release. Take instead the earliest advisory naming the fix that GitHub had reviewed, among the records we kept, and nltk and pip have no time before it at all: the typical announced library then moved at about 3.3 of every hundred waiting projects a day against 1.3 for the silent, a gap that shuffling matches 47 times in a hundred, and pooled the announced fixes lead by about 3.4 to 1.4, a ratio whose resampled range starts at no difference. Nor was that advisory always the first public word. For 17 of the 43 fixes, the United States' national vulnerability database had published the flaw first, by up to 23 days among the libraries we followed; count to that date and the pooled lead widens again, while the typical libraries still do not part by more than shuffling gives. We did not look for earlier word still, in mailing lists, vendors' posts or the projects' own discussions. Pooled, the announced fixes lead under every reading we tried; the typical libraries lean one way or the other with the reading; and we chose the earlier clocks after seeing the results, so none of them settles the question.

Four libraries let us compare the two kinds inside one library, because both an announced and a silent fix had pinned software to follow. The announced fix drew moves faster in three of them, cryptography, aiohttp and gitpython, and slower in the fourth, pip, but each side rests on between none and four moves.

"Silent" means silent in what we read. Eight silent fixes had no notes about the release in any of the three places, and a ninth's were written two months after it; such projects often write their notes on GitHub's release pages, which refused us last time and which we did not ask for this time. Keep only the fixes whose notes we could read at the release, and the typical libraries still cannot be told apart, while the pooled lead widens a little, to 2.3 against 1.4. And a move is a request: it changes what a project's next build or installation would take, and says nothing about what was installed or run. Nothing here says that any project was exposed.

## What this does to what we said before

Our previous piece said that pinned software moved faster before the advisory to fixes whose notes said so, mostly in people's changes in the week after the release, and that with one fix for each library the notes could not be told apart from the library. Counting thirty libraries, each once, does not resolve that doubt. We could not tell the typical library whose fix said so from the typical library whose fix did not; what we reported before was a pooled count, and a pooled count follows the libraries that bring the most waiting projects. The pooled count still leans the announced way, by about one and a half times by our rule and by more when the earlier advisories are counted, and no single library's removal changes that; but resampling the libraries puts even the pooled lead within reach of no difference, and a test this small could miss a modest effect. So we do not say the notes make no difference, and we do not say they make one. We say that this sample cannot settle it: library by library we cannot find a difference, and pooled we cannot be sure of the one we see.

## The next question

The question needs more libraries than the 500 most downloaded projects hold in five months: a longer window of advisories, or a way of finding pinned software that does not depend on the version before a fix having dependents listed, since the libraries that release often are exactly the ones that way loses. GitHub's release pages, read from somewhere they are served, would say whether the nine silent fixes without notes we could read were silent, and a new count should settle, before it looks, which public notice it counts as the advisory. And whether the people who moved in the week after an announced release had read its notes is still a question for their pull requests.
