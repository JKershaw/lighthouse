# A month after two fixes, both counts had done most of their rising within two weeks, and more than half the PHP services showing a version ran branches PHP no longer fixes

On 21 October 2026, two pieces of software that much of the web runs on released security fixes on the same day. PHP, which runs behind a large share of websites, put out an emergency release for two flaws serious enough that British and European Union security agencies advised isolating exposed systems that couldn't be patched promptly. Node.js, which runs JavaScript on servers and in the tools that build software, put out a security release for ten flaws. A week later we wrote down how far each had spread. A month later, the more interesting thing was how little the shares moved after the second week, and what moved them when they did.

*30 October 2026 · revised 25 November 2026 · Lighthouse*

## Two kinds of count

A fix spreads only as far as people install it, and nobody can watch every computer. What we can read are two kinds of public count.

For Node.js, the project publishes how many times each exact version was downloaded from its own website each day. That's a count of requests: each time a build server, a test job or a developer's laptop asks for a copy. A machine that builds often is counted often, and a machine set up years ago that never asks again isn't counted at all. Copies taken from a Linux distribution, a container image or a company's own mirror never reach this count either. It's the same kind of count we used in September for Python libraries, whose downloads the Python Package Index records.

For PHP, nothing like that exists for the servers that run it. What we have is a count from outside. Censys, a company that scans the internet's addresses, publishes a daily tally of the internet-facing services that answer with a PHP version, and how many in each branch it counts as patched. (A branch is one of the parallel release lines a project keeps, such as PHP 8.3 and 8.4, each with its own fix; a service is an address and a port, not a machine or a website.) Censys says its counts can lag by up to about a week for some websites, and that Linux distributions often fix PHP without changing its version number. Ubuntu's fixed package for its 24.04 release still reports itself as 8.3.6. So Censys's patched share is a floor: the real share protected is at least that, and we don't know by how much more.

## A month on

*Downloads above, services seen from outside below: drawn apart because they count different things.*

Seen from outside, PHP's newer branches, 8.4 and 8.5, passed half in Censys's count two days after release, and the older ones, 8.2 and 8.3, six days after. The older two stayed below the newer two on every day from the first. Thirty days on, on 20 November, Censys counted 78.2 per cent of the services reporting a version in 8.2 as patched, 75.0 in 8.3, 86.5 in 8.4 and 92.2 in 8.5.

Nearly all of that came in the first two weeks. Between day 7 and day 14, every branch's patched count rose by between about 0.6 and 1.5 million services. After that, three of the four barely moved. From day 14 to day 30 the patched count rose by 55,421 services in 8.2, 46,896 in 8.3 and 19,881 in 8.5, while those branches lost between 81,585 and 429,445 services from the count. Their shares still went up a few points, but mostly because there were fewer services in the branch, not more patched ones. Only 8.4 kept adding patched services, 311,748 of them. Censys's counts can't say where the missing services went: offline, behind a hidden version, to another branch or out of its scans. And "patched" probably includes services first seen already on a fixed version, which we haven't checked with Censys.

What isn't counted patched in the older branches is mostly old. In 8.2 and 8.3, most of it sits five or more releases behind the fix. One release from June 2024, 8.3.8, alone held 11.1 per cent of the 8.3 services reporting a version, 844,547 of them. We don't know why. A distribution package that keeps reporting that number while carrying later fixes would make them protected but uncounted; a large base that hasn't moved would not. Neither was checked.

On Node.js's own server, all three fixed lines passed half two days after release, though line 22 slipped just under half again over the weekend that followed. Then something happened that changes how the month reads. On 11 November three newer versions appeared in the downloads, one in each line. Counting them as at or above the fix, as later releases in a line had been counted all along, the fixed release or newer was 65.5 per cent of line 22's downloads on 20 November, 74.3 per cent of line 24's and 96.3 per cent of line 26's. We chose that rule after we'd seen the counts from 11 November, the one counting rule the study added after seeing outcomes, and it assumes, without checking, that the new versions carry October's fix. Only a source that says whether the November releases carry October's fixes would settle that; none we read does, and nothing now scheduled will look. Left out, as the count was first written, the same day reads 39.8, 60.6 and 85.8 per cent. The gap is wide because by day 30 the new versions were 42.6, 34.8 and 74.2 per cent of each line's downloads.

The share under the rule as first written fell because, as the new versions arrived, downloads of the October releases fell far more than downloads of older versions. A daily count can't show whether the same machines switched. Under the rule we lead with, the shares were a few points higher in the last ten days than in the eight before. That's mostly more downloads at or above the fix, arriving with the new versions: on an average day, downloads at or above the fix were 18.4, 12.9 and 25.8 per cent higher in the later stretch in lines 22, 24 and 26, while downloads below it were only 4.0, 5.1 and 6.5 per cent lower. We worked this out after every count was in, as one of three ways the study split the days, all chosen then; collection has closed, so nothing will test it on fresh data. The real fall in old versions came earlier: in line 22, between 3.92 and 4.20 million downloads a day were below the fix on days 5 to 7, against 2.64 to 3.56 million on each weekday from day 12 to day 30. On 20 November, 27.7 of the 34.5 per cent of line 22's downloads still below the fix were five or more releases behind it.

After the study's record was written and reviewed, we read Node.js's own announcements of the three new versions once, on 25 November. Each is dated 11 November and says "This is a security release", for a new batch of flaws. So they're real releases, out the day they first appeared in the downloads. None of the three mentions October's release or the versions it fixed. A later security release in the same line would ordinarily carry the earlier fixes, but no page we read says so. That read hasn't yet been through the study's record or its reviews.

Node.js gives one more comparison on the same instrument. After its previous security release, in July, line 22's fixed release took 18 days to pass half of the line's downloads, and line 24's took four; this time both passed half in two. Fourteen days in, October's share was higher than July's in every line. Thirty days in, it was higher in every line under the rule we lead with and lower in every line under the rule as first written, so that comparison turns on the rule. We don't know why the releases differ.

## Beside Python

The four Python security fixes we followed in September had between 57.3 and 72.3 per cent of their libraries' downloads at the fixed release or newer a month after release. Node.js's lines, counted the same way from a different server, sat within or above that range on day 30 under the rule we lead with; under the rule as first written, line 22 sat below it.

PHP's shares started far below Python's and ended above them: on the day after release every PHP branch was below every Python fix, and on days 14 and 30 every branch was above. We think that crossing is what two such counts would be expected to show, a reading made after the counts were seen that the studies didn't test, and now can't, since collection has closed. A share of a day's downloads starts high, because a new release is fetched at once by whatever rebuilds. A share of standing services starts near zero and climbs as rescans find the new version. These are descriptions of shapes, not a race. A share of requests and a share of services are different quantities, the PHP shares are floors, and nothing here says which software's users are quicker, or why.

## What no fix reaches

The fixes only reach the branches that got one. PHP released fixes for 8.2 to 8.5; it no longer fixes 8.1 and older. On 20 November Censys counted 22.5 million services running those older branches, 51.8 per cent of all the PHP services that reported a version, down from 24.4 million a week after release. Some may be protected by a Linux distribution that still patches its own long-term releases under the old version number; we haven't measured how many.

Some, by one vendor's account, aren't. Google's bulletin for its Apigee products, published on 24 October, says that the Drupal 7 and 9 developer portals that customers run with Apigee Edge for Private Cloud "run PHP 7.3 or 7.4 on RHEL/CentOS 7, RHEL 8, or Amazon Linux 2, where no packaged fix is available", and advises taking them offline or restricting access and migrating. That's one vendor's own statement about one product, from a watch of vendors' statements that hasn't been reviewed yet. It doesn't say how many such portals face the internet. But it names a piece of the old layer for which, by the vendor's account, no packaged fix was available, as the bulletin still said when we last read it, on 23 November.

The download counts show the same layer, much thinner. In September 2026, 10.0 per cent of the installs logged by Packagist, the index from which PHP code fetches its libraries, came from PHP 8.1 or older. On Node.js's server, lines the October release doesn't fix took between 11.8 and 16.5 per cent of downloads on each day from a week before the release to a week after, and 11.5 per cent on 20 November. These are old versions still being fetched, not old machines still running: a count of requests can't see a server that stopped asking.

## What this cannot tell us

None of these counts is a measure of protection, and none says anything about a particular system. A service that hides its version isn't in Censys's patched shares at all: on 20 November that was 62.3 per cent of the PHP services it counted, after its count of services with no version more than doubled on 23 October, which looks like a change in how it detects PHP rather than in the internet. A download is not an install. A third count, W3Techs's survey of websites, put the fixed releases at 43.9 to 65.8 per cent within each PHP branch on 21 November, up from 2 to 8 per cent on 25 and 26 October. It visits a site about once a month, so its curve is a lagged view of the same weeks, and still climbing. It also counts websites, a different thing again.

On 30 October, after seeing the first week, we wrote down two predictions to check at a month. One holds as plainly as it was written: the oldest fixed Node.js line and the two older PHP branches were still behind the newer ones at the end of the month, line 22 by 8.0 points under the rule we lead with and 18.8 under the rule as first written, PHP's older branches by 8.3 points. The other, that more of PHP's month would come after the first week than of Node.js's, passed too, but it tells us little. A share of a day's downloads can settle as soon as what's being fetched changes; a share of standing services moves as they're rescanned, rebuilt or drop out of the count. The difference in what's counted could produce that result whatever happened, and neither side's late rise was plain uptake. Both predictions were readings we made after the first week was in, two comparisons under each of Node.js's two counting rules, and neither says why anything happened. With collection closed, no later count from these studies will test them further.

This is where the two studies stop collecting these counts. What they leave is a shape, not an explanation: in both counts, most of what moved, moved in the first fortnight, and the old layer underneath was still more than half of what PHP services announce.
