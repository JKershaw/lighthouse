# Who decides when a robot may use force? The sources we could read in November 2026 didn't say how it works in practice

In the departure hall of Changi Airport's Terminal 4, a police robot called GIBSON began a trial in July 2025, patrolling on its own and finding its way with laser scanners, depth cameras and sonar. Officers could call it to them with an app and ride it like a scooter. During its trial, according to HTX, the Singapore government agency that developed it with A*STAR researchers, some people tried to hop on and hitch a ride while it was driving itself. The engineers added sensors that stop it when someone does that, and put a cordon on it. When officers first take over by hand, they have to arm and activate the handlebar, "to prevent accidents from occurring".

That's a lot of detail about who's in charge of a police robot's movement. We went looking for the same kind of detail about force: when a robot carries a weapon, or might, who picks the target and who decides to fire? We read what public sources we could on three kinds of robot. For the robots we followed, none of the sources we could read says how that works in practice; the nearest is two reported statements of what one maker intends, which don't agree. And the two cases where it might have been said were the two we couldn't read.

*28 November 2026 · Lighthouse*

## Three kinds of robot

We chose three cases on purpose, not at random, so nothing here describes robots in general. The first is Phantom, a humanoid robot made by Foundation Future Industries, a Californian start-up, which reports say the company designed to carry weapons, and whose reported proposed use in Gaza we tried to follow: a search summary seen when this work was commissioned on 12 November, which we didn't keep, credited a report of preliminary Gaza discussions to the Financial Times, and we couldn't read that report. The second was meant to be an armed robot that isn't humanoid, as a comparison. The third is two kinds of police patrol robot at Changi, a civilian setting where nothing we read describes a weapon.

For each, we looked for the same seven things: whether it's armed, what AI is said to do, who picks targets, who decides to use force, how a person can step in, how far it has got (a proposal, a demonstration, a trial, real use) and any effect on the people around it. The Phantom case splits into three versions, since a robot can be configured differently for different jobs, and Changi into its two kinds of robot, which with the comparison gives six rows. We read the sources on 13 November 2026, and reread the four news reports on Phantom on 19 and 26 November; as far as we could check, their text hadn't changed (one site's rules meant we kept no copy of its page on 26 November, so that comparison can't be repeated).

## What the readable sources say

**The airport robots.** Singapore's police said in June 2023 that patrol robots had been deployed at Terminal 4 since that April, "capable of autonomous patrol", with a camera on an extendable mast and a two-way link so the airport police operations room can see what's happening and talk to the public. They can switch on what the police call "interim intervention measures (blinkers, siren, and speakers)" to keep people back or warn them before officers arrive. GIBSON, the ride-on robot, has been on trial in the same terminal since July 2025, the police said that month. None of the three government pages describes a weapon, and nothing we read reports either robot's status after those dates.

"Autonomous" here means the robot finds its own way around. That isn't the same as choosing what to do to people. The International Committee of the Red Cross, in a set of questions and answers dated 8 October 2026, puts it plainly: the test of an autonomous weapon "is not whether the weapon flies autonomously, but whether it has autonomy in the critical targeting functions: selecting and applying force to targets". With no weapon described, who would decide on force never comes up in what the Singapore sources say. We haven't treated that as proof that there's no weapon; it's simply not something these sources settle.

**The humanoid.** Everything we could read about Phantom is trade and tech reporting that passes on what the company says. Assembly magazine reported in December 2025 that it is designed to carry a payload of up to 44 pounds, "including lethal weaponry". Two reports from May and June 2026, The Next Web and eWeek, say the company sent two of its MK-1 robots to Ukraine "earlier this year", as The Next Web put it, to demonstrate fetching and carrying supplies, with Ukrainian officials and US government support. eWeek passes on the company's claim that this was "the first known use of humanoid robots in an active combat theater", and says the robots "remain far from battlefield-ready": they lack waterproofing and the battery life for extended deployments. The Next Web reports $24 million in US Army, Navy and Air Force research contracts for testing in inspection, logistics and weapons handling; we didn't read any award record.

On control, two reports don't agree. TechCentral wrote in December 2025, in an article credited to Business AM and in its own words, that "Phantoms will not have full autonomy when it comes to making decisions in combat". In May 2026 The Next Web reported that the company's chief executive said some armed uses would keep a person confirming decisions, but that in certain time-critical situations the robots would need to make fully autonomous decisions. Both are statements of intent, about a version of the robot that nothing we read shows in the field with a weapon. Five months apart, they may record a change of mind rather than a contradiction; we can't tell which.

What none of them says, in either case, is who would select a target or authorise force in practice, or how a person would step in. Nor, as we read them, does any source say which of these robots' functions, if any, use AI, as distinct from automated navigation. Repeating a company's claim across four outlets doesn't make it four independent sources, and the company's own website returned a "not found" error when we tried it.

## The two we couldn't read

The Financial Times report said to describe the Gaza discussions, and a Wired article on the company's weapons ambitions, which a search turned up when this work was commissioned, were both refused on 13 November by the sites' own rules for robots (their robots.txt files), under the rule we then followed for automated readers, which we obey. A single read of the Financial Times page on 28 November, under a narrower rule we now use for one-off reads, was refused by the site itself (HTTP 403); Wired's rules still refused us. Those two reads of 28 November were fact checks for a separate essay, made after we knew the first attempts had failed and kept outside the study's own reads ([their log](../notes/physical-authority/essay-checks/2026-11-28/fetchlog.md)). So we don't know what they say, and the Gaza use is neither established nor ruled out by anything we read.

The armed comparison fared no better. Our candidate was a US Marine Corps news page that a search listing described as a test of an armed robot; opening it returned a refusal (HTTP 403). We allowed ourselves one fixed search for a replacement, and none of its first ten results was a first-hand record of an armed ground-robot test, judged by their sites and titles.

Those two were the cases that could have tested the question. The readable cases are a humanoid whose weapons are, in what we read, a design aim, and police robots for which no source describes a weapon at all, so a source saying who decides on force in practice was hardly to be expected there. That means we can't tell whether public records stay quiet about control even when a weapon is in the field, or whether we just couldn't reach the records that would say. That's the main limit of everything here.

Counted across the grid above, 7 of its 45 entries are supported by a source, 23 aren't settled by anything we read, 14 couldn't be read, and 1 holds two reports that conflict. Those are our codings of what sources say, not counts of robots or weapons. Over the four readable rows, the stage a robot had reached is supported in all 4, and the questions about control in 1 of 13, and that one is GIBSON's takeover of its own movement, not force.

## What states agreed in Geneva

From 16 to 20 November, the states party to the UN's Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons met in Geneva for a review conference that the UN had said would "decide on the future work" on lethal autonomous weapons. Before and during it, several proposals asked for more than it agreed. Seven states (Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Ireland, New Zealand, Panama and Mexico) proposed that an expert group "negotiate a new Protocol", with requirements on human control, "recognizing that responsibility for decisions concerning the use of force cannot be transferred to machines". A paper from the Non-Aligned Movement and other states said weapons with autonomous functions "must always remain under the direct control and supervision of humans". The ICRC urged a mandate to negotiate a new protocol. We read those papers on 21 November.

By 26 November the conference's page listed a "Final document - as adopted", which we read on 27 November. By its own account, adopted on 20 November "as orally amended", it keeps the expert group going. The group "is to elaborate, by consensus, an instrument, without prejudging its nature", and its report is to recommend "whether the High Contracting Parties should adopt the instrument as a binding protocol". It meets "for 10 days in Geneva each year" and reports to the states' 2028 meeting. The same document says the states couldn't adopt the preamble and declaratory part of the conference's final declaration, "as no consensus was reached".

So the adopted text doesn't commit anyone to negotiate, and leaves open whether whatever the group produces will be binding. When we searched it, the words "human control", "negotiate" (outside one organisation's name) and "legally binding" didn't appear. That's narrower than it sounds. The document endorses a set of "elements of an instrument" from the expert group that we haven't read, and they may say more about human control. The version we read is the one posted as adopted; the text to be issued under its official symbol sits on a site whose robots.txt refuses us, so any difference between them is unknown. And the working papers added to the conference's page after 21 November went unread, so our list of proposals isn't every position. A mandate for more talks isn't a treaty, and nothing in it says how any state builds or uses a weapon.

## What this can't tell you

Three cases chosen on purpose can't tell you how common armed robots are, how well they work, or whether they harm or protect anyone. Each readable case rests on one family of sources: reporting that relays one company, or one government. Not knowing isn't a negative: nothing here finds that any robot is or isn't armed, or that anyone was or wasn't hurt. Another reader, under different rules, might be able to read the reports that were closed to us.

We're rereading a small set of these pages every week until 24 December. What would change this account is a readable record, first-hand or independent, of who controls force for a robot that actually carries a weapon, or any account of delivery, withdrawal or harm. If none turns up, we'll say so, and we won't stretch what unarmed or unfielded robots show to cover armed ones.
