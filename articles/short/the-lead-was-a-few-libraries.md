# Pinned software leaned towards security fixes that said so before the advisory, but across thirty libraries the lean is too uncertain to call

When tornado's security fix of 10 March 2026 said so in its notes, five of six pinned projects we followed moved before the advisory our rule picked, 23 days later, though GitHub had published two others for the release within two days; when python-dotenv's fix nine days earlier said nothing, two of eight moved before its advisory, seven weeks later. Across thirty libraries, each counted once, we could not tell the two kinds apart.

*27 September 2026 · Lighthouse*

Software can pin one exact version of a library, or keep a lockfile of the exact versions it has settled on, and hold still until something moves it. [Our previous piece](../fixes-that-said-so.md) found that, across fourteen libraries with one fix each, pinned software moved before the advisory at about 1.7 times the pace to fixes whose own notes said they mended a security flaw, and warned that two libraries carried much of it. So we took every advisory GitHub had reviewed, of any severity, published 27 March to 27 August 2026 against the 500 most downloaded Python projects, sorted one fix for each library by the same rule, fixed before any note was read, and followed 209 pins on the 30 libraries, 18 announced and 12 silent, that had pinned software to follow.

Counted library by library, the two kinds could not be told apart. Before the advisory, the typical announced library saw about 3.2 of every hundred waiting projects move each day, and the typical silent one about 2.7; shuffling the labels among the libraries gives a gap that large 89 times in 100. In days three to seven after the release, where our last piece found its difference, the typical announced library had no move at all. Pooled over every repository, announced fixes still lead, by about one and a half times, though resampling the libraries puts that anywhere from about half to about four times, and pooling lets a few libraries speak for many: large, slow ones at one end and small, quick ones at the other, and the pooled lead in that week rests on three moves to one fix whose security words were about an earlier release.

This revises what our last piece suggested. The test is weak, thirty libraries with few moves each, so a modest real difference could be missed; counting only a fix's security words leans towards the announced fixes, and setting aside words about an earlier release leans towards the silent. Three fixes, nltk's, tornado's and pip's, had been named by an earlier advisory GitHub had reviewed that our window missed; counted to that one, the pooled lead widens and the typical libraries part, 3.3 against 1.3, still within what chance gives, and the national vulnerability database often had the flaw earlier still. Setting aside the silent fixes whose notes we could not read at their release, four of the thirty libraries, widens the pooled lead a little and still leaves the typical libraries within what chance gives. We chose those two readings after seeing the results, so the question stays open. The twenty libraries of each kind we wanted were not there to be found among the 500 in five months of advisories. A move is a change to what a project asks for, not an installation; nothing here says any project was exposed.
