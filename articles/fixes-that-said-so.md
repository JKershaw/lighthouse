# Pinned software moved faster to security fixes that said so in their notes, mostly by people in the days after the release, but no larger share moved before the advisory

On Thursday 21 May 2026, the developers of pyjwt, a Python library that many websites use to check the signed tokens that keep people logged in, published version 2.13.0. Its changelog, the running list of changes a project keeps beside its code, began the new version with a section headed Security. Beside the first fix, it said the flaw had been reported in GHSA-xgmm-8j9v-c9wx, the kind of name GitHub gives a security advisory. GitHub published that advisory 25 days later, on the evening of 15 June.

In [our last piece](pins-move-over-weeks.md) we followed software that pins pyjwt as it moved to that fix, and counted ten of its 25 moves as coming before the advisory. They did not come before anyone had been told. The project had said so itself, in its own notes, on the day it released the fix. So this time we read the notes of every fix behind the advisories we had studied, sorted the fixes into those that said they mended a security flaw and those that did not, and asked whether pinned software moved sooner to the first kind. It did, in a particular way: at the same pace in the first two days after the release, much of it bots' work, and at about twice the pace in the rest of the first week, when most of the moves were people's. But no larger share of the moves came before the advisory, and with one fix for each library we cannot tell a fix's notes apart from the library it belongs to.

*27 September 2026 · Lighthouse*

## What a release says about itself

Software that uses a library says which versions of it will do. It can pin one exact version, or keep a lockfile, a file where a project writes down the exact versions it has settled on; either way it holds still until someone, or something, moves it. A security advisory is a public notice that some versions of a library have a flaw, with the version that fixes it. Last time we found that in every one of 68 advisories rated high or critical against the 200 most downloaded Python projects, published between late March and late August, the fix was out on an earlier day. Those 68 advisories named 37 fixed releases, across 16 libraries.

A project that releases a fix has three places of its own to say what it is: its changelog, the message it can attach to the version's tag in its repository, and the description it uploads to the Python Package Index, where Python's libraries are kept. We read all three for each of the 37, as they stood when the release came out. We called a fix *announced* if any of them, at or before the release, used a word such as security, vulnerability, CVE or advisory, or described the flaw itself, and *silent* otherwise. We fixed that rule before reading a single note.

Of the 37, 19 were announced and 18 silent, and none said so only later. Every announcement was in the changelog as it stood at the release, dated between under a minute and ten hours before the upload; lxml's was also in its tag's message and its package description. What we had not expected was how much some of them said. Eleven of the 19 named GitHub advisory identifiers, like pyjwt's, between about a day and 25 days before GitHub published those advisories. The notes pointed at warnings that were not yet public.

The silent ones were not all silent in the same way. Nine named the change without saying anything had been wrong. starlette 1.1.0, a toolkit for building web services, said "Reject absolute paths in `StaticFiles.lookup_path`", and its advisory concerned stealing credentials through paths of that kind. cryptography 48.0.1 said it had "Updated ... wheels to be compiled with OpenSSL 4.0.1", and its advisory was titled "Vulnerable OpenSSL included in cryptography wheels". A reader who knew where to look could have guessed. Eight had no notes about the release in any of the three places: five of litellm's, two of mcp's and one of langchain's. Those projects may put their notes on GitHub's release pages instead, and GitHub refused every one of those we asked for, so "silent" here means silent in what we could read.

## Sixteen libraries, one fix each

For each of the 16 libraries we took the same fix as last time, the one whose advisory came first in the window: eight of them announced and eight silent. Their advisories came between a day and seven weeks after the fixes.

Then we followed the software that pins them, as before. For each library, a public index called Open Source Insights lists the packages that depend on it, by version. We took its list for the version just before each fix, screened up to 60 of those packages in an order set by a scrambled form of each name, which nobody could steer, and kept those whose public repository, on the day the fix came out, pinned or locked a version the advisory named as affected. Then we read each repository's history forward to the first change that took the library to the fix or later, up to 27 September. The four libraries of our last piece we used as they stood; the other twelve we read the same way that morning. Two libraries, msgpack and langchain, one of each kind, gave us nothing to follow, because the version before their fix had come out a week or less earlier and the index listed almost nothing against it.

That left 257 pairs of a repository and a library, from 204 repositories: 152 on seven libraries whose fix was announced and 105 on seven whose fix was silent. By 27 September, 105 of the first and 68 of the second had moved to the fix.

## The same share, at a faster pace

The question we set out with was whether more of the moves to an announced fix came before its advisory. They did not, or not by much: 29 of the 105 moves to announced fixes came before the advisory, 28 per cent, and 17 of the 68 to silent ones, 25 per cent.

That share is not a fair comparison by itself, because the announced fixes' advisories came sooner. The typical announced fix, the median, had its advisory 3.9 days after its release, and the typical silent one 10.6 days after, so software pinning an announced fix had less time in which to move before the advisory. To compare fairly we counted, as last time, how many of the projects still waiting moved on each day. From the release to the advisory, a little over two in every hundred waiting projects moved each day to an announced fix (2.4), and a little under one and a half to a silent one (1.4): about 1.7 times the pace.

The faster pace did not come evenly. In the two days after the release, the two kinds moved alike, about four in every hundred a day (3.8 for announced fixes, on 11 moves, and 4.0 for silent ones, on 8), and bots wrote 7 of the 11 and 4 of the 8. From the third day to the end of the first week, stopping at the advisory where it came first, the announced fixes drew about twice the pace: 3.2 on 13 moves, against 1.5 on 5. People made 9 of those 13 moves; bots wrote three, and one was a person merging a bot's change. From the end of the first week to the advisory the two were alike again, below one a day (0.9 on 5 moves, all of them pyjwt's, and 0.9 on 4).

After the advisory, announced fixes drew moves a little faster in its first two days (5.6 on 13 moves, against 3.6 on 6), and the two kinds were alike in the five days after that (2.7 and 2.8).

*Pins moved to fixes that said so and to silent ones at the same pace in the first two days, but faster to those that said so in the rest of the first week, mostly by people.*

## What the moves said

A bot, here, is a program that proposes updates to a project's dependencies, such as GitHub's Dependabot or another called Renovate. Bots wrote or proposed about the same share of the moves to both kinds of fix: 44 of the 105 moves to announced fixes, 42 per cent, and 27 of the 68 to silent ones, 40 per cent, counting a person's merge of a bot's change as the bot's. Their messages were their usual lines saying that they bump or update the library from one version to another, the same for both kinds. A bot that proposes updates on a schedule does not read release notes, and in the first two days, when bots made half or more of the moves, the two kinds moved alike.

The people did not say why they moved either. Of the 46 moves that came before an advisory, one commit message used a word about security at all, and it was a person's move of pyjwt that cited urllib3's advisories, another library's. No message we read said that it moved because a release had announced a fix.

So the difference sits in the few days after the release, and in people's changes rather than bots'. We think that fits people reading a fix's notes, seeing the word security, and moving within the week. It fits just as well something duller.

## Where the reading runs thin

Each library gave one fix, so every difference between the two kinds is also a difference between seven libraries and seven others: their users, their bots, how often the projects that use them change anything. Two libraries carry much of the announced fixes' lead. gitpython's dependents moved fastest of all, and gave 8 of the 29 moves to announced fixes before the advisory, six of them in its first two days, four of those Renovate's. pyjwt's advisory came 25 days after its fix and gave 10 more. Leaving out any one library keeps the announced fixes ahead, but leaving out gitpython leaves them only just ahead (1.8 a day, against between 1.1 and 1.7 for the silent fixes with any one library left out). The days that make the difference rest on 13 moves against 5, and three of the 13 were one organisation's repositories, moved within 46 minutes on one July morning. We ran no test of significance; at counts this small, the noise in a rate is about as large as the difference.

Two judgements could have gone the other way. One fix, mako's, a templating library, was announced only because its notes described the flaw, without any of the security words; counting it silent leaves the pattern as it was. And if the nine silent fixes that named the change without the flaw were counted as announced, only two silent libraries with pinned dependents would remain, and the comparison would rest on six moves.

A few things we could not see. We read authorship from the history alone, and the times are when a change was committed, not when it reached the public repository or when a notice was first seen. And a move is a request. It changes what the project's next build or installation would take; it says nothing about what was installed or run. Nothing here says that any project was exposed.

## What this does to what we said before

Last time we wrote that a move "before the advisory" was not a move before anyone knew. For about half the fixes, somebody had been told: the project's own notes said so on the day, and in eleven cases named the advisory before it was published. Pins did move faster to those fixes in the week after the release, and the difference was in people's changes, not the bots'. But no larger share of the moves came before the advisory, so "release or advisory" does not simply become "announced or unannounced", and with one fix for each library we cannot say the notes caused anything.

## The next question

The comparison would be cleaner inside one library. gitpython published nine announced fixes and two silent ones between April and August, and cryptography one announced and two silent, so the projects that pin one library could be followed through both kinds of release, with the library held still. Whether the people who moved in the week after an announced release had read its notes is a question for their pull requests, which the history alone does not hold. And GitHub's release pages, read from somewhere they are served, would say whether litellm's, mcp's and langchain's fixes were silent or announced where we could not look.
