# The programme

The questions Lighthouse is asking, what it has found, and what comes next. 25 September 2026, revised 27 September 2026 (last after LH008). The records cite this document as LH F04. Sources in square brackets are numbered in sources.md.

The programme follows the order observatories have always followed: catalogue what is visible, calibrate on known objects, explain the mechanism, then measure the dynamics. It is paced by budget and evidence, not by a calendar, and outward work does not wait for local calibration: an outward study validates its own sources within its own boundary and says whose instruments supplied the evidence. The programme is done when someone other than the analyst can reproduce at least one important result from the retained evidence and method; when the records explain a measurement failure or blind spot as clearly as a detection; when at least one outward observation has been compared with its source's coverage limits, and one interpreted through the LH001 calibration or the study says why it could not be; when Lighthouse's own consumption is recorded; and when a synthesis states what the watch would now detect of a self-sustaining propagation pattern and what it would miss. The next programme follows from what these studies teach.

## Answered

**LH008 Do pins move on releases or on advisories.** When software built on a widely used library pins or locks it, what moves the pin to a security fix: the release, or the advisory published later? Both, and neither draws most of the moves. The libraries were fixed by a rule before any dependent was read: the four most downloaded in ClickPy's top 200 for August 2026 [S21] with a high or critical GitHub advisory in OSV [S26] published 27 March to 27 August 2026, urllib3, cryptography, pyjwt and starlette. In all 68 qualifying advisory records against 16 of those projects, the fixed release was on PyPI a day or more before the advisory. From Open Source Insights' dependents of the release before each fix [S11], screened in hash order, 102 repository and library pairs from 88 repositories pinned or locked a version below the fix when it was released. By 27 September 73 had moved to it, a median 18.7 days after the release, 28 had not and one had stopped pinning; 19 of the 73 moved before the advisory, 13 of 30 for the two libraries whose advisories came three weeks after their fixes. Moves ran at 4.1 per 100 waiting repository-days in the two days after the release and at 5.1 and 4.3 in the first week after the advisory, against 1.3 to 1.7 at other times: two short spells of about the same pace, on eight moves each, and no burst like `mcp`'s in LH007 (4 of 102 moved within a day of the advisory). An earlier release of each library that no advisory named moved 23 per cent of 91 pairs within 30 days, against 49 per cent for the fixes, partly because many of those repositories were dormant. 22 of the 73 moves were bot commits and 16 a person merging a bot's branch; no bot message in the two days after an advisory calls itself a security update. Three of the four advisories were published within 48 minutes on 15 June, among ten against the three libraries that evening. Authorship and times are read from git alone, since GitHub's API was refused and neither pull requests nor release notes were read; a move is a request, not an installation, and no project's exposure was checked. The record is studies/LH008/; the piece is articles/pins-move-over-weeks.md. What it leaves open: how much of a fix's uptake before its advisory comes because the release itself announces a security fix; which of the bots' moves were security updates, which their pull requests would say; and whether the pattern holds beyond four libraries and one evening.

**LH007 How much of the installed population moves with the resolver.** When a widely used library releases, how much of the software built on it installs the new version because an installer resolved an open range, and how much waits for a pin? In a sample of the published images of `mcp` 1.28.0's direct dependents (16 June 2026), fixed by a hash-order rule before any image was read, the two paces are distinct. Of 199 dependents Open Source Insights listed for 1.27.2, 1.28.0 and 1.28.1, 120 were screened, 22 name an image of their own, and 13 image repositories gave an installed version in 49 images, read anonymously from ghcr.io [S25], Docker Hub [S24] and public.ecr.aws [S27]. Open-range images took the new release within days, 5 of 6 built in the four weeks after it, the first 2.6 days after, at the pace of the download log [S21], where 1.28.x was 43 to 62 per cent of each day's downloads; 10 of the 20 open-range images held a newer `mcp` than a lockfile their own repository keeps and the image does not use, as agentcrew's did in LH006. Every one of the 29 lockfile and exact-pin images held exactly what its lock or pin named, and 9 of the 12 such repositories had no pin on 1.28.x within four weeks. Of 13 projects whose first 1.28.x pin was found, six made it 0.9 to 27.6 hours after three high-severity advisories against `mcp` were published within 18 minutes on 16 July [S26], a month after the release; four of those six were changes Dependabot wrote. The link to the advisories is timing and commit messages, and no project's exposure was checked. The sample is small and clustered, most of its Docker Hub images went unread when the byte ceiling was spent, and an image is an installation, not a run. The record is studies/LH007/; the piece is articles/what-moves-a-pin.md. What it leaves open: whether pinned dependents in general move on advisories rather than on releases; how often a project's image ignores its own lockfile; and what the unread Docker Hub images hold.

**LH006 What a public container image shows of installation.** Is there a public record of what was installed, past the download? Yes, for software published as a container image, and only as far as the installation. The installer's own record inside an image's layers, a `site-packages/mcp-<version>.dist-info` directory, gives the installed version, and the image's configuration its build time; both were read anonymously from Docker Hub [S24] and GitHub's container registry [S25] by streaming the upper layers, 25 to 211 MB per image, without pulling any image whole. The population is thin: of 37 packages (LH002's ten projects and the 34 direct dependents of `mcp` 1.27.0), 7 name a published image and 6 could be read, giving the installed `mcp` in 37 images. What an image installs follows how it is built, not the project's pin alone: agentcrew's repository locked 1.26.0 until 24 April, and LH002 counted it as not moving, but its images held 1.27.0 from 8 April, 5.5 days after the release, because its Dockerfile resolves an open range and ignores the lockfile. Images built from an exact pin or a lockfile moved only with it: serena 2.9 days after its pin and mcp-snowflake-server 11 minutes after; keboola's moved on 17 April in a build of the package version whose PyPI release, three days later, was the first to require 1.27.0; octobot's still held 1.26.0 at the end of July. The same tag can name different installations over time, and neither registry lists what a tag named before; an earlier build survives only while another tag names it, as nine of agentcrew's version tags show. An image is an installation made to be run, not a run, and a pull count counts requests. Docker Hub's anonymous allowance, 100 pulls an hour through an address shared with other traffic, bounded the reading to 30 counted pulls. The record is studies/LH006/; the piece is articles/what-an-image-holds.md. What it leaves open: how much of a release's installed population moves with the resolver rather than with a maintainer's pin, across a release's dependents; what moving tags named over time, which only watching them would record; and whether any public source records runs of published software at all.

**LH005 Connecting a release to installation.** Can any public record connect a software release to its installation? Partly, and only as far as the download. The Python Package Index's download log [S20], read through ClickHouse's public copy of it [S21] and checked against pypistats.org [S22] and pepy.tech [S23], connects a release to the files fetched by version, day, installer and a build-server flag the client reports. `mcp` 1.27.0 was 22 per cent of the library's downloads in the nine hours left of its release day and 48 per cent the next day, and stayed between 45 and 71 per cent to the end of LH002's window: about half of every day's downloads for five days before the first dependent LH002 watched pinned it. uv made 79 per cent of the window's downloads. The new version was taken far less through Poetry and Bazel, and carried the build-server flag on 11 per cent of its downloads against 49 per cent of the previous version's, which fits installers resolving open ranges at install time rather than dependents' decisions. The log reaches the download and stops: it is not an installation, never a run, and aggregate and anonymous, so the second hop LH002 found, 35,024 downloads of the pinning dependent's releases, cannot be told apart among 109 million downloads of the release on the same days. The copies agree to about one per cent in the window; pypistats's "without mirrors" also drops downloads that name no installer, and one of ClickPy's tables keeps an arbitrary file type. The record is studies/LH005/; the piece is articles/what-a-download-shows.md. What it leaves open: whether any public record reaches what was installed, past the download; and whether a release reaching half of downloads within two days is the rule for widely used libraries, and how much of it is builds.

**LH003 Survey of surveys.** Who already observes the computational world, which layer does each observatory see, and where does its coverage end? Thirty-four candidate observatories were considered and seventeen have a row. Each sees one layer from one narrow population: event archives and dependency resolvers see residue, broker rankings and vendor usage reports see activity, and vulnerability databases see disclosed flaws rather than their exploitation. None observes propagation directly, and none states how its population overlaps another's, so their counts cannot be added into a total. No source read can tell a person's action from an agent's. The record is studies/LH003/, with every source's row in studies/LH003/sources.md; the piece is articles/what-we-can-see.md. What it leaves open: how much the seventeen populations overlap, which only a joint sample can say; and which of the seventeen Lighthouse could read as instruments of its own, which each study that uses one settles for that one, recording its unit, population and access conditions.

**LH004 A historical propagation case.** What would the three layers have shown during a documented propagation event, and which layer carried the first detectable signal? The xz backdoor of 2024. The malicious code was public in release tarballs from 24 February, and nobody detected it there. Both signals that were noticed were activity: a memory checker's errors in Red Hat's distributions from 4 March, which the attacker answered with a fix, and one engineer's slow logins on 29 March. The first fitted no surface in the design, which gained a Correctness surface as a result. None of the seventeen sources LH003 surveyed, and no instrument Lighthouse has proposed, watches the distribution package pipelines the compromise travelled through. The record is studies/LH004/; the piece is articles/the-xz-backdoor.md. What it leaves open: what an undetected case of the same kind would lack, which one detected case cannot say.

**LH002 A bounded public software neighbourhood.** What can public activity reveal about change and dependency in a small software neighbourhood, and where does it fail to reveal running activity? Ten repositories around the Model Context Protocol Python SDK, over twenty-eight days in March and April 2026 fixed before collection by the SDK's 1.27.0 release. Four of the nine dependents came to hold the new release within the window, and in one case the change went a step further, a dependent pinning it and publishing its own release 22 minutes later. Every move fits ordinary maintenance, none was made by an identified bot, and the records tie a move to the release only by the version string and the order in time, which shows what came first and not what prompted it. No source read, git, the registry, Open Source Insights, Software Heritage or one hour of GH Archive, says what anyone installed or ran. Git and GH Archive disagree in both directions for the one hour compared, and a full window of GH Archive would be about 24 GB. The record is studies/LH002/, with the executed design in its brief; the piece is articles/where-a-software-update-went.md. What it leaves open: whether any public record connects a release to installation; how long exact-pin dependents take to move, across all of a release's dependents rather than a cohort selected on uptake; and how complete GH Archive is for a small cohort over a whole window.

**LH000 One dispatch end to end.** A first look at what Harbour records about one of Lighthouse's own tasks and what the host shows while it runs. Harbour's record is of what was asked and what the agent claimed, not of what happened on the host, and the host was close to idle. The record is studies/LH000/.

## Next

### LH009 Does an announced fix move pins before its advisory

**Question.** When a fixed release says in its own notes that it fixes a security flaw, do pinned dependents move to it before the advisory is published, more than they move to a fix released silently? LH008 found 19 of 73 moves to a fix coming before its advisory, and read no release notes, so "before the advisory" there is not "before anyone was told".

**Why this next.** It is the one open question from LH008 that the access this environment has can answer: release notes and changelogs are in the projects' repositories and on PyPI, while Dependabot's pull requests need GitHub's API, which was refused. It also widens the frame beyond four libraries and one evening, which is LH008's largest limit. Issue #7, the download pace of releases, is cheaper but further from the watch's question.

**Design.** Take the 68 qualifying advisory records LH008 found (studies/LH008/data/qualifying_advisories.csv) and, for each distinct fixed release, read by a rule fixed before any dependent is read: the release's changelog entry in the repository at its tag, its PyPI description where it carries notes, and the tag's annotation. Classify each fix as announced (the notes name a security issue, a CVE, a GHSA, a vulnerability, or the flaw the advisory describes, at or before the release) or silent, recording the words; the classification is the project's word. Choose, by a stated rule, a set of libraries that gives both classes, beyond LH008's four, and for them repeat LH008's measure with its scripts: the pinned or locked dependents from Open Source Insights, the first move to the fix, its lag from the release and the advisory, and its authorship from git. Report the share of moves before the advisory and the rate per 100 repository-days in the days after the release, by class.

**Interpretation boundary.** As LH008: a move is a request, a bot's commit shows that a bot runs, and nothing says any project was exposed. An announcement read today may have been edited since the release; where the changelog's git history shows the entry was added after the release, it is classed by when it was added.

**Stop and output.** Close when the stated sample has been read, with the unread part stated. One record; a piece if the record earns one.

### LH001 Observing a known Harbour workflow

**Question.** Which parts of an ordinary workflow can our sensors reconstruct, and which consequential events do they miss?

**Role.** LH001 is Lighthouse's standard candle: a workflow whose inputs are recorded, observed by the instruments that will later observe unknown activity. Its published signature is what makes outward readings interpretable rather than merely countable.

**Design.** Select one bounded Harbour workflow with identifiable initiation, processing, communication and completion records. Prefer a test environment or an existing routine task whose normal execution can be observed without changing its purpose. Choose a dispatch target whose host can be instrumented: a command-line or local agent runs on a machine Lighthouse can observe, whereas a web session runs on a provider's infrastructure and exposes only what the provider reports. Capture an idle baseline, the workflow, and a declared follow-up window for persistent changes.

**Initial sample.** Use a calibration run of three repetitions under comparable conditions, with the ordering and collection interval recorded. Include one ordinary non-LLM operation where available, such as a CI run or a dependency install, to test whether the proposed signature is specific to inference. This is an instrument calibration, not a population estimate. Record each instance and task variation.

**Readings.** Start with Harbour's dispatch fields (identifier, kind, target, model, harness, effort, queue and claim times), feedback entries with their timestamps and terminal marker, usage entries and proxy audit entries [S12]; the repository's pull request, review, CI and merge records; host process activity where available; and versions of affected artefacts. Preserve remote calls as remote activity: local CPU readings do not measure a provider's inference compute. Add electricity only if the measurement boundary is defensible.

**Retention.** Harbour's records are time-limited: dispatch items expire after twenty-four hours, and feedback, status and audit records after thirty days, at the time of writing. Export what the study needs within them and record each export as an observation. Repository and CI records persist longer and can anchor the follow-up window, which is proposed as seven days after the last repetition.

**Comparators.** Reconcile against operator-labelled events and Harbour's operational record. That record is server-timestamped for queueing and claiming but agent-reported for progress and completion, so treat the agent-reported parts as claims to be checked, not as ground truth. Examine disagreements at the event level. Include a known small coordination event if available, so the analysis tests whether low-volume activity with broad consequences remains visible.

**Analysis.** Produce an activity timeline, a flow account with duplicate observations identified, a before-and-after state comparison, and a record of any subsequent activity the change enabled within the follow-up window, such as further dispatches, CI runs or deployments. That last record is the smallest observable instance of propagation and gives the follow-up window its purpose. Report timing error, event detection, missingness, collection overhead and the limits of inferred dependencies. Keep observed relationships separate from hypothesised causal links. Publish the calibration in a form later studies can cite: signal per token, per request and per change, with the instrument version and boundary.

**Alternatives.** Check whether bursts reflect retries, scheduled jobs, cache behaviour, shared host load or sensor overhead. Determine whether apparently persistent activity is ordinary background processing.

**Stop and output.** Close after the planned runs and reconciliation, or document why the instruments cannot answer the question. Produce a study, an instrument revision and an observation sample suitable for sharing. No favourable hypothesis result is required.

**Execution record.** Before collection, write the study's brief with the exact workflow, participating systems, dispatch target, access scope, retention, timing, resource ceiling and review responsibility. LH001 proposes no change to Harbour's production behaviour.

## Questions after these

Choose follow-up work from observed gaps. Candidates from LH008 are reading Dependabot's pull requests, where they are public, to separate its security updates from its version updates, once GitHub's API can be read, and whether projects' pins move together in one change across libraries, which LH008's pairs counted separately. Candidates from LH007 are how often a project's image ignores the project's own lockfile, across a larger frame, which Dockerfiles alone could answer without a registry read, and reading the Docker Hub images LH007 left unread with a byte budget sized for multi-gigabyte images. Candidates from LH006 are recording what moving tags name over time with a HEAD request on a schedule, rereading the images LH006 could not from an address with its own allowance, and whether any public source records runs of published software at all. A candidate from LH005 is whether a release reaching half of a library's downloads within two days is the rule for widely used libraries, and how much of that is builds, which ClickPy's tables for a handful of libraries and releases could answer. Candidate questions from LH002 are how long exact-pin dependents take to move across all of a release's dependents, and how complete GH Archive is for a small cohort over a whole window. Others include whether small control signals predict large downstream changes, which state changes survive after inference ends, how much apparent growth comes from changed visibility, which layer carries the earliest detectable signal in a propagation event, whether a standard candle measured on one workflow transfers to another, and which of Harbour's own workflow findings leave a computational signature that an outside observer could detect. A later risk study could test whether the instruments distinguish benign repeated deployment from a propagation scenario in a contained model.

Prioritise questions by scientific value, decision relevance, evidence access, tractability and cost. Record the reasoning rather than hiding it behind a single numerical priority score. Keep speculative essays connected to the questions in this file so that ideas can become testable when suitable evidence appears.

The first public collection holds the charter, LH003 and LH004 with their pieces, and will hold LH001, LH002 or its feasibility result, and an account of the instruments. Every visual identifies its sample and observation period. When the programme is reviewed, retain the methods that produced useful evidence, repair weak instruments, and close questions that cannot currently be answered. The next investigation should be smaller and clearer because the first programme happened.
