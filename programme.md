# The programme

The questions Lighthouse is asking, what it has found, and what comes next. 25 September 2026, revised 30 September 2026 (last: LH017, whether pinned projects' CI workflows read the pin, under Answered and Next). The records cite this document as LH F04. Sources in square brackets are numbered in sources.md.

The programme follows the order observatories have always followed: catalogue what is visible, calibrate on known objects, explain the mechanism, then measure the dynamics. It is paced by budget and evidence, not by a calendar, and outward work does not wait for local calibration: an outward study validates its own sources within its own boundary and says whose instruments supplied the evidence. The programme is done when someone other than the analyst can reproduce at least one important result from the retained evidence and method; when the records explain a measurement failure or blind spot as clearly as a detection; when at least one outward observation has been compared with its source's coverage limits, and one interpreted through the LH001 calibration or the study says why it could not be; when Lighthouse's own consumption is recorded; and when a synthesis states what the watch would now detect of a self-sustaining propagation pattern and what it would miss. The next programme follows from what these studies teach.

**LH017 Whether a pinned project's CI workflows install what its pin or lockfile names.** For the same 469 pairs as LH015, does some job in the repository's GitHub Actions or GitLab CI workflows, at the pair's snapshot, install the library's version from the file that held the pin or lock, and from the lockfile itself, including where the container recipe leaves it unread? Almost always, where the rules could decide, and for lockfiles mostly, with half of that resting on commands that can re-lock. Each of the 332 repositories was cloned anonymously and read at the pair's snapshot, by rules taken from the installers' and services' documentation and fixed in a brief whose hash was posted on issue #19 before any repository was read, with two amendments made after workflows were seen; scripts, Makefile targets, tox, nox and hatch configurations and local actions were read one level down, and the writer judged 261 jobs. 395 of the 469 pairs kept CI (84.2 per cent, repository-resampled 95 per cent interval 79.7 to 88.3, "as a rule"; 81.5 per cent by repository). Of the 293 whose CI the rules decided, 282 had some job that installs the library from a timed file (96.2 per cent, 93.4 to 98.6, "as a rule"); 48 were undetermined, 14.1 per cent of that frame. By the kind of pin: exact pin 93 of 97 and both 92 of 92, "as a rule"; lockfile 97 of 104 (93.3 per cent), with its label withheld because 31 of the 135 lockfile pairs in that frame were undetermined (23.0 per cent) and the two bounds read differently. Of the 188 pairs with a timed lockfile whose CI was decided for the lock, 164 had some job that installs from the lockfile itself (87.2 per cent, 80.2 to 93.3, "as a rule"), against 45 of 90 for LH015's container builds; but in 83 of the 164 every such job uses a command that keeps the locked versions only while the project's own constraints admit them (`uv run` or `uv sync` without `--locked` or `--frozen`, and the like), and counting those as resolving afresh, the brief's sensitivity, gives 81 of 161 (50.3 per cent). Described, not tested: of the 25 lockfile pairs whose container builds did not read the timed lock, a CI job read it in 20; 51 of the 282 pairs that read the pin also had a job installing the project's requirements without it; in 236 of the 282 a job reading the pin used pip or uv, which send the CI flag, and in 124 every such job restores an installer cache as written. A workflow is a recipe for a run, not a record of one: nothing here says which jobs ran, how often or what they fetched, so the study shows that a road runs from a pin to installs the download log's CI flag can mark, not how many older downloads come down it. The record is studies/LH017/ (version 0.1); the piece is articles/tests-read-the-lockfile.md. What it leaves open: whether that road carries a visible share of the older-version downloads, which the log's CI flag on weeks not yet read could describe library by library and only run logs could count; and whether the jobs that could re-lock ever did.

**LH016 Which steps of the workflow changed the pieces' claims.** In the six research rounds of 28 and 29 September 2026, which steps of the research and review workflow changed what the pieces and records say, what kind of change did each make, at what cost, and what reached release uncaught? An inward study of the repository's own history, each change credited to the step whose commit window held it and coded by a scheme fixed before any change was read. Of 125 changes made while a step ran, 66 changed what a reader of a public surface would believe. The evidence review made 23 of them, mostly figures and how sure a sentence sounded (13); changes to what a claim meant, what a count counts, what caused what or a new reading, came mostly from outside review the keeper relayed after two pieces' release (5 of its 8) and from the reader-and-inference review's second stage (8 of its 17), and all 8 of the relayed review's and 15 of stage 2's altered text an evidence review had passed; that grouping of types was made after the counts. Text written to resolve a review carried problems of its own: 8 of the recheck's 10 public-claim changes corrected text written minutes before in response to a review, six of the eight sentences stage 2's resolution had introduced (a reading made after the counts). Every correction of a released claim in the frames came from relayed review or work the keeper commissioned, directly or in the rounds those readings opened, and three of the twelve pieces released before the reader review existed had their main claims corrected. Replayed on LH011's and LH012's pieces as first released, a fresh reader review found the relayed reading's central point on each (5 of 16 known answers found, 7 in part, 4 missed), with or without its list of prompts, and found two material problems still standing in released pieces, which the driving session confirmed on the retained data: litellm's six late releases all first reached half on 1 May 2026, and none was at half on any later day of the 30 read (issue #21); and a new version's share, counting it and any newer, rose after day 2 in 339 of 355 releases, by a median 7.2 points (issue #22); both were corrected the same night. A second coder, blind to the step, agreed on the tier of 28 of 30 sampled changes (kappa 0.88), on types less often (21 of 29). At list rates the evidence review cost $30.41 over six rounds and the reader review with its recheck $22.47 over three; the relayed review's cost is unknown, since the repository records who relayed it and not who or what produced it, much of it, by the relayed account of Astra, a reviewer outside the workflow, Astra's analysis (studies/LH016/review/relayed-review-astra-2026-09-29.md); and these costs describe the rounds rather than rank the steps. Six rounds are cases, the steps ran in one order, and every coder and reviewer runs on the same family of models as the steps it codes. The record is studies/LH016/ (version 0.2). What it leaves open: how often each review catches a known problem, which one draw per text cannot say; what the pieces that never had a reader review still hold (issue #23); and what the briefs' rules and the literature checks prevent, which leaves no trace in the history.

**LH015 Whether a pinned project's own container recipe installs what its pin or lockfile names.** For the repositories whose pins or lockfiles LH008, LH009 and LH010 timed, does the container recipe a repository keeps at the moment of a release install the project's Python requirements from the file that held the pin or lock, or from something else? From the pinned file for most of the pairs whose recipes the rules could decide, but no label stands, because more than a quarter of the pairs with a recipe could not be decided. The frame was those studies' 469 distinct repository and event pairs, in 332 repositories and 40 releases of 31 libraries from April 2025 to August 2026, fixed by their own rules; each repository was cloned anonymously and read at the pair's snapshot, the last commit before the release, by rules taken from the installers' documentation and fixed in a brief before any repository was read, with seven amendments made after recipes were seen. 247 of the 469 pairs kept a container recipe (52.7 per cent, repository-resampled 95 per cent interval 45.9 to 59.3, "for some pairs"; 45.3 per cent by repository, "not as a rule"). Of the 167 whose recipes the rules decided, 129 installed the library from the pinned file (77.2 per cent, 68.6 to 84.4); the label that share would carry, "as a rule", was withheld by the brief's rule because 68 of the 247 were undetermined (27.5 per cent), and counting all of them one way or the other gives 54.9 or 83.8 per cent. By the kind of pin, described and not tested: an exact pin alone 63 of 77, a lockfile alone 21 of 37, both 45 of 53, with lockfile pairs also the most often undetermined (41 of 153); among the 90 decided pairs whose timed files included a lockfile, a container build read the lockfile itself in 45; 89 of the 129 pairs from the pinned file never read a timed lock, their pin sitting in the project's manifest, which any install of the project reads, or in a requirements file the build installs (a reading made in the reader-and-inference review, notes/R-0022.md). Of the 38 pairs not from the pinned file, 15 installed from another requirements file, 14 the project's own published package from the Python Package Index, 7 left a timed lockfile in the build unread, the pattern LH006 and LH007 found in images, and 5 took another pin; 12 of the 38 rest only on builds that never name the library, and reading their instructions, a fresh build after the release could have taken the new release for 2, could not for 12 and was not determined for 24. A build from a repository's own recipe would therefore have bypassed the timed pin in at least 38 of the 469 pairs (8.1 per cent) and at most 106 (22.6 per cent). A recipe is not a build: no registry or image was read, recipes for development and for release counted alike, and whether a build installs the library at all was not resolved. A reviewer who classed 60 sampled builds without seeing the writer's classes agreed on 57, all 30 judged builds among them; the classifier's rules behind the three errors were corrected after the check, and one amendment applied as written, which moved nine builds at the snapshots and no label (record version 0.2). The record is studies/LH015/ (version 0.3); the piece is articles/recipes-follow-the-pin.md. What it leaves open: whether the same projects' CI workflows, whose installs the download log's CI flag records, read the lockfile their container recipe leaves unread; which recipe, if any, makes the images a project publishes and people run; what the 13 pairs undetermined only by the rule for a build's starting folder would show; and whether the lockfile pairs' lower share holds once more of them are decided.

**LH014 Whether the two-day pace holds below the top fifty.** Below the fifty most downloaded Python projects, does a new newest version reach half of its project's downloads within two days as a rule, and does its share settle by day 2? Not as a rule in either band, by the brief's convention. From ClickPy's August 2026 ranking [S21], after LH011's fifty and the packaging tools, 80 projects were drawn at random from positions 51 to 500 (band A) and 80 from 501 to 5,000 (band B), by a rule fixed before the ranking was read; 52 and 35 of them released 295 and 196 versions from April to August 2026 that became the newest when uploaded. 25 of band A's 52 had most of their releases reach half of the project's downloads, at that version or newer, on day 1 or day 2 (48.1 per cent, project-resampled 95 per cent interval 34.6 to 61.5, which includes one half), and 9 of band B's 35 (25.7 per cent, 11.4 to 40.0), beside LH011's 26 of 37 (70.3 per cent) as a description, not a test. The median of project median day-1 shares was 48.1 per cent in band A and 22.7 in band B, against 57.6 at the top. LH012's settling test held as a rule in band A (39 of 50, 78.0 per cent, 66.0 to 88.0) and for some projects in band B (23 of 35, 65.7 per cent, 51.4 to 80.0), where the median release that reached half within 30 days took 4 days. Described only: a new release's early pip and uv downloads carried the CI flag less often than the replaced release's, by a median 14.2 points in band A and 7.1 in band B (LH011: 6.6); the mirror tools pypistats names that occur in the log (bandersnatch, Artifactory, devpi) made a median 148 and 62 downloads of a release on its first two days, 0.01 and 0.23 per cent of its own, and leaving them out changed no project's outcome. Rank is not a cause and no reason was measured, and a download is a fetch. Downloads were read only to 27 September 2026, so nothing of LH013's weeks was read. Read after the counts by the reader-and-inference review (post hoc, record version 0.2, about twenty comparisons): for 172 releases that replaced a version at least 14 days old and not folded into the lower-version row, the replaced version's share on the eve of the release had project medians of 61.8, 50.5 and 31.2 per cent at the top and in bands A and B, the new release's day-30 share differed from it by project medians of -0.2, -2.1 and 0.0 points, and of releases whose predecessor held less than half on the eve, 2 of 91 reached half within two days, against 68 of 81 of the rest; so the gradient seems to lie mostly in how much of a project's downloads follow its newest version at all, before any release, which is what stays behind, and less in speed. The record is studies/LH014/; the piece is articles/rarer-further-down.md. What it leaves open: whether the gradient and the inherited share hold on a fresh draw and on later releases, and what the smaller floating share further down is made of.

**LH012 What keeps older versions downloaded.** After a new release of one of the most downloaded Python libraries, what keeps older versions downloaded, and which explanations can the public record rule out: other libraries' version bounds, Pythons the new release no longer supports, or environments rebuilt from frozen lists? For most projects the record rules out old Pythons as the main reason and cannot rule identified bounds in or out; for three, most older downloads came from Pythons that no newer release supports, and for four, identified bounds could account for most under an assumption the log cannot check; frozen lists cannot be counted in the log. Over 21 to 27 September 2026, for the 37 projects of LH011, with each project's reference release the newest version uploaded by 21 August, a median 37.3 per cent of a project's downloads were of versions numbered below it (20.0 per cent for pyjwt to 99.3 for pydantic-core, whose downloads follow the version pydantic pins; 45.6 per cent pooled). For 32 of the 37 projects (86.5 per cent, project-resampled 95 per cent interval 75.7 to 97.3, "as a rule" by the brief's convention) less than half of those older downloads came from a Python that every newer release excludes, even counting downloads that report no Python as excluded; the median project's share was 12.2 to 18.0 per cent. Downloads from such Pythons were most of the older downloads in boto3 (76.3 to 77.0 per cent), aiobotocore (63.0 to 63.5) and numpy (62.5 to 66.7), the brief's class "accounts for most", and pandas and urllib3 were undetermined; that class counts fetches that could not have been of the reference release or later, not why they were of the versions they were, and most of boto3's and aiobotocore's were of versions below the last one their Python admits, where a fresh install would land. Read after the release (post hoc, record version 0.3), those two projects' older downloads by version are flat for long runs of versions below each Python's edge (boto3 on Python 3.9: 49 of the 50 versions below 1.42.97 had 882,481 to 905,092 downloads each), the profile an installer's resolution walk leaves when it fetches every version it tries; on that reading most of them are versions inspected and discarded, not installations, which none of the three explanations describes. Walk-shaped fetches, so measured, were 17.1 per cent of the 37 projects' older downloads pooled and none for the median project. Bounds declared by the 500 most downloaded projects could account for most in four projects, the brief's class "accounts for most" for an estimate that assumes each fetch of a held dependent version brings one fetch of the project, which the log does not observe: pydantic-core (pydantic's exact pin), fsspec (s3fs), and botocore and s3transfer (boto3's versions below 1.43, fetched 2.94 and 3.88 times as often as they are, so the assumption is strained there); only for pydantic-core do the dependent's current versions carry much of the hold, the rest coming from older s3fs and boto3 versions. They could not be the main reason for seven, among identified bounds, and were undetermined for 26, whose upper estimates saturate on requirements the log cannot resolve: extras, platform markers and versions not read. Frozen lists were described, not counted, as the brief said in advance: 18 of 37 projects showed the trace fixed for them (most older downloads off every version an old Python or an identified bound would pick, and more often flagged CI than newer ones), and uv made a median 59.6 per cent of older downloads against 43.2 per cent of newer ones. The first-day observation, seen only after LH011's counts, held as a rule on 355 releases of November 2025 to March 2026 that no earlier study read: for 28 of 32 projects most releases' at-or-newer share on day 30 was within ten points of day 2 (87.5 per cent, 75.0 to 96.9), and so were 257 of the 355 releases; the band is absolute, and by the brief's secondary ratio boto3's and aiobotocore's small shares still grew by about two thirds after day 2; within the band the share rose from day 2 to day 30 in 339 of the 355 releases and for the median release of 31 of the 32 projects, by 3.2 points for the median project, so to settle is to stay within ten points, not to stop (read after the counts; record version 0.4). Read after the counts, and not tested: on 23 September 2026, 62.2 per cent of boto3's downloads came from Python 3.9, 99.4 per cent of them pip on glibc Linux with no CI flag, and over the week Python 3.9 fetched boto3 7.51 times as often as botocore; whether such downloads come from one population of machines or many, the log cannot say. A download is a fetch; the Python is the one the installer reports; one week was read; only identified bounds were tested. The record is studies/LH012/ (version 0.4), its brief snapshotted on issue #14 before any count; the piece is articles/old-versions-new-pythons.md. What it leaves open: what the older downloads that neither trace accounts for are, which the log cannot count and public lockfiles could, once each; whether what stays behind, and boto3's Python 3.9 downloads, hold on weeks not yet read; and whether bounds hold back the 26 projects the log leaves undetermined.

**LH011 How fast a release reaches downloads, and how much of it is builds.** When a new version of one of the most downloaded Python projects is released, does it reach half of the project's downloads within two days as a rule, and how much of its early uptake carries the build-server flag, compared with the release it replaces? For some projects, not as a rule, by the brief's convention. Of the 50 projects most downloaded in August 2026 in ClickPy [S21], less setuptools and pip, 37 released 424 versions from April to August 2026 that became the newest when uploaded; 26 of the 37 had most of their releases reach half of the project's downloads, at that version or newer, on day 1 or day 2 (70.3 per cent, project-resampled 95 per cent interval 54.1 to 83.8, which spans the convention's 75 per cent line). The split was sharp: 21 projects had every release reach half, five most, and eleven none (aiobotocore, boto3, litellm, grpcio-status, fsspec, botocore, protobuf, pandas, pytest, numpy, s3transfer), whose median day-1 shares were 2 to 34 per cent and moved little over the month, to 2 to 37 per cent by day 30, litellm's rising most, from 9.5 to 21; no release of ten of them reached half within 30 days, and litellm's six that did all first reached half on one day, 1 May 2026, and were at half on no later day read (record version 0.4). Pooled over releases, 127 of 424 (30.0 per cent) reached half, because boto3, botocore and litellm account for 252 releases and none of theirs did. For one release a project a month (104 releases), the new release's pip and uv downloads on days 0 to 2 carried the CI flag less often than the replaced release's on the same days: medians of project medians 32.2 against 40.7 per cent, every project's median per-release difference below zero, median -6.6 percentage points (interval -11.4 to -5.0), smallest in the three daily releasers. pip's and uv's source shows both send the flag as true when one of four CI variables is set and nothing otherwise, so it is a floor on builds; other installers' 1.7 per cent of downloads were left out. ClickPy's daily totals agreed with pypistats.org [S22] within 1.6 per cent on the 15 days checked. A download is not an installation or a run, ClickPy keeps the day and not the hour, and why older versions keep their downloads was not measured: the record's interpretation, from general knowledge of those packages, is that several of the eleven are held back by their dependents' version bounds, and that Pythons a new release no longer supports and environments rebuilt from frozen lists would fit too; no dependent's requirements, no release's Requires-Python and no download's Python version were read. Described only after the counts were read (record version 0.3): for 34 of the 37 projects the median share on day 30 was within ten points of the median on day 1, though within that band the share rose from day 1 to day 30 in 378 of the 418 releases, by a median 4.1 points (record version 0.4), and a month on from 17.8 to 47.1 per cent of downloads were still at older versions in the 26 projects where new releases took over, and from 63.0 to 97.5 per cent in the eleven. A share of downloads is a share of fetches, weighted towards environments that fetch often, so a low share is not by itself slow uptake. The record is studies/LH011/ (version 0.4); the piece is articles/two-days-for-most.md (version 1.2). What it leaves open: what keeps older versions downloaded, in all 37 projects and most in the eleven, which LH011's retained counts cannot say offline because every version older than a project's first qualifying release is folded into one row a day and no Python version was read; whether a release's share is set on its first full day, which was seen only after the counts were read; whether the two-day pace holds below the top fifty; and what the new release's lower CI share consists of, since the log cannot separate builds holding a version from fresh installs.

*Later evidence, 29 September 2026.* The first-day observation above, a reading made after the counts, held as a rule on releases LH011 did not read, measured from day 2 to day 30 as the checkpoint asked (LH012): for 28 of 32 projects with a release between November 2025 and March 2026. Of the explanations named above for what keeps older versions downloaded, LH012 ruled out Python exclusion as the main reason for 32 of the 37 projects over one week of September 2026, and where downloads from excluded Pythons were most, in three, the old Python does not say which older version was fetched; identified bounds could account for most in four, under an assumption the log cannot check; frozen lists could not be counted.

**LH010 Does the announced fix's pace hold across many libraries.** Do pinned dependents move faster before the advisory to fixes whose notes announce a security flaw than to silent ones, when each class has enough libraries that no one or two carry the result? Not settled. With the library as the unit the classes could not be told apart under any reading; pooled from the release to the advisory, the announced class leads under every clock and every restriction tried, but by the brief's clock a library-clustered bootstrap puts the ratio anywhere from 0.52 to 3.96 (record version 0.3, an outcome-aware reanalysis of the retained evidence). LH008's rule widened to GitHub-reviewed advisories of every severity in OSV [S26] published 27 March to 27 August 2026 against the 500 projects most downloaded in August 2026 in ClickPy [S21] gave 215 qualifying records against 43 libraries, one event each: 22 announced and 21 silent by LH009's rule, fixed before any note was read, not the 20 libraries a class with pinned dependents the design asked for. Open Source Insights [S11] listed few or no dependents for the release before 13 of the fixes, so 18 announced and 12 silent libraries gave 209 repository and event pairs from 150 repositories, and 142 moved by 27 September. Pooled, from release to advisory, announced fixes drew 2.30 moves per 100 waiting repository-days (35 moves) against 1.57 (20), about 1.5 times, 2.04 to 3.73 and 1.41 to 1.95 leaving out any one library; in days two to seven, 3.34 (11) against 2.29 (5). With the library as the unit, the median library rate from release to advisory was 3.21 for announced libraries and 2.74 for silent ones, and a permutation of class labels over the 30 libraries gives p = 0.89; in days two to seven the median announced library had no move (nine of 16) against 1.46 for eight silent libraries, p = 0.68. 36 per cent of moves to announced fixes and 43 per cent to silent ones came before the advisory. The fastest libraries in each class are small frames with few repository-days, and nltk alone holds 691 of the announced class's 1,522 repository-days before the advisory. Sensitivities: security words only (strict class), 3.71 against 0.76 (p = 0.28); security words about an earlier release (idna, aiohttp) counted silent, 3.17 against 4.18 (p = 0.78), and the pooled lead in days two to seven, which rests on aiohttp's three moves, turns to 2.61 against 3.30. Within the four libraries with followed dependents on both sides, the announced fix drew moves faster in three and slower in one (pip), on 0 to 4 moves a side. Three fixes (nltk, tornado, pip) had been named by an earlier reviewed advisory outside the rule's window or before the fix; with the earliest GitHub-reviewed record in the retained OSV records as the clock, the medians are 3.26 against 1.30 (p = 0.47) and the pooled rates 3.42 against 1.42 (ratio 2.42, interval 1.00 to 4.90), and with those records' NVD times, earlier for 17 events, the pooled ratio is 3.53 (1.44 to 6.86) while the medians still do not separate (p = 0.47). Nine silent events (four with pairs: litellm, mcp, pydantic-ai-slim, fastmcp) had no notes readable at release in any source read; setting them aside leaves the medians unseparated (3.21 against 2.47, p = 0.87) and the pooled rates 2.30 against 1.41. These readings were chosen after the outcomes were known: of 36 overlapping comparisons two p values and seven intervals exclude no difference, all under readings chosen after the fact, and none is claimed. The test has little power, and GitHub's release pages were not requested. The record is studies/LH010/; the piece is articles/the-lead-was-a-few-libraries.md. What it leaves open: whether a longer window of advisories, or a frame that does not depend on the release before the fix having listed dependents, would give enough libraries to see a modest difference; what GitHub's release pages say of the nine silent events without readable notes; which public notice should count as the advisory, fixed before outcomes are read; and whether the people who moved in the week after an announced release had read its notes.

**LH009 Does an announced fix move pins before its advisory.** When a fixed release says in its own notes that it fixes a security flaw, do pinned dependents move to it before the advisory more than to a fix released silently? Faster, but not in a larger share, and not separably from the library. Of the 37 fixed releases behind LH008's 68 advisories, read by a rule fixed before any note was read (the changelog at the tag, the tag's annotation and the PyPI description), 19 said at the release that they fixed a security flaw and 18 did not; none was announced late, and eleven of the 19 named GHSA identifiers 1.2 to 25 days before GitHub published them. GitHub's release pages were refused, so litellm's, mcp's and langchain's fixes are silent only in the sources read. Taking LH008's one event per library, eight of each class, seven of each with pinned dependents from Open Source Insights [S11], 257 repository and event pairs from 204 repositories held an affected version at the release and 173 moved by 27 September. The share of moves before the advisory was alike, 29 of 105 for announced fixes and 17 of 68 for silent ones, but the announced fixes' advisories came sooner (median 3.9 days against 10.6); between release and advisory the announced fixes drew 2.36 moves per 100 waiting repository-days against 1.36 (1.84 to 2.84 and 1.13 to 1.68 leaving out any one library). The classes ran alike in the first two days after the release (3.77 on 11 moves, 3.99 on 8), much of it bots'; the difference was in days two to seven (3.22 on 13 against 1.45 on 5), when people made 9 of the 13 announced moves. One of the 46 commit messages before an advisory has a security word. With one fix per library, gitpython and pyjwt carry much of the lead and class cannot be told from library. The record is studies/LH009/; the piece is articles/fixes-that-said-so.md. What it leaves open: whether the pace holds across many libraries of each class, so that none carries it; whether the people who moved in that week had read the notes, which their pull requests would say; and what GitHub's release pages say of the three unread libraries.

*Later evidence, 27 September 2026.* Across thirty libraries, one fix each (LH010, version 0.3), the question is not settled: library by library the classes could not be told apart, and by the brief's clock the pooled lead's interval spans no difference. "Faster" above holds for these fourteen libraries only. "Before the advisory" here means before OSV's publication of GitHub's reviewed advisory: pyjwt, one of the two libraries carrying the lead, had a reviewed record naming its fix with an NVD time 18 days earlier (LH010's data/reanalysis/clocks.csv), and LH010 found that which notice counts as the advisory moves this comparison more than any other choice tried.

**LH008 Do pins move on releases or on advisories.** When software built on a widely used library pins or locks it, what moves the pin to a security fix: the release, or the advisory published later? Both, and neither draws most of the moves. The libraries were fixed by a rule before any dependent was read: the four most downloaded in ClickPy's top 200 for August 2026 [S21] with a high or critical GitHub advisory in OSV [S26] published 27 March to 27 August 2026, urllib3, cryptography, pyjwt and starlette. In all 68 qualifying advisory records against 16 of those projects, the fixed release was on PyPI a day or more before the advisory. From Open Source Insights' dependents of the release before each fix [S11], screened in hash order, 102 repository and library pairs from 88 repositories pinned or locked a version below the fix when it was released. By 27 September 73 had moved to it, a median 18.7 days after the release, 28 had not and one had stopped pinning; 19 of the 73 moved before the advisory, 13 of 30 for the two libraries whose advisories came three weeks after their fixes. Moves ran at 4.1 per 100 waiting repository-days in the two days after the release and at 5.1 and 4.3 in the first week after the advisory, against 1.3 to 1.7 at other times: two short spells of about the same pace, on eight moves each, and no burst like `mcp`'s in LH007 (4 of 102 moved within a day of the advisory). An earlier release of each library that no advisory named moved 23 per cent of 91 pairs within 30 days, against 49 per cent for the fixes, partly because many of those repositories were dormant. 22 of the 73 moves were bot commits and 16 a person merging a bot's branch; no bot message in the two days after an advisory calls itself a security update. Three of the four advisories were published within 48 minutes on 15 June, among ten against the three libraries that evening. Authorship and times are read from git alone, since GitHub's API was refused and neither pull requests nor release notes were read; a move is a request, not an installation, and no project's exposure was checked. The record is studies/LH008/; the piece is articles/pins-move-over-weeks.md. **LH007 How much of the installed population moves with the resolver.** When a widely used library releases, how much of the software built on it installs the new version because an installer resolved an open range, and how much waits for a pin? In a sample of the published images of `mcp` 1.28.0's direct dependents (16 June 2026), fixed by a hash-order rule before any image was read, the two paces are distinct. Of 199 dependents Open Source Insights listed for 1.27.2, 1.28.0 and 1.28.1, 120 were screened, 22 name an image of their own, and 13 image repositories gave an installed version in 49 images, read anonymously from ghcr.io [S25], Docker Hub [S24] and public.ecr.aws [S27]. Open-range images took the new release within days, 5 of 6 built in the four weeks after it, the first 2.6 days after, at the pace of the download log [S21], where 1.28.x was 43 to 62 per cent of each day's downloads; 10 of the 20 open-range images held a newer `mcp` than a lockfile their own repository keeps and the image does not use, as agentcrew's did in LH006. Every one of the 29 lockfile and exact-pin images held exactly what its lock or pin named, and 9 of the 12 such repositories had no pin on 1.28.x within four weeks. Of 13 projects whose first 1.28.x pin was found, six made it 0.9 to 27.6 hours after three high-severity advisories against `mcp` were published within 18 minutes on 16 July [S26], a month after the release; four of those six were changes Dependabot wrote. The link to the advisories is timing and commit messages, and no project's exposure was checked. The sample is small and clustered, most of its Docker Hub images went unread when the byte ceiling was spent, and an image is an installation, not a run. The record is studies/LH007/; the piece is articles/what-moves-a-pin.md. What it leaves open: whether pinned dependents in general move on advisories rather than on releases; how often a project's image ignores its own lockfile; and what the unread Docker Hub images hold.

*Later evidence, 27 September 2026.* Across four other libraries (LH008), pinned dependents moved to security fixes over weeks, after both the release and the advisory, with no burst like this one (4 of 102 moved within a day of the advisory), so the advisory reading here is one library on one evening. **LH006 What a public container image shows of installation.** Is there a public record of what was installed, past the download? Yes, for software published as a container image, and only as far as the installation. The installer's own record inside an image's layers, a `site-packages/mcp-<version>.dist-info` directory, gives the installed version, and the image's configuration its build time; both were read anonymously from Docker Hub [S24] and GitHub's container registry [S25] by streaming the upper layers, 25 to 211 MB per image, without pulling any image whole. The population is thin: of 37 packages (LH002's ten projects and the 34 direct dependents of `mcp` 1.27.0), 7 name a published image and 6 could be read, giving the installed `mcp` in 37 images. What an image installs follows how it is built, not the project's pin alone: agentcrew's repository locked 1.26.0 until 24 April, and LH002 counted it as not moving, but its images held 1.27.0 from 8 April, 5.5 days after the release, because its Dockerfile resolves an open range and ignores the lockfile. Images built from an exact pin or a lockfile moved only with it: serena 2.9 days after its pin and mcp-snowflake-server 11 minutes after; keboola's moved on 17 April in a build of the package version whose PyPI release, three days later, was the first to require 1.27.0; octobot's still held 1.26.0 at the end of July. The same tag can name different installations over time, and neither registry lists what a tag named before; an earlier build survives only while another tag names it, as nine of agentcrew's version tags show. An image is an installation made to be run, not a run, and a pull count counts requests. Docker Hub's anonymous allowance, 100 pulls an hour through an address shared with other traffic, bounded the reading to 30 counted pulls. The record is studies/LH006/; the piece is articles/what-an-image-holds.md. What it leaves open: how much of a release's installed population moves with the resolver rather than with a maintainer's pin, across a release's dependents; what moving tags named over time, which only watching them would record; and whether any public source records runs of published software at all.

**LH005 Connecting a release to installation.** Can any public record connect a software release to its installation? Partly, and only as far as the download. The Python Package Index's download log [S20], read through ClickHouse's public copy of it [S21] and checked against pypistats.org [S22] and pepy.tech [S23], connects a release to the files fetched by version, day, installer and a build-server flag the client reports. `mcp` 1.27.0 was 22 per cent of the library's downloads in the nine hours left of its release day and 48 per cent the next day, and stayed between 45 and 71 per cent to the end of LH002's window: about half of every day's downloads for five days before the first dependent LH002 watched pinned it. uv made 79 per cent of the window's downloads. The new version was taken far less through Poetry and Bazel, and carried the build-server flag on 11 per cent of its downloads against 49 per cent of the previous version's, which fits installers resolving open ranges at install time rather than dependents' decisions. The log reaches the download and stops: it is not an installation, never a run, and aggregate and anonymous, so the second hop LH002 found, 35,024 downloads of the pinning dependent's releases, cannot be told apart among 109 million downloads of the release on the same days. The record is studies/LH005/; the piece is articles/what-a-download-shows.md. What it leaves open: whether any public record reaches what was installed, past the download; and whether a release reaching half of downloads within two days is the rule for widely used libraries, and how much of it is builds.

**LH002 A bounded public software neighbourhood.** What can public activity reveal about change and dependency in a small software neighbourhood, and where does it fail to reveal running activity? ### Checkpoint, 28 September 2026: what to ask next

### LH001 Observing a known Harbour workflow

**Question.** Which parts of an ordinary workflow can our sensors reconstruct, and which consequential events do they miss?

**Role.** LH001 is Lighthouse's standard candle: a workflow whose inputs are recorded, observed by the instruments that will later observe unknown activity. Its published signature is what makes outward readings interpretable rather than merely countable.

**Design.** Select one bounded Harbour workflow with identifiable initiation, processing, communication and completion records. Prefer a test environment or an existing routine task whose normal execution can be observed without changing its purpose. Choose a dispatch target whose host can be instrumented: a command-line or local agent runs on a machine Lighthouse can observe, whereas a web session runs on a provider's infrastructure and exposes only what the provider reports. Capture an idle baseline, the workflow, and a declared follow-up window for persistent changes.

**Initial sample.** Use a calibration run of three repetitions under comparable conditions, with the ordering and collection interval recorded. Include one ordinary non-LLM operation where available, such as a CI run or a dependency install, to test whether the proposed signature is specific to inference. This is an instrument calibration, not a population estimate. Record each instance and task variation.

**Readings.** Start with Harbour's dispatch fields (identifier, kind, target, model, harness, effort, queue and claim times), feedback entries with their timestamps and terminal marker, usage entries and proxy audit entries [S12]; the repository's pull request, review, CI and merge records; host process activity where available; and versions of affected artefacts. Preserve remote calls as remote activity: local CPU readings do not measure a provider's inference compute. Add electricity only if the measurement boundary is defensible.

**Retention.** Harbour's records are time-limited: dispatch items expire after twenty-four hours, and feedback, status and audit records after thirty days, at the time of writing. Export what the study needs within them and record each export as an observation. Repository and CI records persist longer and can anchor the follow-up window, which is proposed as seven days after the last repetition.

**Comparators.** Reconcile against operator-labelled events and Harbour's operational record. That record is server-timestamped for queueing and claiming but agent-reported for progress and completion, so treat the agent-reported parts as claims to be checked, not as ground truth. Examine disagreements at the event level. Include a known small coordination event if available, so the analysis tests whether low-volume activity with broad consequences remains visible.

**Analysis.** Produce an activity timeline, a flow account with duplicate observations identified, a before-and-after state comparison, and a record of any subsequent activity the change enabled within the follow-up window, such as further dispatches, CI runs or deployments. That last record is the smallest observable instance of propagation and gives the follow-up window its purpose. Report timing error, event detection, missingness, collection overhead and the limits of inferred dependencies. Keep observed relationships separate from hypothesised causal links. Publish the calibration in a form later studies can cite: signal per token, per request and per change, with the instrument version and boundary.

**Alternatives.** Check whether bursts reflect retries, scheduled jobs, cache behaviour, shared host load or sensor overhead. Determine whether apparently persistent activity is ordinary background processing.

**Stop and output.** Close after the planned runs and reconciliation, or document why the instruments cannot answer the question. Produce a study, an instrument revision and an observation sample suitable for sharing. No favourable hypothesis result is required.

**Execution record.** Before collection, write the study's brief with the exact workflow, participating systems, dispatch target, access scope, retention, timing, resource ceiling and review responsibility. LH001 proposes no change to Harbour's production behaviour.
