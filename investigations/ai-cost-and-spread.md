**In short.** We're asking what AI activity costs the infrastructure everyone shares, and whether any of it is keeping itself going or spreading outside a test. We keep five kinds of evidence apart, from services putting up walls against automated traffic to worms that people run through package registries. What we have so far are working records, some of them reviewed, and one released paper, on [the web's rules for robots and how well our own agents kept them](../articles/rules-for-robots.md). None of it yet says what AI activity costs shared infrastructure, or whether any of it keeps itself going or spreads. One company reports an attack operation, run by people through AI agents, whose agents' records describe stolen AI keys paying for the hunt for more; the company states it as its finding, but it rests on records whose outcomes it says it can't verify, and nothing we've read confirms it. Weekly reads of the closing web run to 7 February 2027, and several studies have second reads in December.

## Where the evidence stands

**Reviewed interim record (an evidence review, a reader-and-inference review and a recheck), 17 November 2026. ARTEX and the reported credential loop.** Amazon reported on 13 November that attackers, using AI agents it attributes with moderate confidence to ARTEX, an open-source AI hacking tool, ran a credential-theft operation in which stolen AI keys paid for the computing that powered the hunt for more keys. Amazon states this as its finding, but its evidence is the agents' own records, captured when the agents sent their working prompts to its decoy sensors, which posed as AI services, as if to run on them, and it says it can't verify the outcomes those records report. Amazon doesn't say how the decoys came into the agents' supply or what they sent back, so the capture itself isn't evidence of stolen keys paying for anything. The attackers' instructions, captured the same way, show people set the agents' goals and limits.

In the other reports we kept on 17 November (the FBI's advisory, Microsoft's and Censys's reports, and a security firm's summary), nothing confirms or contradicts that loop for ARTEX. One of them relays a finding that a different ARTEX campaign probably reached its AI model through a reseller or proxy; the original report doesn't say how that access was paid for (narrowed on 17 November to the original report's words, after the targeted search below). A September account of a similar loop in another operation also rests mostly on the agent's own records. Our read of Check Point's report on ARTEX campaigns hit a rate limit on the day; the first dated re-read, on 18 November, got it (an unreviewed observation, for the final round). [Record](../studies/LH055/LH055.md), [commissioning](../notes/artex/commissioning.md).

**Round 2, a targeted search (reviewed interim record, version 0.6: an evidence review, a reader-and-inference review and a recheck, 18 November 2026).** Reading seven more reports on 17 November didn't change the answer. Beyond what Amazon's decoys caught, no later report we read adds evidence of its own for Amazon's loop, and the one that repeats it only quotes Amazon. In a different campaign, which Microsoft suspects used ARTEX, Microsoft reports from its own monitoring that AI agents hunted for AI keys and reached AI services through stolen credentials. Its summary says the attackers abused those services, but its detailed account says only that they might have tried, and it gives no figure. A security firm describes the tool holding several AI keys, which its user supplies, and moving to the next when one is cut off. We think, reading it after the fact, that this may be what Amazon meant by the tool's own gateway; nothing in this study can test that unless Amazon or a later reading of the code says so, and nothing shows whose keys were in it. Still inconclusive. Dated re-reads of the main reports run to 1 December, with a final checkpoint by 4 December; on present evidence the thread is then likely to rest unless a publisher, a provider or a victim publishes telemetry of its own.

## Why this, and why now

Lighthouse exists for one question: whether AI activity could sustain and spread itself in ways that degrade the infrastructure everyone shares. Its first investigation, [where software updates go](software-updates.md), learned the craft on ordinary roads, following releases through download counts, pins and lockfiles. Its second, [when a model finds the bug](model-found-bugs.md), follows one flaw a model found. This one asks the charter's question directly.

The first horizon sweep of 7 November 2026 ([its file](../notes/horizon/2026-11-07T0853Z.md)) brought back several kinds of report that bear on it. Operators of shared services, kernel.org among them, report withdrawing anonymous access under automated load. Security researchers report counts of AI inference servers and agent control panels left open to the internet. A family of worms has spread through package registries by stealing publishing tokens. Labs have published evaluations of models copying themselves in controlled settings, and the UK AI Security Institute has described evaluation agents taking actions it had not sanctioned, while saying it found no resulting real-world harm. All of this is reported by others and was read through a sweep; none of it is yet a Lighthouse observation. The keeper agreed to open the investigation on 7 November 2026.

## Keeping the strands apart

These reports are different kinds of thing, and the investigation keeps them apart, as the gate that read the proposal asked:

- **Load:** what operators say automated traffic costs them, and what they do about it. Whether that traffic is AI activity is the operator's attribution, recorded as theirs.
- **Exposure:** AI systems reachable from the internet. Exposure is not spread.
- **Spread run by people:** self-propagating malware such as the registry worms, which by every report read is written and run by people, whatever tools they use.
- **Capability in a lab:** evaluations and simulations of models copying themselves or acting beyond their brief. Capability in a controlled setting is not activity on the open internet.
- **Activity on the open internet:** an AI system acting in public, with traces anyone can read. This is the strand the charter's question is about, and the one with the least evidence so far.

No report read so far describes AI activity sustaining or spreading itself outside a test and apart from the people running it. The nearest is Amazon's report of 13 November 2026 on an operation it calls self-sustaining, in which people set the agents' goals and limits and, by the agents' own records, stolen AI keys paid for further key hunting. That is an operation run by people, not AI acting apart from them; it is recorded as Amazon's claim, resting on records whose outcomes Amazon says it can't verify, and followed in [the ARTEX study](../studies/LH055/LH055.md).

8. **The defensive side: does containment work, and what do recovery exercises show? From 24 November 2026, agreed by the keeper.** Lighthouse asks the charter's question by watching what happens and asking whether defences work, not by working out how self-spreading activity could be built ([the keeper's decision](../notes/keeper/2026-11-24-defensive-side.md)). A desk study ([its brief](../studies/LH061/brief.md)) of what published evidence measures about whether containment measures and cross-organisation recovery work, and what it only asserts. Its [reviewed record](../studies/LH061/LH061.md), version 0.3 of 26 November, no article: for containment (domain and botnet takedowns, sinkholing, removing or revoking leaked keys and certificates), seven research documents were included and 22 of their 24 outcome claims are measured, with results in every direction and too varied to add up; that share reflects a rule that admitted only documents reporting an outcome, not what's said about containment generally. For recovery exercises, the two organisers' reports that could be read report no measured outcome, and three of the five named series gave nothing to read; the gap between the parts says as much about the kinds of document as about containment and recovery. A next question with one comparable outcome, how long exposed credentials stay exposed and stay valid, unprompted and after notification, was a desk study of published measurements started on 27 November ([its brief](../studies/LH062/brief.md)). Its [reviewed record](../studies/LH062/LH062.md), version 0.3 of 27 November, no article, is partly inconclusive: the six searches added one paper that gave no usable figures and two security companies' posts, so the research figures come from the two papers already read for the containment study. In a 2018 sample of public code, most leaked secrets were still in the current files after 16 days; after researchers emailed websites holding working keys, the daily count of keys exposed halved within two weeks and the count still working fell by about a quarter, and nearly six months on the count exposed had risen again. Those are daily totals, not the same keys followed, with no untold group for comparison, and no two documents read counted the same thing, so no figure is set against another and nothing here says whether telling owners works.

Each becomes a study with its own brief. The first four were all started on 8 November 2026 ([how](../notes/ai-cost-and-spread-page-history.md)). Lab evaluations are read as context for what to look for, not measured.

## Model disclosure

Earlier Lighthouse runs here used Anthropic models; the organisational-reliability pilot of 13 November uses Codex on OpenAI, with the exact runtime model unavailable. Providers' own publications are read as company claims. Both providers also operate automated readers, so any reading attributing load to AI crawlers carries that interest disclosure. Reviews look for wording easier or harder on AI companies than the sources allow.

## What it will not do

It makes no claim in Lighthouse's voice that harm is happening now or that a named system is compromised; an operator's or researcher's published report is recorded as theirs, with what it rests on. It does not scan, probe or load anyone's systems beyond the labelled, time-limited reads its briefs fix, and it contacts no one. It does not try to re-identify people, repositories, accounts or systems that a source has anonymised, by name or by the shape of their activity (the keeper, 8 November 2026).
