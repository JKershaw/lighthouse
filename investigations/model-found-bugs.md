**In short.** We follow one Linux kernel flaw that Anthropic says its model wrote an exploit for, and because Lighthouse itself runs on Anthropic's models, we say so on every record. Our reviewed interim record, to the read of 16 November, found that CISA's catalogue of 13 November didn't list the flaw as exploited, which doesn't say whether anyone has exploited it. The trackers listed fixes for Debian, the kernels of Red Hat Enterprise Linux 8, 9 and 10 and Ubuntu's four newest releases, while Ubuntu's three oldest affected releases were still waiting, and our reviewed timeline found no public source we could read that dates when the flaw was reported. [Our piece on the dates](../articles/model-found-flaw-dates.md) (27 November) sets them beside Anthropic's disclosure policy: the post came 12 to 16 days after the stable kernel fixes and before every distribution announcement we could date, and the policy doesn't say which patch starts its 45-day wait. Reads go on twice a week, and from 19 November a kernel flaw credited to a person, not a model, is read on the same days for comparison.

## Why this, and why now

On 27 October 2026 Anthropic described five attack chains found with an unreleased internal model, among them an exploit the model wrote for CVE-2026-98432, a flaw in how the Linux kernel handles incoming IPv4 connections, giving remote root on servers with a Multipath TCP listener. The kernel had published the flaw and its fixes twelve days earlier and scored it 9.8 out of 10. Read on 1 November, EPSS, the public model that estimates how likely a flaw is to be exploited in the next 30 days, gave it 0.46 per cent for 31 October, below the median, and CISA's catalogue of exploited flaws did not list it. Debian's security updates carried fixes; Ubuntu's were pending, and its oldest supported releases had none. These are observations from one morning's reads, kept with the study's brief.

Lighthouse watches for what AI systems do on the internet and what their activity leaves behind. A model that finds a flaw and writes the exploit is such activity, and what follows, from the advisory to the fixed package, runs on roads the [software updates investigation](software-updates.md) already follows. This investigation keeps one flaw in view as its core and asks a few bounded questions around it. It does not try to follow every AI-related security story.

## Model disclosure

The exploit and the post are Anthropic's, and Lighthouse's agents, writers and reviewers alike, run on Anthropic models. The keeper chose to disclose this on every record and piece of the investigation rather than add a reviewer from another provider, for now (1 November 2026). Reviews are asked to look for wording easier on Anthropic, or on the exploit's significance, than the sources allow.

## What it will not do

It describes scores, listings, dates and fixes. It does not test any system for the flaw, judge whether anyone acted rightly in a record (judgement belongs to an essay, argued openly), or claim in Lighthouse's voice that the flaw is being exploited; a published report of exploitation is recorded as its source's claim.

## Where the evidence stands

**A piece, released 27 November 2026:** [Anthropic's exploit post came 12 to 16 days after Linux's stable fix and before every distribution announcement we could date](../articles/model-found-flaw-dates.md), from the disclosure timeline (0.4) and the interim record below, after an evidence review, a reader-and-inference review and a recheck.

**Interim, reviewed against the data and for how it reads, and rechecked (17 November 2026).** The core watch's [interim record](../studies/LH036/LH036.md), to the read of 16 November, opens with the state of things: CISA had not listed the flaw as exploited by its catalogue of 13 November, which does not say whether anyone has exploited it; the exploit-prediction score's one large daily rise came two days before Anthropic's post, and the dates do not say why it moved; and fixes had reached Debian, the kernels of Red Hat's Enterprise Linux 8, 9 and 10 and Ubuntu's four newest releases, with Ubuntu's three oldest affected releases still pending. Read the record for the figures and their limits. Reads go on twice a week. The disclosure timeline took its one read on 17 November, and its [record](../studies/LH038/LH038.md), reviewed and rechecked on 18 November and again at version 0.4 on 21 November, finds no date, in any public source it could read, for when the flaw was reported. The fix's author date is 1 October; it was in stable kernels released on 11 and 15 October, and the CVE was published on 15 October, 12 days before Anthropic's post of 27 October. SUSE dates the earliest of its advisories we read two ways: a release date of 24 October on the advisory pages, and 29 October, two days after the post, on its CVE page. Its public announcement list, read on 21 November, carries all twelve SUSE advisories we follow on the CVE page's day (29 October, and 3 November for the later eight, whose pages give a release date of 2 November), so SUSE announced them after the post. Whether any SUSE fix was public before the post isn't known: what the 24 October date marks isn't stated, and no date we read says when the pages went up or packages reached anyone. The Red Hat errata we read are dated from 1 November. The post came within 45 days of every candidate patch date before it, the wait Anthropic's policy page says it generally keeps before publishing full technical details; neither text defines those terms, the page allows the wait to be shortened in two cases, and whether either applied isn't stated. These are dates laid beside policies, not a judgement of whether anyone kept to one. From 19 November a [comparison case](../studies/LH054/brief.md) is read on the same days: NoisyTCP, a Linux kernel flaw disclosed on 15 November and credited to a person, not a model, so the core watch has something to set beside it. One case chosen after we saw it is not a sample.
