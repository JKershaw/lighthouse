# When AI acquires physical authority

**In short.** An armed robot can carry out a person's instructions reliably and still put other people at risk. We are beginning a small investigation into what these machines can do, what decisions people delegate to them, and what public evidence says about civilian consequences. The first study is planned; no deployment or harm finding has been made.

## Why we are watching

John commissioned this investigation on 12 November 2026, following a discussion about armed AI-powered robots and preliminary reporting about Foundation's proposed military applications. Tracking the subject is agreed. The open question is how much a small observatory can establish from public evidence.

The purpose is broader than watching a machine act beyond its instructions. Institutions can use obedient systems to exercise physical power. We will ask who benefits, who bears the risks, what human control means in practice, and who can account for the decisions.

The [commissioning record](../notes/physical-authority/commissioning.md) keeps the decision and the earlier proposal. This is an explicit extension of the watch's emphasis, not evidence of self-sustaining AI activity on the internet.

## The first question

[The baseline study](../studies/LH050/brief.md) compares three deliberately selected cases: Foundation/Phantom and the proposed Gaza application; an armed non-humanoid robot; and a civilian airport patrol comparison whose armament is not established by the sources read. Their selection reflects prior reading, not a representative sample. It will record armament, the functions said to use AI, who chooses targets and authorises force, and whether the evidence concerns a proposal, test, purchase, delivery or operational use.

Civilian consequences and accountability are fields in every core case, including when the sources cannot establish them. An unknown is not a finding that nobody was harmed. Military and law-enforcement settings require separate contextual treatment.

An official-policy comparison around the November weapons conference and a small weekly source panel sit inside the same study. The pilot ends on 24 December 2026. Its [working log](../notes/physical-authority-watch-log.md) will distinguish unreviewed readings from findings; it contains no source observations yet.

## Building on other people's work

The [source register](../studies/LH050/sources.md) starts with Autonomous Weapons Watch's catalogue, SIPRI's research, UNIDIR's policy records, ICRC's humanitarian analysis and specialist civilian-harm methods. These are research foundations and discovery routes, not independent confirmation of a manufacturer's claim. We will follow their references where access allows and preserve the distinction between several reports and several independent sources.

The first reader account, if the baseline earns one, will ask what an armed robot is allowed to decide. The supplied essay is [commissioning context](../notes/physical-authority/original-essay.md), not a released account. Its specific claims need checking before any publication.

## What we will not infer

Autonomous navigation does not establish autonomous targeting. An armed robot need not use AI, and an AI-enabled robot need not be armed. Demonstration, procurement and deployment are separate events. Price claims do not establish cheaper policing or warfare. Evidence of improved accuracy does not by itself establish reduced total civilian harm.

The work uses public records. It does not test weapons, optimise tactics, contact witnesses or identify people whom sources have anonymised. Existing Lighthouse rules for access, ongoing-harm claims and named compromise continue to apply.
