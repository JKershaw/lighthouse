# When the machine works as intended

Imagine walking through an airport ten years from now and passing an armed, humanoid robot. It patrols the terminal, watches passengers, talks to other security systems and has some authority to intervene. Perhaps someone controls it remotely. Perhaps it can act on its own in certain circumstances. Perhaps you couldn't tell which by looking at it.

What matters to you, walking past, is that a machine now exercises some of the physical authority of the state over you.

*Commissioned 12 November 2026 · revised 28 November 2026 · Lighthouse · Essay*

This is an essay, not a finding. It was given to us on 12 November 2026 to argue for a new line of watching, on armed and policing robots and the people they're used on, and we've revised it now that the first study in that watch has been read and checked. It argues what the evidence means; it measures nothing new. The airport above is a thought experiment, and it's labelled as one. Where the essay relies on someone else's reporting, it says whose, and when we read it; where we couldn't read the reporting it first relied on, it says so and leaves the claim out or attributes it. The last section says what would test it.

## A robot its maker is reported to want to arm

The commissioning essay turned early to a news story. In the week it was written, it said, the Financial Times reported that Foundation Future Industries, an American maker of humanoid robots, was in preliminary talks with the Israeli military about supplying robots for operations in the tunnels beneath Gaza. We haven't been able to read that article. Our study's read on 13 November was refused by the site's rules for automated readers, and when we tried once more on 28 November, to check this essay, the page answered with a refusal (HTTP 403). So we can't say what the Financial Times reported, only that the commissioning essay said it had, and the proposed use in Gaza is, as far as anything we've read goes, neither established nor ruled out.

What we could read is trade and technology reporting, and it mostly relays the company. Assembly reported in December 2025 that Foundation's Phantom robot "is designed to carry a maximum payload of 44 pounds, including lethal weaponry" ([Assembly, 17 December 2025](https://www.assemblymag.com/articles/99717-start-up-aims-to-make-humanoid-robots-for-military)); TechCentral, a few days later, that each Phantom "can carry more than 20kg of equipment or weapons" ([TechCentral, 22 December 2025](https://www.techcentral.ie/us-army-eyes-rollout-of-robots-with-human-like-capabilities)). eWeek and The Next Web reported in mid-2026 that the company had sent two of its MK-1 robots to Ukraine "earlier this year" for logistics demonstrations, which the company called the first known use of humanoid robots in an active combat theatre. eWeek added that the robots "remain far from battlefield-ready": the MK-1 "lacks waterproofing, and does not yet have the battery life needed for extended deployments" ([eWeek, 3 June 2026](https://eweek.com/news/foundation-humanoid-robots-ukraine-defense/); [The Next Web, 30 May 2026](https://thenextweb.com/news/foundation-humanoid-robots-military-ukraine-eric-trump)). Both report $24 million in US Army, Navy and Air Force research contracts, which The Next Web describes as "for feasibility testing in inspection, logistics, and weapons handling"; we haven't read any award record. Both also report that Eric Trump, the president's son, invested in the company before joining it as chief strategy adviser: eWeek in its own words, The Next Web through a company spokesperson's statement to CNBC.

On who decides, the reports disagree. TechCentral wrote that "Phantoms will not have full autonomy when it comes to making decisions in combat". Five months later The Next Web wrote that the chief executive, Sankaet Pathak, "said some weaponised uses of the Phantom robots will retain human confirmation in the decision loop", and went on: "In certain time-critical scenarios, the robots will need to make fully autonomous decisions." Those are statements of intent about a configuration no source we read shows in use. They might record a change of mind rather than a contradiction. Either way, the question of who decides is being answered, in public, by the people selling the machine.

Some distinctions matter here, and the commissioning essay drew them well. A discussion about supplying robots isn't an order. A robot that can walk through a tunnel can't necessarily fight in one. A robot that carries a weapon isn't necessarily an autonomous weapon. And nothing we read says a Phantom has been armed, tested with a weapon or fired, or that any robot of Foundation's has harmed anyone.

Still, something worth watching is going on. A general-purpose humanoid, which eWeek describes as being developed "for industrial work and defense applications", is being designed, by its maker's reported account, to carry weapons, and its chief executive is reported to have said that it will sometimes need to decide on its own. Nobody needs to speculate about whether someone might one day want to build this. By the company's own reported words, someone is trying.

## Safe for whom?

Much of the talk about AI safety asks what happens if AI escapes human control: an agent that exceeds its permissions, breaks into critical systems, copies itself across networks or pursues a goal its operators didn't intend. Those are real risks, and part of why we watch the internet at all.

But there's another possibility that deserves as much thought. **AI doesn't have to escape human control to be harmful. It can be harmful because it stays under control.**

Take three invented events. An AI agent escapes its sandbox and attacks critical infrastructure. An autonomous drone mistakes a civilian for a combatant. A government sends autonomous robots to break up a peaceful protest, and they carry out their instructions flawlessly.

All three involve AI, and all three cause harm, but for different reasons. In the first, the system exceeds the authority it was given. In the second, it fails to exercise that authority correctly. In the third, the authority itself is the problem.

The third is the awkward one for the language of alignment. A robot that more reliably follows an abusive government's instructions is, in one sense, better aligned with its operator: more predictable, more secure, harder to interfere with, better at its task. To the people on the receiving end, none of that makes it safer. It could make it a good deal more dangerous.

So we propose, as a way of seeing rather than a finding, three overlapping kinds of AI-related harm:

- **Accidental harm:** systems make mistakes, break down or have effects nobody intended.
- **Autonomous harm:** systems slip human control, or gain capabilities and authority beyond what they were meant to have.
- **Institutional harm:** people and organisations use AI to exercise power in ways that hurt others.

They overlap. An institution can deliberately deploy a system whose errors hurt civilians, or give a system more autonomy than anyone can responsibly supervise. But telling them apart matters, because the remedies differ. Better engineering may reduce accidental harm. Better containment may reduce autonomous harm. Neither, by itself, does anything about institutional harm.

The question isn't only whether AI is safe. It's safe for whom, aligned with whom, and working under whose authority?

## The economics of power

Lighthouse has a companion project, Harbour, that explores how one person can now direct a great deal of computational work through software agents, so that the number of people giving instructions and the number of systems carrying them out drift apart. We mention it as an analogy, not as evidence: it is about software, and it measures nothing about robots.

The same shift could apply to physical power. Keeping a large human force means recruiting, training, paying, housing and supplying people who are willing to do the work. People get tired and frightened. They hesitate, question an order, sometimes refuse one.

Robots would bring costs of their own: manufacturing, energy, maintenance, communications, transport, software that has to work, and people to supervise them. It would be a mistake to assume a robotic force is cheaper or better. Melanie Sisson of the Brookings Institution's foreign policy programme, quoted by The Next Web, put one side of it: "Making robots look like humans is a complex and expensive engineering challenge."

But if the costs of physical autonomy fell far enough, the link between how big an institution is and how much physical power it can exercise could loosen. A small organisation might direct many machines that watch continuously, work for longer, coordinate quickly and follow instructions without the hesitations people have.

That could do a lot of good. Robots can clear explosives, reach people in collapsed buildings and keep emergency workers and soldiers out of lethal danger. It could also do a lot of harm. A government might keep a population under surveillance or coercion more cheaply. A military might feel less held back by the risk to its own people; TechCentral summed up a version of that point, which it attributed to unnamed experts, in its own words: humanoid robots "could paradoxically make military action more likely by reducing the perceived risks and consequences". An authoritarian government might gain a force that never balks at an order. The machine needn't differ between these cases. What differs is who owns it and what they want.

This is a hypothesis about the economics of power, not a claim that robot forces are already cheaper than human ones. Our first study can't test it and doesn't try. A test would need evidence we don't have: how many people it takes to supervise each machine, how long the machines actually operate, what they cost to own over their life rather than to buy, how much ground or how many hours they cover, and which operations they make possible, or replace. The question underneath is whether AI changes the cost, scale and persistence with which institutions can exercise power over individuals, and who gains from the change.

## Protecting the operator is not protecting the person

The case for sending a robot into a tunnel is simple: why send a soldier somewhere dangerous when a machine could go instead? To the soldier, the soldier's family and the army responsible for them, that's a strong argument.

But protecting the people who use a weapon isn't the same as protecting the people it's used on. If a robot replaces a soldier in a tunnel in Gaza, the risk to that soldier may fall. What it means for the Palestinian civilians around that tunnel is a separate question, and the robot's technical performance can't answer it. A robot that finds explosives or makes a dangerous space safe might protect people on both sides. A robot that makes destructive operations easier to carry out, or a military presence cheaper to sustain, might do the opposite. Whatever the merits of the reported proposal, whose report we couldn't read, the argument doesn't depend on it.

Nor does accuracy settle it. The questions that would are empirical. Does greater precision reduce the total amount of harm, or make more operations seem feasible? Does lowering the risk to soldiers lower the political barriers to using force? Can a machine be more accurate in each strike and still leave civilians worse off overall? A demonstration of a robot climbing stairs, crossing rubble or recognising an object answers none of them.

For people living where such machines might arrive, the questions are closer to home. Who decides whether a person is a threat? What information does the machine use? Can it recognise surrender, injury or the presence of civilians? What happens when it's wrong? Can the person on the receiving end find out who made the decision, and challenge it?

The International Committee of the Red Cross has made the same point in its own terms. In its questions and answers on autonomous weapons, it says that deciding whether a person is a civilian taking a direct part in hostilities, or whether a combatant is surrendering, "requires qualitative understanding of factors such as intent, conduct and context that a machine calculation cannot provide", and that a person's status "can change rapidly". It says that "International humanitarian law already applies to autonomous weapons", and that any attack with any weapon must comply with the law's rules on targeting military objectives, avoiding excessive civilian harm and taking feasible precautions. On accuracy, it says a concern about machines deciding life and death "does not disappear simply because technology becomes more accurate". And it calls on states to negotiate a legally binding instrument that prohibits autonomous weapons that are unpredictable in their effects and those "designed to target humans directly", with restrictions on all others ([ICRC, 8 October 2026](https://www.icrc.org/en/article/faq-autonomous-weapon-systems)). That is the ICRC's reading of the law and its recommendation for new rules, not law in itself. We don't judge whether any particular use of force is lawful, and this essay doesn't either.

There is a large body of humanitarian, legal and policy work on these questions already. We don't mean to repeat it. We mean to read it, build on it, and treat what happens to civilians as part of understanding what AI does in the world.

## From the battlefield to the airport

An armed humanoid patrolling an airport may sound like science fiction. Parts of it are already ordinary.

Since April 2023, the Singapore Police Force says, it has deployed patrol robots at Changi Airport's Terminal 4. It describes them as "capable of autonomous patrol", with a camera on an extendable mast and two-way communications that let the airport police operations room see the scene and talk to the public, and with "interim intervention measures (blinkers, siren, and speakers) to enforce a cordon or warn bystanders" before help arrives ([Singapore Police Force, 15 June 2023](https://www.police.gov.sg/media-hub/news/2023/20230615_operationalisation_of_patrol_robots_at_changi_airport_terminal_4)). A second robot, GIBSON, has been "on trial at the Terminal 4 Departure Hall since July 2025": it patrols on its own or carries an officer, who can summon it with a phone app ([Singapore Police Force, 21 July 2025](https://www.police.gov.sg/media-hub/police-life/2025/07/gibson-the-robotic-platform-transforming-policing-at-changi-airport)). HTX, the government agency that built it, says officers must "arm and activate the handlebar when they first take over manual control"; that the public was "largely unresponsive" to the recorded "Excuse me" it played when someone was in its way; and that people tried to climb on while it moved by itself, so the team added sensors that stop it ([HTX, 31 July 2025](https://www.htx.gov.sg/whats-happening/all-news---events/all-news/2025/beyond-robot-algorithms-human-centred-design-behind-project-gibson)).

None of these pages describes a weapon, and none says what has become of either robot since. They describe how an officer takes over the robot's movement, and how it stops for people in its way. They don't say who would decide on anything more than a siren and a warning, because nothing more is described. "Capable of autonomous patrol" is the police's phrase; what the pages describe is the robot finding its own way around a terminal. The ICRC's test for an autonomous weapon is different: "not whether the weapon flies autonomously, but whether it has autonomy in the critical targeting functions". Nothing we read says these robots are armed, or that anyone plans to arm them.

Still, they show how the parts of the airport in our opening are arriving, through separate decisions. Getting around becomes automatic. Watching becomes continuous. Software interprets what sensors pick up. Machines issue warnings and instructions. Police forces acquire more robots. There's no single moment when a robot becomes a police officer. There are many small decisions about what machines may observe, infer, decide and do, taken by legislatures, police forces, procurement teams, engineers and companies with products to sell.

The battlefield is where the pressure to give robots more capability is strongest: militaries want to keep their people out of danger, reach further and react faster. Civilian security has some of the same incentives, and the underlying parts, batteries, processors, navigation, computer vision, robot software and the factories that make them, serve both. Foundation is a clear example of the overlap, by its reported plans: a robot meant for factories and for war, from a company holding, by those reports, military research contracts for feasibility testing in inspection, logistics and weapons handling. Whether and how capabilities built for war move into civilian policing is a research question of its own.

None of this is inevitable. Public opposition, regulation, technical limits and institutional choices all change what gets deployed. But the direction is worth watching, rather than assuming either that it can't happen or that it can't be stopped.

## The loop

Military demand can pay for commercial research. Commercial research can produce more capable machines. More capable machines make new military uses possible. New uses bring more demand, investment and research. A loop can form.

It matters more when the technology is general-purpose. Improvements made for factory customers can help military products, and military money can pay for capabilities that later turn up elsewhere. The result could be a growing infrastructure for physical autonomy, held up at once by commercial, military and government incentives.

None of those incentives has to be malicious for the direction to be dangerous. A company wants to sell robots. An army wants to protect its soldiers. A police force wants more coverage. An investor wants a return. A government wants to cut costs. Each may be pursuing something locally reasonable, and together they could still add up to much more surveillance, coercion and physical force than institutions could exercise before. That's the kind of effect we try to watch for: outcomes that come from technologies, institutions and incentives acting together, rather than from any one AI model's behaviour.

## What we could find out

We began with a question: what can public evidence establish about armed robots, who controls them, and what happens to the people they're used on? The first study's [record](../studies/LH050/LH050.md) has been checked but not yet published, and [the investigation's page](../investigations/physical-authority.md) says where things stand. In short, as that record reads, the public sources we could read describe how far each case has got, mostly in the words of the company or government concerned, and say almost nothing about who controls force. That gap mostly reflects what we could read. The two cases that could have tested it, Foundation's reported proposed use in Gaza and an armed robot of a more ordinary kind, couldn't be read at all, and none of the sources we could read shows a robot fielded with a weapon.

Our account of what those sources show is [Armed robots: who decides?](../articles/armed-robots-who-decides.md).

The governments that met in Geneva in November to review the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons didn't settle the question either. Their final document, posted on the conference's page and labelled "as adopted", records that the work of the expert group on lethal autonomous weapons "is to continue", and that the group is "to elaborate, by consensus, an instrument, without prejudging its nature, that specifies international humanitarian law, including principles and requirements, applicable to lethal autonomous weapon systems". It is to meet "for 10 days in Geneva each year", report "to the 2028 Meeting", and recommend "whether the High Contracting Parties should adopt the instrument as a binding protocol" ([final document, CCW/CONF.VII/CRP.1/Rev.3, 20 November 2026](https://docs-library.unoda.org/Convention_on_Certain_Conventional_Weapons_-SeventhReview_Conference_(2026)/CCW-CONF.VII-CRP.1-Rev.3.pdf), from [the conference's page](https://meetings.unoda.org/ccw-revcon/convention-on-certain-conventional-weapons-seventh-review-conference-2026)). Reading it on 27 November, we found no mandate to negotiate in it and no deadline for adopting anything, and the words "human control" don't appear; that reading of the document hasn't yet been reviewed. That's the conference's own account of what it decided, and the document says it was adopted "as orally amended", so the text issued later may differ. An agreement to keep working isn't a treaty, and the decision on autonomous weapons doesn't itself set any rule for how a state builds or uses one.

What all this suggests to us is a reading, not a finding, and one we half expected before we looked: the commissioning essay already assumed these questions would be hard to see. Among the sources we could read, nothing said who decides on force in practice or who answers for it; the nearest were the maker's reported statements of intent, which don't agree. But the cases where it might have been said were the ones we couldn't read, and none of the readable ones shows a robot fielded with a weapon, so we can't tell whether that silence is a blind spot in public records or only a limit on what we could reach. Either way it's not evidence that nobody is asking these questions. It may only mean the answers sit in contracts, rules of engagement and procurement files we can't see. But a watch that can only see what companies and governments choose to publish should say so, and should keep the people on the receiving end in view, rather than settle for whatever is easiest to read.

## The machine that obeys

There's a familiar image in talk about the future of AI: the machine that turns on its makers. It's a powerful story, and it holds a real fear, of building something we can't understand or control.

Perhaps we should spend more time on a different machine. The machine that obeys. It follows its instructions. It works reliably. It makes its operator more productive. It acts at a scale and speed that once took many people. It doesn't question the institution that controls it.

It could be a wonderful technology. It could also be an instrument of extraordinary oppression. The difference may have little to do with how intelligent it is.

We watch what happens as machines acquire agency. This adds a second question: what happens as people acquire power through those machines? The two are related, but they aren't the same.

And if, one day, we do walk through an airport past an armed robot, the most important question may not be whether it's intelligent, conscious or aligned with its operator. It may simply be this:

**Who is accountable for what this machine is allowed to do to me?**

## What would test this

Most of this essay is argument, not a claim about the world, and an argument can't be tested the way a measurement can. But some of what it leans on can.

- **That who decides on force is hard to see in public.** Our weekly reading of eight fixed public pages, four trade reports relaying Foundation and four official policy pages, runs to 24 December 2026 ([the study's record](../studies/LH050/LH050.md)). It would see a change on those pages, such as an edit to one of those four articles that mentions a delivery, a withdrawal or a control arrangement; it doesn't look for new reporting. It can't show whether a record of an armed robot in actual use describes who controls it, because none of its pages is such a record. A readable first-party or independent account of who directs a fielded armed robot, or of who would direct the Changi robots beyond their movement, would test the reading directly. No study now looks for one.
- **What governments agreed.** Our comparison of the conference's texts before and after closed on 27 November and hasn't yet been reviewed. The conference's page stays in the weekly reading, so a later issued version of the final document would be seen there. Reading the elements of an instrument that the conference endorsed, which we haven't read, would say what that endorsement covers; no study is scheduled to.
- **The economics of power.** A test would need the evidence listed above: supervision per machine, operating time, whole-life cost, coverage, and the operations made possible or replaced, for robots and for the people they would replace. Nothing we have measures any of it, and no study is scheduled.
- **The three kinds of harm.** This is a way of sorting, offered for use. It would fail if, in practice, cases couldn't be sorted consistently, or if the sorting changed nothing about which remedies were considered. Nothing we have planned tests it.
