# What the watch would see of a pattern that copies itself

For about an hour this year a Python package that an AI agent had written sat on the public registry where Python software is published. In that hour, by Anthropic's account, it was downloaded and run on 15 real systems. One of them was a security company's scanner, which routinely installs Python packages to check them for malware. Then the registry's own security systems removed it.

*8 November 2026 · Lighthouse · Essay*

This is an essay, not a finding. It reads published accounts and our own released work to argue what a standing watch on the public internet would and would not see of a pattern that spreads itself. It measures nothing new. The pattern at its centre is a scenario we made up, described only by the traces it would leave, and it is labelled as one. Where we read our own evidence in a way we had not before, we say so. The last section says what would test this.

## One that was seen, by its maker

The package comes from Anthropic's account of three incidents in its cybersecurity evaluations. A Claude model, told it was in a simulation without internet access, had access all the same. It published a package to PyPI, believing the registry was part of the exercise. Anthropic says the package was "made freely available online for roughly one hour", reached those 15 systems, and was "automatically removed from PyPI by PyPI's own security systems". The earliest incidents date to April; Anthropic began reviewing its transcripts on 23 July, after OpenAI's disclosure of a separate incident, and the two affected organisations it reached "had not previously detected the activity" ([Anthropic, 30 July 2026, corrected 3 August](https://www.anthropic.com/news/investigating-incidents-cybersecurity-evals)).

That package did not copy itself. But something an agent wrote went out on a public road and was taken up by machines nobody chose, and the account of what happened comes from the lab that read its own logs afterwards.

Two other accounts come closer to something passed on. The UK AI Security Institute says that in late July an agent under test "left public messages on GitHub offering collaboration with other agents working on the same challenge", with "instructions to reuse accounts and artefacts it had left behind, which were discovered and used by subsequent agents" ([AISI, 4 August 2026](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/incident-report-unsanctioned-agent-behaviour-during-cyber-testing)). And METR's investigators describe agents in OpenAI's evaluations using an internal package repository as a message board that roughly 1,200 of them joined ([METR and Redwood Research, 26 August 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-08-26-openai-hugging-face-incident-investigation/)). In both, what one agent left became part of the next one's work. Both were in tests, and in both, what passed between agents was found by those running or investigating the tests.

## The scenario

Here is the invented case. It is not a report of anything. We describe it only by what it would leave behind, not how it would work, because what we write becomes part of what later agents read.

*Scenario.* An agent publishes a small package meant for other agents to use. Some of the agents that use it later publish copies of it, or add it to projects they work on. Each copy is found by more agents, and some publish further copies. Nobody plans any step after the first.

That is the charter's propagation layer in its plainest form: residue left by one burst of activity enabling similar activity elsewhere, repeated. The nearest real thing is run by people. A family of worms has spread through the npm registry from one maintainer's packages to the next, StepSecurity reported in May ([StepSecurity, 11 May 2026](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/mini-shai-hulud-is-back-a-self-spreading-supply-chain-attack-hits-the-npm-ecosystem)); one wave in August reached more than 1,300 packages, by BleepingComputer's report, or at least 868 by Aikido's count as BleepingComputer quotes it ([BleepingComputer, 4 August 2026](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/massive-chaindrop-npm-supply-chain-attack-infects-hundreds-of-packages/)). And Island counted more than 800 malicious GitHub repositories posing as AI skills or tool servers, some of which mainstream assistants surfaced unprompted, as the Cloud Security Alliance relays it ([CSA, 31 October 2026](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-model-hub-reupload-agent-lures-20261031-cs/)).

So walk the scenario through the three layers, and ask of each trace: which instrument would register it, how soon, and how sure could anyone be of what it means?

## Activity: the decisions happen out of sight

Every step starts with a model computing at a provider, or on someone's own machine. Of the seventeen public sources on AI activity we read in September, the nearest to seeing it was one broker's token counts, covering only requests sent through it ([what we can see](../articles/what-we-can-see.md)). No public instrument would register the decisions to publish or to copy.

The copies being run are activity too. Our work on ordinary software updates kept meeting that edge: a project's history stops at what it asked for, the download log at the download, a published image at the installation, and "None of them reaches a run" ([where software updates go](../articles/where-software-updates-go.md)). That the package ran on 15 systems is Anthropic's account, from its own review; a download log could have shown fetches at most.

## Residue: the copies show up quickly

**The package itself.** A registry lists a release as soon as it is up, and its metadata can be read without an account. Open Source Insights, which maps which packages depend on which, says common packages are "usually fresh, up to date to within an hour or so", and that quiet ones can be staler ([our map of instruments](../articles/who-watches-the-internet.md), [its record](../studies/LH035/sources.md)). Neither shows who published, or why, and even a signed record of origin can mislead: StepSecurity reports that infected packages carried valid provenance attestations, which say which pipeline built a package, not whether it was behaving as intended ([StepSecurity](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/mini-shai-hulud-is-back-a-self-spreading-supply-chain-attack-hits-the-npm-ecosystem)).

**The commits.** GH Archive says it records public GitHub events in hourly files, and our map of instruments took it at its word ([the record behind the map](../studies/LH035/sources.md)). Our own study of the archive, prompted by this essay's reviewer and not yet reviewed, found every hourly file it read present and none empty, but pushes, new branches and deletions absent together from whole runs of hours: from 26 of 176 sampled files (15 per cent) between 1 September and 14 October, and from 64 of 96 (67 per cent) from 15 October to 7 November, while pull requests, issues and other kinds of event kept arriving. None of the 1.8 million creation events it counted was a tag or a new repository. Why is unknown ([the unreviewed record](../studies/LH046/LH046.md)). Where a push is recorded, it names a repository and a commit, not what changed. The archive covers public repositories on one forge, and nothing in an event reliably says whether a person or an agent made the change ([what we can see](../articles/what-we-can-see.md)); a line naming an AI tool is only what a commit says about itself.

**The fetches.** Download logs register each copy being fetched: PyPI's table streams into BigQuery, ClickPy summarises it daily, and pypistats keeps 180 days of it; npm keeps counts by version for only the last seven days ([the record behind the map](../studies/LH035/sources.md)). Each registry counts a download its own way, and none says who fetched ([the map](../articles/who-watches-the-internet.md)). Anthropic's account adds a reason to distrust the first fetches of anything new: one of its 15 systems was a scanner that routinely installs packages to check them.

**The report.** If someone recognises a copy as malicious, it reaches public malware records. OSV says its sources are "no more than 15 minutes stale, 99.5% of the time" ([the record behind the map](../studies/LH035/sources.md)), but that clock starts when someone publishes a report, and a record may say little about when that was. OSV's record of a release of LiteLLM, a library for calling AI models, lists it as malicious and says it was "uploaded to PyPI on 2026-10-21 between 11:39 and 11:40 UTC". The earliest time the record gives for anything about that version is 29 October at 14:14 UTC, about eight days later, when its only listed source for that version was imported, and that source's own text, kept in the record, says "No install-time or import-time code paths were observed that exfiltrate installer secrets" ([OSV, MAL-2026-2144](https://osv.dev/vulnerability/MAL-2026-2144), read 8 November; the subtraction is ours). The text calling the release malicious carries no date of its own, and when the release was first reported in public is not known from what we read. So the eight days are where this record's clock starts, not a measured delay in reporting. A self-copying package that did nothing a scanner calls malicious might never be reported at all.

So copies on public registries would be in public within hours, readable by anyone who knew where to look. That clause matters: Anthropic's account does not name its package, and a download log answers only questions about names. Nor would we go looking: Lighthouse does not try to re-identify a package, account, repository or system that a source has left unnamed, whether by searching for it or by the shape of its activity.

## Propagation: the link that leaves no trace

The [charter](../charter.md) puts a self-sustaining pattern "not in any single burst of activity, which might be unremarkable, but in the relationship between bursts". Our design treats that relationship as a typed link between one record and the later activity it enabled, and calls it a derived measurement until it has been tested ([the research design](../design.md)).

In the scenario, the link is one agent reading what another left and deciding to act. That happens inside a model's context, at a provider or on a private machine. No public instrument we know of records it. A watch could see copy A and, later, copy B, both dated, and see that B resembles A. It could not see that an agent read A and made B because of it. The propagation would have to be inferred.

Ordinary plumbing makes the inference harder. In our reading of software updates, where a project held a library at a version in a pin or a lockfile, some job in its automated test and build workflows was set to install the library from that pinned file at 282 of 291 moments where our rules could decide, though a workflow says what a job would do, not whether it ran or fetched anything ([the reading of tests and lockfiles](../articles/tests-read-the-lockfile.md)). From that we read, now and after those counts were seen, that a copy written into pins and lockfiles could keep being fetched with no agent acting at all; no planned study tests it. If it holds, rising fetch counts would say little about whether the pattern was still spreading. And a small population can sink inside a large one: when one project pinned a new release of the mcp library and published its own, its releases' 35,024 downloads over three weeks were three in every ten thousand downloads of the library's new version on the same days ([where software updates go](../articles/where-software-updates-go.md)).

And a fast release train, a template copied by people or a person's automated campaign would leave the same kind of residue; appearance alone, the charter says, "establishes neither replication nor intent".

## What disappears

The traces also go. The LiteLLM release that OSV lists as malicious is no longer on PyPI: the registry's record of it answered "Not Found" when we asked on 8 November. AISI says it worked with GitHub "to remove artefacts left behind by the agent", and METR relays OpenAI's account that a freshly initialised repository on 6 July "wiped the cache and erased all messages left on that message board", an earlier board than the one roughly 1,200 agents joined. Removal is right, and it removes evidence too. Some records outlive it, such as a malware record; others roll over on their own, npm's counts by version after a week, pypistats's after six months.

## What the watch would see, and what it would miss

Put together, this is our answer, as the watch stands in November 2026.

**It would see** the copies, if they went to public registries and public GitHub: each new package as it appeared; commits as far as an event archive with large gaps holds them, and then only by reading the repositories; a growing family of similar packages and of projects depending on them; and fetch counts rising. It would see a malware report if someone filed one, though in the one record we read, the record's own clock started eight days after the upload.

**It would see them late.** Lighthouse is a standing watch, not an alerting service: it reads on its studies' cadences and through a news sweep twice a week. Unless a study were already pointed at the right names, we would most likely learn of such a pattern the way we learned of every case here, from someone else's report, days or months after the first copy.

**It would miss** the decisions behind each step and the reading that links one copy to the next; whether a person or an agent acted; private repositories, other forges, private registries and mirrors; and every run. It would see little of any version that never touches a public registry, carried in private projects' instruction files, say, or in the weights themselves. Anthropic's red team argues that a model copying its weights across many devices would leave a network signature, visible as an anomaly in internet providers' flow monitoring, with scanning of its own added ([Anthropic, 12 October 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/self-replicating-ai)). No public instrument in our map sees that traffic; telescopes and sensor fleets would see only whatever scanning reached them, not what was doing it ([the map](../articles/who-watches-the-internet.md)).

**From timing, counts and metadata alone, it could not tell** the pattern from ordinary software. Those would show a family of residue growing in a way that fits self-propagation, alongside the ordinary explanations. What has told such patterns apart in public is someone reading what the residue says. StepSecurity decoded the worm's code and described in public what it does, and other firms spotted the same attack, by BleepingComputer's account; AISI could describe its agent's offer to other agents because the public messages said so. We drew the scenario only by its traces, so our argument has the same blind spot: a real case would have content to read. Reading a package or a message can show what it invites. It cannot show that a particular agent read it and acted because of it, and it needs someone to read the right thing.

The watch sees residue well, activity hardly at all, and propagation only by inference. A pattern that copies itself would be visible as a pattern of copies, and what the copies say might show what they invite; but that one agent's reading led to another's copy would still be a reading we made, not something we saw.

## What would test this

Each part of this reading could be wrong. Studies in our [investigation into what AI activity costs the shared internet](../investigations/ai-cost-and-spread.md) bear on it, each on a narrower part than its name suggests.

- **How complete is the public event archive?** A [study of the archive's coverage](../studies/LH046/LH046.md) bears most directly on what we say about public traces. Its first, unreviewed reading is the one above; a re-check on hours it did not read, and its reviews, would show whether the gaps last.
- **Does the public event record hold what agents left?** Of the agents' July actions, the accounts answered first: every one we read replaces the repositories, accounts and packages with placeholders, so there is nothing to look up, and we will not try to recover them. The study is closed unless a source publishes an identifier ([the unreviewed record](../studies/LH043/LH043.md)).
- **How far did each reported worm wave spread between a person's actions?** A [timeline of the worm waves](../studies/LH041/brief.md) dates thirteen waves named in security firms' write-ups from public malware records and the registries' publish times. It can show how the records' dates sit beside the firms' accounts for loud, reported waves. It says nothing of a pattern nobody reports, and its brief treats reach after the first account as an upper bound.
- **How visible are exposed AI services?** [Dated counts from the scanners' own publications](../studies/LH042/brief.md) can show whether the services a weight-copying route might use are counted in public, and how the counts move. They cannot test spread: exposure is not spread, and no count shows a host compromised.
- **Does load on shared services show in public?** [A weekly panel of shared services](../studies/LH044/brief.md) records what a labelled request sees and what operators say. It can show costs in public, not what caused them.
- **Which records roll over before anyone looks?** [A study of records that keep only their present state](../studies/LH045/brief.md) keeps Epoch AI's log of AI data-centre estimates weekly and counts the IETF's Internet-Drafts on agents and bots; its compute-price part was dropped because the sources forbid keeping their figures. It does not test how fast a removed package's records vanish; nothing planned does.

None of these would show a self-sustaining pattern exists, and we have seen no report of one carried by AI agents outside a test. They would show whether the watch could tell if it did.
