# Seen from outside, seen from inside

On 21 October 2026, PHP, which runs behind many websites, and Node.js, which runs JavaScript on servers and in the tools that build software, each released security fixes. PHP's was an emergency, followed by advisories from government agencies and coverage in the technology press; Node.js's was a regular security release, its first since July. We set out to watch both spread, and found we could not watch them the same way. PHP we could see only from outside, by the servers that announce their version. Node.js we could see only from inside, by the project's record of what was downloaded. That difference, we came to think, is part of what we were seeing.

## Two ways of looking

PHP we saw from outside. Censys, a company that scans the internet's addresses, counts the services that answer with a PHP version (a service is an address and a port, not a machine or a website) and publishes, day by day, how many in each branch it counts as patched. A branch is one of the parallel release lines a project keeps, such as PHP 8.3 and 8.4, each with its own fix. PHP makes this possible for many servers: by default it writes its version into a header, a label on every page it sends that browsers receive but do not show. (Censys does not say how it detects versions.) On 28 October, a week after the release, Censys counted 110.9 million PHP services, and two in five still reported a version; by 20 November, fewer than two in five did.

Node.js we saw from inside. The project publishes how many times each exact version was downloaded from nodejs.org each day. That is a count not of machines but of fetches: each time a build server, a test job or a developer's laptop asked for a copy. Copies taken from a Linux distribution, a ready-made container image or a company's mirror never reach it. We know of no public count of Node.js servers by version. The Python libraries we followed in September are seen the same way, through the Python Package Index's download records.

So the first lesson came before any number. Each instrument sees the side its software turns towards the world. Part of the difference is also who chose to count: Censys's daily tally follows this one emergency, and we know of nothing like it for Node.js.

## Standing and rebuilt

Picture a PHP website set up years ago, a blog, a shop or a small organisation's pages on a shared host, serving pages ever since. A fix reaches it only when someone acts on that machine: the owner, the hosting company or the Linux distribution it runs on.

Now picture what a download log sees. A test job starts from nothing and fetches what it needs; a container image is built again from its recipe; a developer reinstalls. This world is rebuilt constantly, and a fix reaches it as fast as the rebuilding turns over, unless the list it is rebuilt from names an older version.

Both worlds exist for both: Packagist, the index from which PHP's package manager Composer fetches libraries, logged 5.8 billion installs in September, and Node.js has servers that stand for years. But each instrument sees mainly one: the scanner what stands, the download log what is rebuilt. Our reading is that the two curves are not one race run at different speeds. They describe two ways for software to exist.

A week after release, between 54 and 72 per cent of each fixed Node.js line's downloads were of the fixed release or newer, and between 52 and 79 per cent of the PHP services in each fixed branch were counted as patched. A month after, on 20 November, the PHP branches stood at 75.0 to 92.2 per cent, and the Node.js lines at 65.5 to 96.3 per cent, counting three newer versions first seen on 11 November as fixed, a rule our study chose after seeing those counts that assumes, unchecked, that they carry October's fix (39.8 to 85.8 per cent without them) ([our piece on the two fixes](../../articles/a-week-after-two-fixes.md)). The numbers look alike, but one counts requests in a day and the other services as last scanned. That they look alike is a coincidence, not an agreement.

What the month did not show is the slow accumulation this picture led us to expect of the standing world. In both counts most of what moved had moved within two weeks. From day 7 to day 14, Censys's patched count rose by about 0.6 to 1.5 million services in every PHP branch; after that, in three of the four, it barely moved, and the shares crept up mostly because Censys counted fewer services in those branches. Censys's "patched" probably also includes services first seen already on a fixed version, so a rising count isn't only machines that changed. On Node.js's side, the late rise under the rule we lead with was mostly more downloads of the newer versions, while downloads of older versions fell only a little; that split, too, was made after the counts were seen. We still read the two curves as two ways for software to exist, but a month of these counts adds little support to the claim that the standing kind changes slowly, and what the late weeks show is mostly what each instrument counted, not what anyone did.

## What attention looks like

In the rebuilt world much attention is automatic, and it cuts both ways. A pipeline that asks for the newest version picks up a fix without anyone deciding anything. A pipeline rebuilt from a lockfile, a file listing the exact versions a project has settled on, fetches the old version again, just as automatically, until someone moves the number. Python's September figures fit both, though they cannot show which mechanism was at work. Four security fixes held between 54 and 61 per cent of their libraries' downloads the day after release, and only 57 to 72 per cent a month on. Of 102 pins on those fixes in public projects, 18 had moved within a week and 50 within a month. And in the typical library, downloads flagged as coming from build servers were 32 per cent of a new release's first three days, against 41 per cent of the old release's on the same days. Fast uptake may say as much about the plumbing as about anyone's choices.

In the standing world a fix needs an act on a machine, and the plainest sign of what happens without one is the part of the count no fix reaches. On 20 November, a month after the release, 22.5 million of the PHP services Censys counted, 51.8 per cent of those reporting a version, ran branches PHP no longer supports, some first released more than a decade ago; a week after the release it had been 24.4 million. PHP released no fix for them. Some Linux distributions keep old branches patched for their long-term releases, usually under the old version number, so some of these may be protected; we have not measured how many.

## What each view cannot see

A forgotten Node.js server downloads nothing. Installed in 2021 and never touched, it never asks nodejs.org for anything again, and the download log never sees it. A download log leans, by construction, towards whatever keeps coming back for more.

The scanner leans the other way. It sees a server left as it was set up, still announcing its version, and misses two kinds of care. An administrator who turns the version header off drops out of the count. A server patched by its Linux distribution often keeps its old version number (Ubuntu's fixed package for its 24.04 release still calls itself PHP 8.3.6), so it is counted as unpatched; Censys's patched share is a floor. Nor can silence be read as care: the share of PHP services reporting no version rose from 28 per cent on the day of the release to 60 per cent a week later, the jump coming on 23 October, when Censys's count of PHP services grew by more than half in a day, which looks like a change in the instrument, not in the internet. On 20 November it was 62.3 per cent.

The old branches show how far apart the two views sit. From outside, branches no fix reaches were more than half of the PHP services that announced a version, a week after the release and a month after. From inside, only 10 per cent of the Composer installs Packagist logged in September came from those branches. Node.js's unfixed share, 11.8 to 16.5 per cent of daily downloads from a week before the release to a week after, and 11.5 per cent a month on, is an inside number of the same kind: old lines still fetched, not old servers still running. Months, units and populations differ, so no number here measures another. But the same layer of old software is most of the picture from one side and a small part of it from the other.

These are blind spots by construction, not measurements of neglect or care, and they sit on opposite sides. Neither instrument sees the machine itself, only a request or an answer. In an earlier piece we described public sources on AI activity as windows, clear within their frame and showing nothing beyond it. That still holds. What this comparison adds is that where a window's frame falls can tell you something about the thing it is pointed at.

## The working week

Two details we did not set out to measure, noticed after the counts were in.

Node.js's downloads roughly halve at the weekend. On Saturday 24 and Sunday 25 October nodejs.org served about 16 million downloads a day, against 27 to 35 million on the weekdays around them, and every weekend day from late September on was below every weekday. Over the month after the release it stayed that way: 15.6 to 18.1 million on each weekend day, 26.7 to 38.9 million on each weekday. We read that as the working week in a server log: builds run when people push code. Censys's count, of services as last scanned with rescans that can lag by days, is too coarse to show whether patching keeps office hours.

And the PHP branches no fix reaches read like strata: at our first read, on 25 October, the largest were 7.4, 8.1, 7.2, 5.6 and 5.2, each first released in a different year, each still answering on the internet.

## Side by side

No single instrument here shows the shape. Set side by side, with their blind spots stated, they begin to outline one: a rebuilt layer that turns over quickly unless a list holds it still, seen by its fetches; a standing layer that changes when someone acts on it, seen by what it announces; and a large, old layer beneath, seen because it still announces versions nobody has moved.

We think that is the internet AI systems now work in. An agent that writes code and a bot that moves a pin work in the rebuilt layer; a crawler reads pages the standing layer serves. Anything that spread through the internet, deliberately or not, would, we expect, travel through these layers at their speeds and past their blind spots. We keep this watch to be able to tell whether AI activity could sustain and spread itself in ways that wear down the infrastructure everyone shares. That needs a map of the terrain, made the way this sketch was: partial views set side by side, each with a note of where it stops. Much of that map is already in other people's research, in internet-wide scans, certificate logs, routing data, web crawls and technology surveys, and part of our work from here is to read it beside our own.

## What would test this

Each reading above was made after the first week was seen, could be wrong, and is reported as made after the fact. Our [PHP](../../studies/LH033/brief.md) and [Node.js](../../studies/LH034/brief.md) studies both read their counts to day 30 and stopped collecting them on 22 November. When this essay was first released, their plans fixed the day-30 figures but not the comparisons below. The same day, 30 October, the first two were added to both plans as two comparisons ([the PHP study's amendment 2](../../studies/LH033/brief.md) and [the Node.js study's amendment 1](../../studies/LH034/brief.md)), after the first eight days of PHP's counts and the first seven of Node.js's had been read and before any later day was. So the day-30 figures tested those two as predictions, though of readings made after the first week was seen.

- **Standing and rebuilt.** Changes that wait for someone to act accumulate slowly, as Python's pins did. If the distinction holds, PHP's patched shares should still be climbing in the fourth week, while most of the month's movement in Node.js's download shares comes in the first. It could fail: after Node.js's July security release, one line moved more after its first week than in it. *Checked at day 30:* it passed as fixed, but tells us little. Measured as the part of each day-30 share that arrived after the first week, the typical PHP branch's was larger than the typical Node.js line's under both counting rules, and every PHP branch rose by at least a point from day 23 to day 30, by 1.3 to 3.0 points. But the patched count grew by more than a point of the branch in that last week only in 8.2 and 8.4; in 8.3 and 8.5 the rise came from a shrinking count of services. Under the rule as first written, Node.js's side passed only because its share fell once newer versions, which that rule doesn't count, took a growing part of each line's downloads, and under the rule we lead with its late rise was mostly added downloads of those versions. A share of a day's downloads can settle as soon as what is fetched changes, while a share of standing services moves as they are rescanned or drop out of the count, so the test's result could follow from the units alone. It was two comparisons under each of Node.js's two rules, one of them chosen after the counts.
- **Older lines lag in both.** In the first week the oldest fixed Node.js line (22) and the two older PHP branches (8.2 and 8.3) lagged. If they have caught up by day 30, this reading fails. Even if it holds at day 30, the two measure different things, and in PHP part of the lag may be the instrument's: 8.3 is the branch Ubuntu 24.04 ships, whose fixed build keeps its old number. *Checked at day 30:* it held. On the last three days line 22 sat 8.0 points below the lower of the newer lines under the rule we lead with and 18.8 under the rule as first written, and the higher of PHP 8.2 and 8.3 sat 8.3 points below the lower of 8.4 and 8.5. The caveat stands, and one detail sharpens it: a single release of 8.3 from June 2024, 8.3.8, held 11.1 per cent of the 8.3 services reporting a version on 21 November, and nobody has checked whether a distribution's package keeps reporting that number while carrying later fixes. That a lag lasted says nothing about why.
- **The download log cannot see what stops fetching.** How much it misses is unknown. A published sample of Node.js servers seen from outside would show whether old lines are as rare on running servers as in the download log. None of our studies or proposals looks for one.
- **Automatic attention cuts both ways.** If lockfiles hold automated builds back, a fix's share of build-server downloads should rise only as lockfiles move. A study could follow the flag installers send, day by day, through one fix's first month. None is scheduled; the nearest is a ranked [proposal](../../proposals.md) for a census of the versions public lockfiles name, set beside the versions downloads sit on.

Beyond these, the essay's broadest reading, that an instrument's blind spot falls on a characteristic side, is itself a ranked [proposal](../../proposals.md) for a study that would class each instrument's vantage without knowing the grouping. With collection closed, no later count from the PHP and Node.js studies will test the readings above further.
